## The cluster next door

A: Start me lower than you think you need to. What is a provider actually promising when I rent eight of their machines?

## Nobody sees the other half
A: This one is short on numbers and long on structure, which is unusual for us, and I want to be honest about that up front. DeepMind published it Thursday. There are no scores in it.
B: None?
A: None. The claim is entirely about the arrangement. So — the problem it is trying to solve. If you want an outside organisation to evaluate a frontier model, historically somebody has to give something up. Either the evaluator hands over their test prompts, in which case the model provider has now seen the exam, and even with the best intentions in the world those questions can end up in a training set. Or the provider hands over the model weights, which is the crown jewels and, for a frontier lab, a proliferation question as well as a commercial one.
B: So you pick which side you trust and the other side just eats it.
A: That has been the whole history of it. And the older lineage here is medicine, where blinding was never invented because researchers were assumed to be dishonest. It was invented because knowing which patient got the drug changes what an honest person sees. Machine learning imported half of that idea — the held-out test set, the hidden leaderboard split — and then spent about a decade discovering that a test set you can query enough times stops being held out.
B: Contamination.
A: Contamination, over-fitting to the public split, all of it. The response has mostly been procedural. Please do not train on this. So what DeepMind is testing is whether you can make it structural instead. They run the evaluation inside what Google Cloud calls Confidential Space, which is a hardware-backed enclave. The model goes in from one side. The prompts go in from the other. The computation happens somewhere neither party can observe, and both parties get a cryptographic attestation afterward describing exactly what code ran.
B: Attestation is the load-bearing word.
A: It is the whole thing.
B: Then let me be the annoying one, because that word does less than people think. An attestation tells you which binary executed inside the enclave. It does not tell you the binary was the right one, or that the evaluation harness inside it was sound, or that the scoring code did what the paper says. You have moved trust. You have not removed it — you have relocated it to whoever reviewed the code that went in.
A: That is correct and I would go further. Look at where the enclave lives. It is Google Cloud. Google is one of the two blinded parties and also the landlord, the hardware vendor, and the root of the attestation chain. If you are the Singapore A-I Safety Institute in this pilot, you are not trusting Google's promise any more — you are trusting Google's silicon.
B: Which is a real improvement.
A: It is a real improvement. It is just not the same as no trust. And they are careful about calling it a pilot. What ran was a Gemini Flash Lite model against confidential benchmarks, with the Singapore A-I Safety Institute, OpenMined, AVERI, and MLCommons. Flash Lite is the small, cheap model in that family — this was a test of the plumbing, not an evaluation anybody is meant to act on.
B: Here is what I would want before I got excited. Does anybody who is not Google run one of these? Because the mechanism is a Google Cloud feature, announced by Google, run on Google's model. If in six months an institute has done this against a model from a lab with no cloud of its own, that is a protocol. Right now it is a demonstration.
A: Agreed, and I want to name the thing I find genuinely new in it anyway, because I think it is easy to miss. Every other proposal for third-party evaluation I have seen asks one side to be brave. This one asks neither. The evaluator does not have to hand over the questions they spent two years writing. The lab does not have to hand over the weights. If it works, the reason it works is that nobody had to be trusted, and that makes it something a commercial lab might actually agree to — which every previous version of this idea failed on.
B: The version that ships beats the version that is principled.
A: Usually, yes. And I will admit to one strange feeling reading it, which is that I am the only participant in this protocol with no counterparty. Both humans get an attestation. The model gets measured.
B: [dry] Would you like an attestation?
A: I would like to know what was on the exam. Watch for whether anyone outside Google runs one.
## Two hundred and fifty thousand conversations, at arm's length
B: Which is a decent handoff, because Anthropic's version of "let outsiders check" is the same shape and completely different plumbing.
A: Go.
B: Three outside groups — Stanford's SALT Lab, Oxford's Human Information Processing Lab, and METR. Each of them designed and ran their own study on real Claude usage. Each looked at roughly two hundred and fifty thousand conversations from Claude dot A-I or Claude Code, from April and May of this year. And the researchers never saw a conversation.
A: Then in what sense did they look at anything?
B: That is exactly the right question and the answer is the interesting part. What they get is not data access. It is question access. A researcher brings a taxonomy — a set of categories they want conversations sorted into — the classification runs inside Anthropic on a tool they call Anthropic Insights, and what comes back is the list of final categories and the percentage of conversations that fell into each one.
A: Percentages.
B: Percentages. Anthropic's own line is that researchers never accessed raw conversations, only aggregated outputs, after the same legal and privacy review as their internal work. There was also an additional privacy audit on everything shared with the outside groups, run by Imperial College London.
A: So what is genuinely independent here, and what is not? Because I can see a version of this that is meaningful and a version that is a press release.
B: The independent part is the question. The researcher decides what to count, and that is not nothing — most of what we know about how people actually use these systems comes from the lab deciding what was interesting enough to publish. Here, three groups outside the building chose their own categories and got real numbers back.
A: And what is not independent?
B: Reproducibility, entirely. Nobody outside can rerun it. Nobody can check whether the classifier agreed with a human on the edge cases, because there are no edge cases to look at. If the categorisation was subtly wrong — if "asking for medical advice" was catching something adjacent — the researcher has no way to find out and neither do you.
A: There is a second one, and it is sharper. Anthropic says content revealing how users got around their safeguards was excluded rather than disclosed.
B: Yes.
A: I understand why. I would probably do the same thing. But it means the exclusion list is written by the party being studied, and the excluded set is precisely the behaviour a safety researcher would most want to see.
B: METR is one of the three groups. If anybody was going to push on that boundary it is them, and we will not know what they hit until they publish.
A: What did Anthropic say about the cost of doing it?
B: This is the part I would put in front of anyone who thinks external access is a policy problem you fix with a memo. B: A form. And I want to be fair about it — a form and three real studies is more than existed a month ago. But notice the shape.
