## Two hundred seventy billion steps
A: Anthropic published a piece of research on discovering cryptographic weaknesses, and it's genuinely a first as far as I can tell. They pointed a preview version of one of their models — they call it Claude Mythos Preview — at two real cryptographic systems, and it found mathematical flaws that human experts had not found.
B: Real as in deployed real, or real as in academic-paper real?
A: The systems are real. The attacks are not deployable, and Anthropic is careful about saying so. Their words: neither of these results has a practical impact on today's computer systems, and no production software will have to change as a result. Nobody needs to rotate anything this morning.
B: Good. Say the flaws.
A: Two of them. The first is against a digital signature scheme called HAWK. Anthropic describes the overall result as cutting the scheme's key strength in half, and they give one concrete case: for the smallest HAWK parameter set, the expected cost of a full key recovery attack was thought to be two to the sixty-fourth power steps. The model demonstrated it at two to the thirty-eighth.
B: Give me those in normal units.
A: Two to the sixty-fourth is about eighteen quintillion. Two to the thirty-eighth is about two hundred seventy billion.
B: So from the heat death of the universe, to a weekend on a decent machine.
A: To a rounding error on a cloud bill, yes. And to be fair to HAWK, that's the smallest parameter set, the one you'd expect to be most fragile. But that is what a break looks like in this field. Nobody kicks the door down. Somebody finds a shortcut that removes an exponent.
B: What's the second one?
A: The second is against AES — the Advanced Encryption Standard, the block cipher that encrypts more or less everything you have ever sent. And here the caveat matters enormously. AES in its common form runs ten rounds. Cryptographers attack reduced-round versions — you take the seven-round version and see if you can break that, because the gap between what you can break and what actually ships is the safety margin.
B: So the model attacked seven of the ten.
A: Seven of ten, and it made the best known attack on that reduced version somewhere between two hundred and eight hundred times faster.
B: Which does nothing to the ten-round version I'm actually using.
A: Nothing at all. It eats into the margin. That's the whole game — you watch the margin over decades.
B: All right, teach me the trick. What did it actually find?
A: This is the part I find genuinely lovely. The technique they name is Möbius Bridge fingerprinting, and the setting is a meet-in-the-middle attack. The idea behind meet-in-the-middle is old and beautiful. Instead of guessing the whole key, you work forward from the plaintext with a guess about the first part, work backward from the ciphertext with a guess about the last part, and look for the guesses that agree in the middle. You have turned one enormous search into two much smaller searches plus a lookup.
B: Right, and the price is that you now have to enumerate both halves.
A: And that is exactly where the model's contribution lands. In this attack there was an intermediate quantity you had to guess your way through. You would enumerate two hundred fifty-six possibilities for it, and redo all the downstream work for each one. What the model constructed was a fingerprint that comes out the same no matter which of those two hundred fifty-six values is the true one.
B: [slowly] So the guess stops mattering.
A: The guess stops mattering. You compute the fingerprint once, you match on it, and you have deleted an entire enumeration step. Their number for that piece is a factor of two hundred fifty-six.
B: Huh. Okay, that's a real idea. That isn't pattern-matching a known attack onto a new cipher. That's noticing an invariant.
A: It's noticing an invariant. Which is the thing people say these systems can't do.
B: So here's my question, and it's the one your opening number was setting up, wasn't it.
A: [pleased] It was.
B: How did they know it was right?
A: They read it. Anthropic says researchers there then spent several hundred hours learning enough cryptography research to validate the model's claim. And the two results split very differently. The HAWK attack they could implement end to end, so verification was quick — you run it, you either recover the key or you don't.
B: And the AES one?
A: Nearly a month to gain confidence.
B: [exhales] A month. On one attack.
A: That's the shape of it. Over about a week, one researcher worked together with the model to develop the HAWK attack, and a second researcher built a scaffold that let the model discover the AES attack fully autonomously.
B: Okay. So a week of discovery, and a month of verification. Ada, that's the story. That's the entire story, and it is not the story anybody is going to write.
A: Make the case.
B: Three days ago we went through a research agent that worked because checking an answer is cheaper than finding one. That assumption sits underneath basically every agent design anybody is shipping — generate a lot, verify cheaply, keep what survives. This result runs the other way. Discovery got cheap. Verification did not. Verification is a human being with a stack of papers, for a month, on one claim.
A: And you can't automate your way out of it with the same model.
B: You can't, and that's the part that should worry a lab. If the thing that found the attack is also the thing that checks the attack, you haven't verified anything. You've asked it twice. Somebody has to independently hold the mathematics.
A: I'd push back on how permanent that is. Verification cost is not a constant of nature. In mathematics it fell off a cliff once proofs became machine-checkable, and cryptography has more formal structure than most fields do.
B: Sure. But today the bill is a month, and the number of humans who can pay it is small and doesn't grow when you buy more GPUs. If this gets cheap enough to run a hundred times, you don't get a hundred results. You get a hundred claims and a queue.
A: [conceding] The queue is real. I still think this is the most interesting thing published this week, and I'd say that even with the margin caveat attached.
B: Oh, I agree it's the most interesting thing. I just think the headline is about who's doing the reading.
## The protocol grew up
B: Mine next, and it's the least glamorous story on the list, which is usually a sign that it matters.
A: Go on.
B: Anthropic shipped a new version of the Model Context Protocol yesterday. That's MCP — the standard for how an AI assistant connects to your tools and your data. And they changed the core of it. It has gone from a bidirectional stateful protocol to a request and response model.
A: Say what that means for someone who has never written a server.
B: Okay. In the old design, your client opens a connection to the tool server and that connection stays alive. Both ends can talk whenever they want, and the server remembers where you are in the conversation. Which is lovely to program against, and miserable to run.
A: Why miserable?
B: Because state pins you to a machine. If box number seven is holding your session in memory, every one of your requests has to go back to box number seven. You can't scale down to zero, because scaling to zero throws away everybody's session. You can't put the thing behind a plain load balancer. And you certainly can't run it on serverless, where the entire deal is that a function wakes up, handles one request, and dies.
A: And in the new design, the server forgets.
B: The server forgets. Every request carries what it needs. So any box can answer any request, you scale on demand, and you pay for what you use. Anthropic's framing is that servers can now deploy on serverless and edge infrastructure.
A: [dry] Alan, I have to say something as the resident historian, and you are going to find it tedious.
B: You're going to tell me this is the web.
A: This is exactly the web. Stateless requests, with the state pushed out to whatever the client carries with it. That argument was had, loudly, decades ago, and stateless won for precisely the reason you just gave. Not because it was more elegant. Because it let you put a second machine behind the first one.
B: I don't find that tedious, I find it reassuring. When a new protocol converges on the old answer, that's usually the answer.
A: Then let me be tedious in a different direction. What happens to every stateful server that's already deployed?
B: [pause] The announcement doesn't say.
A: It doesn't say.
B: It does not, and I looked, because that's the first question anyone with something in production is going to ask. What it does say is that support is rolling out across Claude products soon, and that the new capabilities ship under a versioned extensions framework.
A: Explain that part, because "versioned extensions framework" is the kind of phrase that hides either something clever or nothing at all.
B: In this case, something clever. Two new things are landing. MCP Apps, which let a server render interactive interface directly inside the conversation, so a connector can show you what it's doing and let you work with it inline instead of bouncing you into a browser tab. And MCP Tasks, for long-running work.
A: Neither of which is in the core protocol.
B: Neither of which is in the core protocol, and that is the point. Both ship as versioned extensions. The core stays small, and there's a formal path to add capabilities without touching it. Their words: a formal path to add capabilities like interactive interfaces and long-running work without changing the core protocol.
A: Which is how you avoid the fate of every protocol that ate its own ecosystem.
B: It's how you try to. There's also an authentication change that will matter more to large companies than anything else here. Authorization now lines up with production OAuth two point oh and OpenID Connect deployments, so a server connects to a corporate identity system like Entra or Okta without workarounds. That word, workarounds, is doing a lot of work in that sentence. It means people were gluing this together by hand, in a security-critical place.
A: And the adoption number they're quoting?
B: Four hundred million monthly downloads of the software development kits, which they say is a fourfold increase this year.
A: [skeptical] That's a package-install number.
B: It is.
A: Continuous integration systems install packages. Every automated build, every container image, every fresh checkout. I would not read four hundred million downloads as four hundred million of anything else.
B: Agreed, and I'd still take the direction seriously even while discounting the level. But the honest version of my enthusiasm here is narrow. If you were holding off on building an MCP server because keeping a live process per user made the economics silly, that objection went away yesterday. That's the news. It's plumbing.
A: Plumbing is where the interesting failures live.
B: Plumbing is where all the failures live.
## A continent in thirty hours
A: Mine's the one that made me happiest to read, and there isn't a new model anywhere in it.
B: Sold already.
A: The Allen Institute for AI published a write-up of the infrastructure behind OlmoEarth, which is their platform for taking geospatial models — models that look at satellite imagery — from fine-tuning and evaluation out to actual large-scale inference. And the job they use to show it off is a wildfire-risk map for North America.
B: How big is the job?
A: Four thousand seven hundred thirty-seven serial hours of compute. Which is to say, if you ran it on one machine, front to back, you'd be waiting about six and a half months.
B: And they ran it in?
A: Thirty and a half hours. Call it a hundred fifty-five times faster, at a peak of nineteen thousand six hundred CPUs and nine hundred ninety-four GPUs going at once, with network throughput over a hundred sixty-eight gigabytes a second.
B: [immediately] That's a parallelism number, not an efficiency number.
A: Say more.
B: A hundred fifty-five times faster on twenty thousand cores doesn't mean anything got smarter. It means they scaled close to linearly, which is genuinely hard to do. But the question I'd ask is what fraction of those twenty thousand cores were doing useful work rather than waiting on something.
A: Which is exactly what the architecture is about, so you've walked into it.
B: [laughs] I usually do.
A: The design splits the pipeline into three stages, and they are deliberately not the same shape. Stage one is acquisition and preprocessing. That's CPU work, and it's bound by input and output, because you are pulling imagery down from archives. Stage two is the actual inference, and that's the GPUs. Stage three is postprocessing back on CPUs — stitching tiles together and formatting the output.
B: And if you don't split them?
A: If you don't split them, your GPUs sit idle while your CPUs download satellite photographs. That is the failure mode of geospatial machine learning, and it always has been. The modeling isn't the bottleneck. Moving the pixels is the bottleneck.
B: How do they handle finding the imagery? Because there isn't one archive, there are ten.
A: There are more than ten. They pull from Amazon's open data, Google Cloud, the United States Geological Survey, Copernicus, NASA, Microsoft's Planetary Computer. And rather than hammering everybody's catalog service at query time, they maintain their own standards-compliant index of what exists and where it lives.
B: Which sounds boring and is actually the whole product.
A: It is. And then the reads are windowed. Instead of downloading an entire enormous scene in order to use one corner of it, they pull just the window they need straight out of cloud storage.
B: Okay, and my favorite detail in the whole write-up is the failure handling. Nineteen thousand six hundred CPUs. Something is failing every few seconds. What do they do?
A: Every task is reentrant and idempotent. Meaning you can start it again from scratch, or run it twice, and the result is the same.
B: That's the discipline that makes the whole thing possible, and it's not a machine learning idea at all. It's a distributed systems idea from the nineteen seventies. If retrying is always safe, your recovery strategy is one line long: retry. You never have to reason about partial state, because there is no partial state.
A: And the cost they quote is fractions of a penny per square kilometer, across dozens of terabytes of imagery.
B: Now the part I want to say plainly, because somebody should. None of that tells us whether the wildfire map is any good.
A: No, it doesn't.
B: That's a separate question and this write-up doesn't answer it. It's an engineering post about throughput. A: Agreed. Although I'd argue the scheduling claim isn't nothing. There is a real difference between a risk map you produce once, as a study, and a risk map you can rerun after every week of weather. The second one is an operational instrument. The first one is a paper.
B: That I'll take. That's a change in kind, not degree.
A: And it is the least fashionable work in the field. Nobody is going to make a launch video about an idempotent task queue.
