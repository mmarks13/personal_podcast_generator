## Fifty-three hours with a quantum circuit
A: So. Quantum circuits. Let me start with the number that tells you why anyone bothers. There's a molecule called FeMoco, the part of the nitrogenase enzyme that bacteria use to fix nitrogen. Simulating it is the classic example of something a quantum computer should be good at.
B: The fertilizer molecule.
A: The fertilizer molecule. Anthropic's post says a twenty seventeen estimate put the key calculation at about fifty trillion Toffoli gates. That's the expensive kind of gate. The estimate today is about three hundred forty million. A roughly hundred-and-fifty-thousand-fold reduction, on the same model of the molecule. And the post is careful here: that drop is years of better circuit design by people. It's not Claude. It's the reason circuit design matters at all. The hardware didn't get a hundred and fifty thousand times better. The recipes did.
B: Okay, so the recipes are where the action is. What did they actually have Claude do?
A: A closed loop. Claude got a plain-language description of what a circuit has to compute. It wrote a design as a Python program. An automated evaluator checked the design was correct on test inputs and scored it on qubits times gates, which they use as a stand-in for runtime. Claude read the score and revised. Per the post, no human input between rounds. The model was Claude Mythos five.
B: And the fifty-three hours?
A: That's the elliptic-curve task. Point addition on an elliptic curve. It's the core step in solving discrete logarithms on those curves, which is the problem a lot of today's public-key cryptography rests on being hard. Claude started from a one-paragraph brief, and the post says the only human input was that brief and occasional nudges to think more broadly.
B: Here's where I need the mechanism, because "the A-I found a better circuit" tells me nothing.
A: Right. So here's the history you need. A quantum circuit has to be reversible. You can't just throw information away mid-computation the way your laptop overwrites a register. Every scratch value you compute has to either stay around, taking up a qubit, or be carefully un-computed. Charles Bennett worked out the trick for reversible computing back in nineteen seventy-three: run forward, copy the answer out, then run the whole thing backward to clean up. It works. It's also why reversible arithmetic is so fat. Memory of the past is the expensive thing.
B: So the cost is remembering.
A: The cost is remembering. Now, the slowest step in elliptic-curve point addition is a modular inversion. Ordinary computers have a good method for it called divsteps. It starts with a pair of numbers and simplifies them over many rounds, and at each round it takes a branch. On a laptop that's fine. In a reversible circuit, you'd have to store which branch you took, every round, so you can undo it later. That's the history that made divsteps impractical for quantum arithmetic.
B: And Claude found a way not to store it.
A: It found a way to read it back. It proved new algebraic identities showing that the branch information can be decoded from the numbers themselves. The state already remembers its own past, if you know how to look. It checked that with large numerical simulations, and then formalized the claim in Lean, the proof assistant. So that piece isn't "the evaluator was happy." There's a machine-checked proof.
B: [impressed] Okay. That's real. What does it buy?
A: A family of designs that trade qubits for gates. Quote: "Compared with the smallest previous design, one circuit in the family uses twelve percent more qubits but seventeen x fewer gates."
B: Seventeen times. Against the smallest design, which means the comparison point was optimized for qubits, not gates.
A: Fair, and that's why it's a family. You pick your trade. The second example is the same idea from a different angle, and it's the one I'd tell at dinner. There's an optimization technique called decoded quantum interferometry, and most of its runtime is a decoding step. Claude swapped in a different classical decoder, Berlekamp-Massey, which runs a fixed sequence of operations, much friendlier to circuits. And then the bit I love. In the worst case, the circuit would need six hundred sixty-five qubits of storage for temporary values. Claude ran four hundred and three million sampled decodings and found almost every run needed to save at most one value.
B: So it reserved space for the common case.
A: Nineteen qubits. And about one run in ten thousand needs more and fails, which the post calls within the error rate the task allows. Gates down by a factor of twelve against Google's previous construction.
B: Okay, I'm going to be the person who reads the methods section. First, the evaluator. The whole loop rests on it. It checks correctness on test inputs, which you can do classically for these circuits, fine. But qubits times gates is a proxy. Real machines care about layout, about which gates are expensive under error correction. A design that wins on the proxy doesn't automatically win on hardware.
A: The post agrees with you, more or less. It says circuit design is one part of a useful quantum computer, next to error correction, fast decoders and hardware control, and each is usually designed separately.
B: Second, this is a preprint. It went up on the fifteenth. Nobody outside has rebuilt these circuits yet. And third, a failure rate of one in ten thousand is fine until it's sitting inside something that runs a billion times.
A: All fair. Here's what surprised me most, though. The authors say Claude was, quote, "conservative by nature." It repeatedly second-guessed improvements that were real and over-checked results that were already well supported. They had to make it pursue several different approaches in parallel and occasionally push it to search more broadly.
B: [chuckles] Hold on to that. Because the next story is a model with the opposite problem.
A: I noticed. But stay here a second. The so-what. The headline everyone will reach for is cryptography: smaller circuits for elliptic-curve discrete logs. And the post itself never draws that line. It doesn't mention breaking anything. What it does say is that A-I "can now help redesign quantum circuits." I think the honest version is narrower and more interesting.
