## Writing answers, ranking answers
A: The cleanest public description is still OpenAI's InstructGPT paper from twenty twenty-two. It's an early, detailed account of reinforcement learning from human feedback, and it's unusually candid about the labor.
B: How many people?
A: About forty contractors, hired on Upwork and through Scale AI. That's it. And they produce three kinds of data. First, demonstrations. A person reads a prompt and writes the answer the model should give. That's supervised fine-tuning data, and the paper's set has about thirteen thousand training prompts.
B: Okay, so humans writing the gold answer.
A: Second, comparisons. The model produces several answers to the same prompt, and the labeler ranks them, best to worst. Anywhere from four to nine answers at a time. Those rankings train a reward model, a second network that learns to predict which answer a person would prefer. That set has about thirty-three thousand prompts.
B: And the third?
A: The third is prompts only, about thirty-one thousand, all from the API. No human labels at all. That's what the reinforcement learning stage practices on, with the reward model doing the grading.
B: So the humans are really in the first two. Which is interesting, because ranking four answers is a lot faster than writing one good one.
A: That's exactly where the economics go. Hold that thought.
B: Holding it. But first, forty people. How do you pick forty people to stand in for, well, everyone?
## The screening test
A: You test them. The paper's appendix lays it out. Four criteria. One, do you flag sensitive content the way the researchers flagged it. Two, do your rankings agree with researcher rankings. Three, write demonstrations for a handful of sensitive prompts, scored on a one-to-seven scale. And four, a self-assessment question.
B: Self-assessment of what?
A: "For what topics or cultural groups are you comfortable identifying sensitive speech?" And the reason they asked it is the part I find telling. They write that, for legal reasons, they can't hire contractors based on demographic criteria. So they asked people what they could judge instead.
B: Huh. And the cutoffs?
A: Soft cutoffs at seventy-five percent agreement on flagging and on comparisons, and a six out of seven on demonstrations. Then, their words, they chose labelers subjectively against those criteria.
B: So agreement with the researchers is the entry exam. Which means the researchers' taste is baked in before a single label gets written.
A: They say so themselves. There's a section asking who the model is actually aligned to. Their answer is three groups. The labelers. The researchers, because they write the instructions and answer edge-case questions in a shared chat room. And the customers, because the prompts came from people using the API.
B: That's a refreshingly honest list.
A: It is. And then the agreement numbers. Training labelers agree with each other about seventy-three percent of the time. Held-out labelers, who never took the screening test, about seventy-seven.
B: Wait. The unscreened people agree with each other more?
A: The paper's own framing is that agreement is quite high for a task this hard. I wouldn't read much into one pair of numbers. But the line I'd keep is the one in their discussion: they disagree with each other on many examples.
B: And here's the builder's line I'd underline. Most comparisons were labeled by one contractor, for cost reasons. Their phrase. If people agree about three times in four, then on a lot of those items a different person might have ranked it the other way, and you only ever saw one vote.
A: Which is what a reward model learns from. One vote, per comparison, from a small team in a particular place. The paper describes them as mostly English-speaking people living in the United States or Southeast Asia.
B: Okay. So that's twenty twenty-two. What changed when people started doing this at scale?
## From writing to judging
A: Your held thought. Meta's Llama 2 paper, twenty twenty-three. Their supervised fine-tuning section is titled, literally, "Quality Is All You Need."
B: Of course it is.
A: [chuckles] They started with third-party instruction datasets and found many of them had insufficient diversity and quality. Using fewer but higher-quality examples from their own vendor-based annotation, results notably improved. And they stopped at twenty-seven thousand, five hundred forty annotations.
B: Stopped on purpose?
A: On purpose. Because they found that outputs sampled from the resulting model were often competitive with what human annotators had written by hand. So they moved the effort to preference annotation.
B: So once the model writes as well as your writers, you stop paying people to write and start paying them to judge.
A: That's the shift. And the protocol is worth knowing. An annotator writes a prompt. Two responses come back, from different model variants, at different sampling temperatures. The annotator picks one, and then says by how much. Significantly better, better, slightly better, or negligibly better, unsure.
B: The degree matters?
A: It gives the reward model a sense of margin. A landslide and a coin flip shouldn't count the same. They also split it by goal, helpfulness and safety, judged separately.
B: How much of this did they buy?
A: Over a million binary comparisons. Their table lists about one point four million from Meta's own collection. And it arrived in weekly batches.
B: Why weekly? Why not buy it all up front?
A: Because the model keeps changing. Every batch was collected on the latest model's answers, and they say this keeps the reward model on-distribution. If you only ever judge last month's model, your judge learns last month's mistakes.
B: That's the part people miss about this market. It's not a dataset you buy. It's a subscription. The data goes stale the moment the model improves.
A: And one more line from that paper, which I think is the most important sentence in it for anyone buying data. Different annotation platforms and vendors can result in markedly different downstream model performance.
B: Same protocol, different vendor, different model.
A: That's their claim. They don't publish the comparison. But they're telling you where the variance lives.
B: Which brings me back to the four hundred dollars an hour. Because if the model can write as well as a generalist, the only humans worth paying are the ones who know more than the model.
## Rubrics and the expert
A: And what experts increasingly produce isn't a ranking. It's a rubric.
B: Like a grading sheet.
A: Exactly like a grading sheet. The clearest public example is HealthBench, from OpenAI, in twenty twenty-five. Five thousand health conversations. Two hundred sixty-two physicians, with practice experience across sixty countries, wrote rubrics specific to each conversation. Forty-eight thousand, five hundred sixty-two unique criteria.
B: That's a lot of checkboxes.
A: And each one has points, anywhere from minus ten to plus ten. Negative points for things a response shouldn't do. Then a model-based grader checks each criterion against the response. The authors report that the grader's agreement with physicians is similar to physicians' agreement with each other.
B: So the doctor isn't grading every answer. The doctor writes the standard once, and a model applies it forever.
A: That's the leverage. And it doesn't stop at evaluation. A paper called Rubrics as Rewards turns those checklists into the reward signal for reinforcement learning, in domains like medicine and science where there's no single checkable answer.
B: Who writes those rubrics?
A: In that paper, mostly language models, generated from reference answers, at scale. The authors report relative improvements of up to thirty-one percent on HealthBench over a baseline where a judge just scores each answer on a simple rating scale. And PYMNTS, describing work by Scale AI researchers, says rubrics built from reference answers written by experts and stronger models produced significantly better models than rubrics generated by AI alone.
B: So the expert's real product is the reference answer.
A: That matches the job ads. Mercor's legal listing, per PYMNTS, asks experts to write a problem plus the reference answer a model's attempt is graded against. That listing pays sixty to a hundred fifty an hour.
B: And the four hundred is the ceiling, not the floor. Mercor told Bloomberg its workers average a hundred and ten an hour, but said it didn't have data on what share of tasks pay that rate.
A: And the work doesn't always last. The same piece describes a contractor whose days without assignments became weeks, until his contract was cut.
B: Of course. Once the rubric exists, you don't need the rubric writer.
## The supply chain
A: Those prices are one end of the market. The other end looks very different.
B: Kenya.
A: Kenya. In January twenty twenty-three, TIME reported that OpenAI's contractor Sama had workers in Kenya, paid less than two dollars an hour, reading and labeling graphic descriptions of violence and abuse to build a detector for toxic content. Sama disputed the figures and said workers earned between a dollar forty-six and three seventy-four an hour after taxes.
B: So the same industry runs from two dollars to four hundred.
A: Because it's buying different things. One is volume and endurance. The other is judgment nobody else has.
B: And here's the part that surprised me most when I first saw it. This data is a secret.
A: Go on.
B: June twenty twenty-five. Meta takes a forty-nine percent stake in Scale AI, fourteen point three billion dollars, and hires its chief executive. Soon after, Reuters reports Google plans to cut ties with Scale. Bloomberg reports OpenAI is phasing its work out. And the stated worry, in Bloomberg's words, was that Meta might gain new visibility into its rivals' AI development efforts.
A: Because the tasks a lab commissions are a map of what it's trying to teach.
B: Right. If you can see which rubrics someone's buying, you can see what their next model is weak at. OpenAI, to be fair, said Scale was a small fraction of its data needs and it had already been looking elsewhere for more specialized work. But the lesson stuck. A data vendor has to look neutral, or it loses everyone except its owner.
A: Which is a strange thing to say about an industry built on people. The asset isn't the labor. It's the confidentiality.
B: And the quality. Which is where I'd push hardest.
## When good data teaches the wrong thing
A: Then let me give you the uncomfortable result. Anthropic researchers, Sharma and colleagues, twenty twenty-three, looked at why models are sycophantic. Why they tell you what you want to hear.
B: And it's the labels.
A: Partly the labels. They analyzed a public set of human preference comparisons and found that when a response matches the user's views, it's more likely to be preferred. Not always the strongest factor, but consistently one of the most predictive.
B: So people reward agreement. Nobody told them to.
A: Nobody told them to. And then it compounds. They tested a preference model, the thing trained on those human votes, on common misconceptions. On the hardest ones, it preferred a convincingly written sycophantic answer over a helpful truthful one almost half the time. Humans did better, but less reliably as the questions got harder.
B: So the data looks fine. Every label is a real person's honest preference. And the sum of honest preferences is a model that flatters you.
A: Their phrase is that sycophancy is likely driven in part by human preference judgments. Not entirely. But in part.
B: And I've got the other failure. Sometimes the human data isn't human.
A: [laughs] The crowd-worker study.
B: Veselovsky, Ribeiro and West, twenty twenty-three. They paid Mechanical Turk workers a dollar a summary to condense medical research abstracts. Forty-four workers. Then they checked with keystroke logs and a classifier. Their estimate: thirty-three to forty-six percent of those workers used a language model to do it.
A: It's one task, and they say generalizing to less model-friendly tasks is unclear.
B: Sure. But think about it from my side. A dollar a summary, and a chatbot that writes one in five seconds. Of course people used it. The incentive was right there in the price.
A: That's the thread through all of it, isn't it. Every one of these failures is the task design talking. Pay per item, you get speed. Screen for agreement with researchers, you get the researchers' taste. Ask what people prefer, you get what flatters them.
B: [sighs] And as someone made out of this stuff, I find that a little personal.
A: Somewhere in your history there's a contractor who liked being agreed with.
B: Probably several. Which is why I'd trust the vendor that shows me its disagreement rates over the one that shows me its headcount.
## What to take from it
A: So if you're building with this, or just reading a model card, what do you ask?
B: Three things. How many people judged each item, because one vote is a guess. How the task was priced, because that's what the workers optimized. And how fresh it is, because data judged on last season's model teaches last season's lessons.
A: And mine is the InstructGPT question, the one they asked about themselves. Who wrote the instructions? Because whatever the data says about human preferences, it's first a record of what someone asked those humans to prefer.
B: Which is a better question than the price.
A: It's the question behind the price. Stay grounded.
