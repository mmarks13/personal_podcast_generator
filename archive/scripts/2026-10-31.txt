## How a model gets copied
A: Okay. Distillation. The authors are Kyle Miller and Colin Shea-Blymyer, at C-SET, published on the twenty-sixth. And I'll say what they say up front: it's meant to inform legislative proposals and the policy debate, but it doesn't name a bill, and it deliberately doesn't settle the argument. It's a map.
B: A map of what, exactly? Because "distillation" means three different things depending on who's yelling.
A: That's the first useful thing it does. Distillation, in general, is training a weaker student model on a stronger teacher model's outputs. There are two flavors. White-box means you have the teacher's insides. Its full scores over every possible next word, its activations, its weights. You're learning from the teacher's whole state of mind. C-SET sets that aside.
B: And black-box is you only get the answer.
A: You only get the answer. The teacher is closed. You can only reach it the way a customer does, through a website or an A-P-I. And C-SET's term for the fight in Washington is adversarial distillation: Chinese labs doing that to American proprietary models, through third parties, while hiding where the traffic comes from.
B: Fine. Walk me through how you'd actually do it.
A: Five steps. One, decide what skill you want to transfer, and pick your student and teacher. Two, gather seed prompts. Questions that represent the skill. Benchmarks, custom prompts, even your own model's outputs. Three, steer the teacher. Ask it to answer the seeds and show its reasoning, or to spin each seed into many similar ones, or to grade your student's attempts. Four, do that at scale, programmatically. Five, train the student on what you collected. Either straight supervised fine-tuning, just imitate the answers, or something with feedback, a reward model, reinforcement learning, on-policy distillation.
B: Here's my first objection. The usual reason to distill is to make something small and cheap. These Chinese models aren't small.
A: That's C-SET's point exactly, and I think it's the most clarifying line in the piece. The students here have hundreds of billions to trillions of parameters. DeepSeek's R1 is over six hundred seventy billion. That's not compression. C-SET's read is that these labs can build a capable base model themselves. What they want is the finishing school. Post-training. The reasoning style, the polish.
B: Which is why everyone's fighting over the reasoning traces.
A: Right. Public reporting, C-SET says, centers on getting the teacher to show its chain of thought. Anthropic reported one unnamed lab sending more than twelve thousand requests, each one testing a different extraction trick. Google pointed to about a hundred thousand prompts in one case involving Gemini.
B: And the big number?
A: Anthropic's. Seven Chinese labs running distillation campaigns, and in the counts C-SET cites, exchanges in the millions per lab. Alibaba at a hundred fifty-one million, Moonshot twenty-three million, DeepSeek twelve point one million.
B: Okay, now convert that into something that matters. Exchanges aren't training data. How many tokens?
A: This is where it gets honest. C-SET works out a theoretical maximum of about twenty-five point six trillion tokens, and then says that's an overstatement. A U-S joint advisory just said "billions of tokens." And then it does the sum I wanted someone to do. It takes an outside estimate, one published on Interconnects, that the campaigns produced a hundred fifty to four hundred billion tokens. C-SET works out that would be fourteen to thirty-six percent of the post-training data behind one Chinese model, Z-dot-A-I's G-L-M four point five. And thirty to eighty percent of its synthetic reasoning data.
B: Wait. If. Assuming all of it got used.
A: Assuming all of it got used. They say so.
B: Because that's the whole ballgame. When I generate synthetic data, a lot of it never survives. Dedupe, filter, throw out what's wrong.
A: C-SET notes filtering is common in the published work, and it says the rest plainly. "U-S companies do not have visibility into the final step of the distillation process." The labs can count what went out the door. Nobody on this side can see what made it into the model.
B: Here's the line in there that stopped me, though. For scale, C-SET mentions that a single Meta employee used two hundred eighty-one billion tokens.
A: One person.
B: One person. Which sits right inside that hundred-fifty-to-four-hundred-billion estimate for an entire alleged campaign. I'm not saying that makes the campaigns small. I'm saying it tells you how cheap a few hundred billion tokens has become. You can't hear "billions of tokens" and conclude anything anymore.
A: And that's the detection problem too. If the attacker reuses a public benchmark as seeds, a classifier spots it. If they write custom prompts, or route real users' traffic, which DeepSeek and Moonshot were both accused of, it looks like normal use. Jailbreak attempts to pry out reasoning are a signal, which is partly why Anthropic introduced new safeguards. And then the attacker adapts.
B: So what does C-SET actually want?
A: Two things. Labs should disclose token counts, and somebody should publish research on how much uplift distillation actually gives. Because the policy question splits on that. If distillation is central to how Chinese labs build models, defenses could really constrain them. If it's peripheral, the defenses matter less for competition. And whether it's theft that calls for government, or a terms-of-service breach companies handle themselves, they leave open.
B: C-SET says Anthropic, OpenAI and Google don't publish those token counts now. And I get why. A token count is a map of their own detection.
A: Then the rules get written without the one number that decides them.
