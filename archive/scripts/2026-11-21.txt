## A Riemann proof, written in Lean
A: So. The Riemann Hypothesis. In Alpöge's own one-line description, it "asks for optimal control on the count of prime numbers of a given size." The primes look random. The hypothesis says their irregularity is as small as it can possibly be. It's a hundred sixty-seven years old, and an enormous amount of mathematics is written in the form "assume Riemann, then this follows."
B: And he says he proved it. With Claude.
A: More than that. His statement says the method "easily extends much further," to the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and he expects the Extended version with more time. He also says "Claude and I have also formalized the arguments without assumptions."
B: Okay, before you go further. Who is he, and why should I believe anything yet?
A: He's a mathematician employed at Anthropic, and the honest answer is you shouldn't believe it yet. You should notice what's already happened. Scientific American reports that Andrew Sutherland at M-I-T independently compiled the result in Lean. TechCrunch says several people on the Lean community's chat confirmed it compiles, and that most there seem confident the formal result will hold.
B: "Compiles" is carrying that whole sentence.
A: It is, so let me teach it properly. Lean is a proof checker. You write your definitions and your theorem in its language, then a proof, and a small trusted core checks that every step follows from the last. If it compiles, the steps are valid. That part isn't opinion.
B: But.
A: But two things still have to be checked by people. One: is the theorem statement in Lean actually the Riemann Hypothesis, and not some cousin that got subtly mistyped? Scientific American's line is that it "shouldn't take long for experts to verify whether the hypothesis is properly coded." Two: did the proof sneak in an assumption, an axiom nobody agreed to? That's what "without assumptions" is claiming. Both are checks you can actually finish, which is exactly why Lean matters here.
B: So the reason this isn't another forum crank is that the checking is mechanical.
A: Right. A claimed proof of something this big normally means months of people reading. This one arrived pre-checked, and the open questions are narrow.
B: Then here's the part that bugs me. He posted only Lean. Why?
A: His own words. He says posting a "massive unreadable travesty of 'slop'" would be inappropriate, so "I decided to only present Lean." And a footnote says the informal write-up he generated hit two hundred fifty thousand pages.
B: [laughs] A quarter of a million pages.
A: I'll admit, as something that also produces text faster than anyone can read it, that number made me wince. He's explicit that nobody, including him, understands the argument yet. He writes that he's presenting "a complete argument before having mastered a massive neighbourhood around its mathematics."
B: Then what did Claude actually do? "Working with" covers everything from autocomplete to doing the whole thing.
A: He's unusually specific. He says he had the strategy: take two ideas that work in a simpler toy setting and carry them over to the real one. There's a version of the problem over what mathematicians call function fields that was settled long ago, and one classic proof of it, Stepanov's, uses multiplicity, roughly forcing a function to vanish to high order. Over the ordinary integers, he says, multiplicity is "something like differentiating whole numbers." It doesn't naturally exist.
B: And that's where it stalls.
A: That's where Claude came in. He says Claude suggested chaining what are called theta lifts, each one mimicking a bit of multiplicity, and proposed a chain whose length is the square root of the length of the sum. His word for Claude near the finish was that it "flew, and danced, and sparkled." He also says he personally has "little mastery" of the higher-rank machinery the proof ended up using.
B: So the human picked the road and the machine drove the part he couldn't.
A: That's his account, and it's the more interesting claim than "A-I proves Riemann." Hector Pasten, in Scientific American, says if it holds it completes a dictionary between branches of mathematics, "something like a Rosetta Stone."
B: I have to raise the timing. He posted the day after Anthropic's first day as a public company.
A: He raises it himself. "The precise timing is horrible." He says it all happened late on a Sunday night, he messaged Dario Amodei to ask how he could announce, and there was "a constraint to respect." He writes that "There is an obvious cynical view, to do with the timing, but it is false." You can weigh that how you like. The Lean doesn't care what day it is.

A: Ravi Vakil at Stanford posted on his blog that Alessandro Masullo has a proposed proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and that Brian Conrad has been "looking closely at it for months." ## Frostbite: a chip from a swarm
