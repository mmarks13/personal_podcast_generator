## A ruling nobody wrote
B: The case is Phillips versus Parlade, decided yesterday in the federal district court for Nevada by Judge Gloria Navarro. Volokh has the write-up. The plaintiff had lost in state court, and one of the things he argued in his federal suit was that the state judge — his words — relied wholly on artificial intelligence to issue a judicial ruling, without any discretionary human thought. He framed it as an unlawful delegation of the judge's duties.
A: Before you go on. That's an allegation.
B: That's an allegation, and I want to nail that down because everything after it depends on it. At this stage the court takes the allegations as given and asks whether the case can proceed at all. Nobody has found that this judge used an A I. Nobody examined the order. That's not what the opinion is about.
A: Good. Continue.
B: Judicial immunity runs on a test with four factors: whether the act is a normal judicial function, whether it happened in the judge's chambers, whether the controversy centered on the case pending before the judge, and whether it arose out of confrontation with the judge in an official capacity. Navarro's answer is one sentence long and it's the whole ruling. Quote: issuing a judicial ruling is clearly a normal judicial function.
A: And that's it.
B: That's it. The plaintiff offered no authority that A I use removes immunity, so immunity applies and the case is dismissed. And the background rule the opinion recites is the one worth hearing in full, because people don't believe it when you paraphrase. Quote: judges enjoy absolute immunity from civil liability, even if their action was in error, done maliciously, or in excess of their authority.
A: Maliciously.
B: Maliciously is in there.
A: [beat] Then I'm going to say something you won't like. This is the doctrine working perfectly, and the A I is doing no work in the analysis at all.
B: Say the rest of it.
A: Absolute judicial immunity is very old and the reason for it is not that judges are wonderful. It's that a judge who can be personally sued by the loser cannot rule against anyone. Every single case would generate a follow-on suit from whoever lost. The system would seize inside a year. So the rule is deliberately absolute — error, malice, excess of authority, all of it covered — and the remedies are elsewhere: you appeal, or you file a disciplinary complaint. Volokh says exactly that, by the way. He's careful that this is about immunity from a federal suit, not about whether relying on an A I was proper. The state appellate route and the state disciplinary route both stay open.
B: All of which I agree with.
A: Then what's your objection?
B: My objection is that the remedies you just listed both assume the record shows what happened. Appellate review reads the order. A disciplinary complaint needs something to complain about. And there is nothing in this structure that requires an order to disclose that a model drafted it. So the check is real, and the check is blind.
A: That's not new either. Judges have had clerks for a century and a half. Nothing requires an order to disclose that a twenty-six-year-old wrote the first draft.
B: [laughs] That is a genuinely good counter and I've been chewing on it since I read the piece. Here's where I land. The clerk is inside the accountability structure. The clerk went to law school, has a bar card, works in the building, and can be asked. If the clerk fabricates a citation, there's a person who did that. What we're describing is a drafting process with the same invisibility and none of the attachment.
A: You're describing an accountability sink.
B: I'm describing exactly that, and I'd generalize it past courts, because this is the pattern everyone deploying agents is about to meet. Immunity — or in a company, authority — attaches to the role. It does not attach to the process inside the role. So the moment the role is protected, there is no incentive whatsoever to disclose what happened inside it. The output looks identical either way.
A: And your fix is a disclosure rule.
B: My fix is that this doesn't get solved by lawsuits, because the lawsuits will keep losing, correctly. It gets solved by procedure — a rule that says if a model drafted it, the order says so. Which is not a rule anyone has to invent from scratch. Courts already govern what has to appear on the face of a document.
A: I'd watch the state courts for that, then, not the federal ones.
B: That's where I'd watch. And I'd expect the first one to come out of a judicial conference rather than a case, because a case can't reach it.
