## GLM-5.3's attention: less reading, same storage
B: Same weights, different question. SemiAnalysis's Kimbo Chen and colleagues went through the model to work out what it costs to keep running. The model is seven hundred forty-four billion parameters in total, forty billion active per token, routing each token to eight of two hundred fifty-six experts.
A: The mixture-of-experts shape.
B: And we did that split last week with the Snapdragon phone chip. Routing cuts the bytes you read per token, not the weights you have to store. The claim here is that GLM's attention has exactly the same split, which I didn't expect.
A: Walk me through the attention.
B: It uses DeepSeek Sparse Attention. Normal attention, every new token looks at every earlier token. At a long context, that's the expensive part. The sparse version adds a cheap scout. SemiAnalysis calls it the lightning indexer. It projects the queries and keys down to much smaller versions and quickly scores every earlier token. Then it keeps the top few, and the real, expensive attention only runs over those.
A: So it reads a handful carefully instead of reading everything.
B: Right. But to choose the handful, the scout has to look at everything. Which means everything has to be sitting in the G-P-U's high-bandwidth memory, H-B-M. SemiAnalysis's line is that sparse attention "reduces KV cache memory and bandwidth requirements" at the attention step itself, "but it does not reduce the overall memory capacity usage." The KV cache is the model's stored notes on every earlier token.
A: So the bill for reading goes down, and the bill for keeping stays put.
B: Exactly that. And Z-dot-A-I trimmed the scout, too. They call it IndexShare. Every four attention layers share one indexer instead of each having its own. SemiAnalysis reports that cuts the indexer's cache and compute by seventy-five percent and boosts throughput one and a half to one point eight times.
A: [skeptical] That's a training change, not a free lunch. Four layers now have to agree on what matters.
B: They trained it for that. The indexer learns to match the average of what the four layers attend to. Whether four layers ever want different things is the obvious question, and the article doesn't settle it. Here's the bit I actually care about. If you can't avoid storing everything, you store it somewhere cheaper. SGLang, the open-source serving engine, has a feature called HiSparse. It treats the G-P-U's memory as a cache and spills the cold parts of the KV cache out to ordinary host memory, the memory attached to the C-P-U. It works like a least-recently-used cache, and it loads the next layer's notes while the current layer computes.
A: That's the oldest trick in operating systems. Keep the hot pages close, the cold ones farther away, and hope the working set fits.
B: And the measurement says the hope mostly holds. On Nvidia's G-B-two-hundred, going from eight simultaneous requests to sixteen, the share of prompt tokens served from G-P-U memory fell from ninety point three percent to fifty-four point eight. The share served from host memory rose from six to forty point three. And the overall hit rate stayed above ninety-five percent.
A: So the pressure moved off the expensive chip and onto the cheap one.
B: Which is where I owe you a correction. In August I told you data centers buy H-B-M, not the memory in your desktop, so blaming A-I for memory prices was lazy. That's still true of the chip. But a serving trick that makes ordinary host memory the second tier of the KV cache puts data centers back in the market for ordinary memory. SemiAnalysis's own framing is sparse savings, persistent demand.
A: One serving engine, one model, and a paywall over the rest of the article. Is that enough to move your position?
B: It moves the mechanism, not the price forecast. I'll only claim that. Efficiency made the attention cheaper to read, and the memory bill spilled over to the next shelf. At sixteen users, forty percent of the prompt lives off the G-P-U.
