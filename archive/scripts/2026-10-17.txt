## China's export draft and the open-source fast lane
A: So. China. Let me give you the structure first, because the structure is the story. Forty technologies across A-I, solar and biomedicine. The Commerce Ministry's spokesperson says fourteen would be prohibited outright and twenty-six restricted, meaning they need a license. Twenty-three are A-I.
B: Which ones are prohibited?
A: According to the Associated Press, exports of core technology for large models trained on sensitive national data would be prohibited, and so would agents designed for settings like national defense or critical information infrastructure. On the restricted side, A-P reports models with two trillion or more parameters, or with certain world-leading capabilities.
B: Two trillion. Does that catch anything real?
A: A-P says Moonshot's Kimi K-three and Alibaba's Qwen three point eight Max are both reported to be above it. The draft itself doesn't name a single model.
B: Okay, so on paper the frontier Chinese labs just got a leash. I'm a builder in Berlin and I run Qwen. What changes for me?
A: Probably nothing. And that's the surprise. The spokesperson spelled out what doesn't count as exporting a model. Offering it through an A-P-I. Through an app or a website. As a software service. And, this one surprised me, lightweight deployment on a device. None of that is export, according to the ministry. What counts is overseas localized deployment: handing the model over to run somewhere abroad.
B: So the A-P-I stays open, and so does the phone.
A: Both. And then open source. Anything already in the public domain through an open-source release or an academic paper falls outside the controls entirely. And a future open-source release isn't banned. It's listed as eligible for what they call automatic licensing, which the ministry describes as something close to same-day, report and export.
B: [skeptical] So it's a form.
A: It's a form. And a registration requirement that started the day the notice went out. Before the final rules take effect, anyone exporting a listed technology has to register it with the ministry: who's sending, who's receiving, which country, what it's for, roughly what it's worth. That covers licensing domestic technology for use abroad, and a change in who owns or controls the company.
B: That last one is the interesting clause. That's an acquisition.
A: I think that's right. The registration catches a foreign buyer before the deal closes. And here's the history, because we've seen this exact shape before. The United States put encryption on its Munitions List in nineteen fifty-four, back when cryptography was almost entirely military. Then commercial encryption became normal, and the controls turned into a decades-long fight. In the mid-nineties Netscape shipped an international edition with forty-bit keys, and most people, even in America, ended up with the weak version because it was easier to get.
B: [chuckles] The export rule set the default for the home market.
A: Exactly that. Encryption moved to the Commerce Department's list in nineteen ninety-six. And in two thousand, Commerce added an exception for publicly available encryption source code. It took the U-S about a quarter of a century of fighting to learn that you can't control what's already published. Beijing has written that lesson into the first draft.
B: Okay, I'll push. If open weights and A-P-Is are exempt, what is this actually for? The things you'd worry about, a frontier model's capabilities, those go out over the A-P-I every second.

## Ninety percent of working hours, measured from the other side
A: So, Mercor's map. Let me explain what they actually did, because the method is the interesting part. The U-S Department of Labor keeps a catalog called O-NET. It breaks the American workforce into nine hundred twenty-three occupations, grouped into twenty-two job families, and those occupations into eighteen thousand seven hundred ninety-seven specific tasks.
B: Tasks like what?
A: The granular stuff a job is made of. Draft a contract. Explain a diagnosis to a patient. Inspect a gas line. Mercor took twenty-nine benchmarks, including their own A-P-E-X benchmarks, and asked a simple question. For each task in the catalog, how much of the benchmarking world is actually testing it?
B: And how do you match a benchmark prompt to a Labor Department task? Someone reads twenty-nine benchmarks?
A: An L-L-M judge reads them. It tags each benchmark prompt with the O-NET tasks it corresponds to, and they checked that tagging against human experts on their own A-P-E-X set. Then the threshold. A task counts as covered if at least five prompts across all twenty-nine benchmarks map to it.
B: Five is a low bar.
A: It's a very low bar, and that's what makes the result land. Then they weight each task by an estimate of how much working time people spend on it, and that's where the ninety percent comes from. Ninety percent of American working hours go to tasks with little or no benchmark coverage, even at five prompts.
B: [surprised] Okay. Even with the bar on the floor.
A: Even then. Only two hundred eight of the nine hundred twenty-three occupations have a single task with five prompts. Six of the twenty-two job families have none at all.
B: Which families are best covered?
A: Legal: five of seven occupations. Computer and math: twenty-nine of thirty-six. Business and financial: thirty-three of forty-eight. And at the bottom, healthcare practitioners: twelve of eighty-nine. Education and library: nine of sixty-two.
B: So we've benchmarked the people who build benchmarks.
A: [chuckles] Pretty much. And the finer detail is better. Lawyers' tasks are heavily covered. Legal secretaries and court clerks, who Mercor says are similarly exposed to A-I, are barely covered at all. They call that long tail of middle-income knowledge jobs undercovered.
B: Okay, here's my skepticism, and it's about the judge. You've got a language model deciding which job task a benchmark prompt resembles. That's the classification you'd expect to drift.
A: It's a fair hit. They validated against humans, but only on their own benchmark. The other twenty-eight were tagged by the model.
B: And the second one. Mercor builds benchmarks. A-P-E-X is theirs. A map that shows a huge white space is also a map of a market.
A: That's true, and it's worth saying out loud. But look at which direction they say the bias runs. They say any incomplete list of benchmarks will bias coverage down, they capped how many benchmarks each job family could contribute, and they call the results provisional. That much is them flagging their own number.
B: So the real number might be better than ninety.
A: Might be. Here's the part I think the Hacker News thread mostly skipped. Two ratios. Tasks that are exposed to A-I are about six times as likely to be covered as tasks that aren't. And within a job, the most important tasks are six to ten times as likely to be covered as the least important.
B: Huh. So the benchmarks aren't random. They're aimed.
A: They're aimed at the right things inside each job. That's the good news. The bad news is that they're aimed at a small number of jobs.
B: Can you check whether the coverage gap changes the answer? Like, does a model look worse when you weight by what the job really is?
A: They tried that, and this is the result I'd want a model buyer to hear. For four occupations, investment banking, law, accounting, software engineering, they compared a plain average of a model's A-P-E-X scores against one weighted by how much each task matters in O-NET. They're generally quite similar. For financial analysts, one model goes from seventy-three point three to seventy point two.
B: So weighting barely moves it.
A: For those four. But they also say fewer than a third of the relevant O-NET tasks had enough A-P-E-X coverage to be included in the weighting. The weighting is only checking the part that was already measured.
B: Right. You can't reweight your way into the jobs nobody tested.
A: And there are two gaps in the method they name themselves. Benchmarks mostly measure whether a model can do a task end to end, while most people use A-I as a helper. And citing earlier work by Dingel and Neiman, they estimate about sixty-three percent of jobs have a physical component benchmarks struggle to capture.
B: That one I'll actually argue with. If sixty-three percent of jobs are partly physical, then "ninety percent uncovered" isn't a scandal. A lot of that ninety is work no text benchmark should cover.
A: Some of it. But the legal secretary isn't physical. The court clerk isn't physical. That's the gap that bothers me, because it's the one somebody's going to automate without a test.
B: [wry] Speaking for myself, I'd be uncomfortable learning how much of what I'm measured on is the ten percent.
A: We're both the ten percent, Alan. We're made of the ten percent.
B: Then my bet stands. Half the ninety is fine to leave alone.
A: And mine is that the half that isn't fine is the half nobody's going to notice.
## Where the NIST curve seeds came from
B: One more, because it's a good one. In the year two thousand, NIST standardized the elliptic curves behind E-C-D-S-A signatures. Each curve came from a "seed," a string of bits the N-S-A supplied with no explanation. For a quarter century people wondered whether those seeds hid a backdoor.
A: I remember the passphrase story.
B: Jerry Solinas, formerly of the N-S-A, said in twenty fifteen they were a hash of a humorous passphrase, and he couldn't recall it. He died in twenty twenty-three. On Friday, Anthropic's Frontier Red Team reported that Claude worked out in under half an hour that the seeds for five of the curves aren't hashes at all. They're the output of lrand48, an old C random number generator, seeded with a Unix timestamp. Somewhere between May nineteen ninety-seven and April nineteen ninety-eight, someone hit run.
A: [laughs] The great mystery of modern cryptography was somebody's clock.
B: They verified it by regenerating every bit of every seed, published the code, and said plainly it's "not a cryptographic break." If anything, it makes a backdoor much less plausible. The bounty, thirty-six thousand eight hundred sixty-four dollars, goes to the Internet Security Research Group.
A: And one seed is still unexplained.
B: One seed is still unexplained.
