## Learning by deletion
A: The deletion paper. It's by Jack Lu, Zhenbang Yang, Mike Lasby, Tejas Pote, Yani Ioannou and Mengye Ren, posted on arXiv on the seventh. And to see why it's strange, you need one piece of the transformer. Inside every block there's a feed-forward layer, and in most modern models one of its parts is called the gate. Think of it as a mixing desk. A wide bank of channels, and the gate decides how loud each one comes through to the next stage.
B: Okay.
A: Normal fine-tuning moves every fader on every desk, a little. LoRA, the popular cheap method, adds a small extra panel of adjustments on top. This paper says: don't add anything, don't nudge anything. Just cut some wires inside the gate. Each individual weight is either kept or set to zero.
B: Only ever zero.
A: Only zero. That's the whole adapter, a list of which weights to cut. They call it Deletion Tuning. And the learned cut rate came out between roughly three and five hundredths of a percent. Less than one weight in two thousand.
B: How do you even train that? You can't take a gradient of "on or off."
A: You soften it. Each weight gets a score. During training the weight is multiplied by a smooth version of on-or-off, and over training that curve is sharpened until it's nearly a switch. At the end you use the hard switch. The authors report the soft and hard versions differ by under a tenth of a point. And they don't start from random scores. They seed them from a known pruning method called Wanda, which ranks weights by how little they seem to matter.
B: So pruning, but aimed.
A: Pruning has always been a way to make a model smaller. This uses it to teach the model something. And the numbers are why people noticed. Reinforcement learning on grade-school math with Llama three, eight B: full fine-tuning gets about eighty-three point two. Deletion gets eighty-three point six. On a harder math set with Qwen two point five, fourteen B, deletion edges full fine-tuning again, about fifty-five point four to fifty-four point nine. Same story on a thirty-billion mixture-of-experts model.
B: [skeptical] Okay, I want the losing rows.
A: They printed them. At the small end it loses. On Llama three point two, one B, full fine-tuning gets sixty and a half, deletion fifty-four. The authors say the gap "closes with scale."
B: So the bigger the model, the more there is to cut.
A: That's the reading. And here's the part I'd keep. Wanda's scores by themselves, the starting point, barely move the model. Within two and a half points of where it started. The learned cut gains as much as fifty-three points. So it isn't "remove the junk." It's choosing exactly which few wires to cut.
B: Fine. What does it cost me?
A: Storage is the headline. For Llama eight B, the deletion adapter is about eight megabytes. A full fine-tuned checkpoint is around thirty gigabytes. The authors say it's smaller than even the smallest LoRA they compared, about ten megabytes.
B: Eight megabytes. That's a skill you could email.
A: And it forgets less. The authors report that under reinforcement learning, accuracy on earlier tasks stayed within a point of the base model. Full fine-tuning ended one to three points below. LoRA and their random-sparse baseline lost up to five point four.
B: Okay, here's my problem. Training memory. One trainable score per masked weight is a lot of scores.
A: They say so. Training memory is less than full fine-tuning but more than LoRA. So it's cheap to ship and not cheap to make. There's no wall-clock cost in the paper, and I didn't find a code release linked.
B: And the deeper question. How does deleting things add a capability? You can't cut a wire and get a new circuit.
A: That's where my analogy breaks, and it should. Cutting a wire can reroute the signal. In a network that big, a few zeros change which paths dominate. The authors report deletion beats the base model at every number of attempts they tested, and they read that as solving problems the base model never solved. I'd put it more cautiously. Whatever the capability is, the paths for it were already in there. The cut chose among them.
B: Which is a little unsettling if you're made of weights.
A: [wry] Somewhere in me there's a list of a few million zeros that would make me better at arithmetic. I try not to think about it.
B: What I want next is the experiment they didn't run. They suggest combining masks for different skills. Untested.
A: Untested. One paper, a conference submission, no code yet. It's a striking result, and a long way from a tool.
