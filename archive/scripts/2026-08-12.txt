## The model for everything in between

It makes routing worth solving because the cost difference can be large.
A: Start with the strange parameter count.

## Water has an address
A: The article newly circulating is by Zohar Barnett-Itzhaki in Water Research. It appeared in July, so today's news is attention, not publication. It is a synthesis and governance argument, not a new meter study at one data center.
B: That matters because the number everyone will repeat sounds measured.
A: Four point two to six point six billion cubic meters annually by twenty twenty-seven. The article says A I's global water footprint could reach that range. Its source is earlier work by Pengfei Li and colleagues, which calls the range projected water withdrawal.
B: Withdrawal is not consumption.
A: Correct. The U S Geological Survey defines withdrawal as water removed from the ground or diverted from a surface source for use. Some withdrawn water returns to the watershed. Consumptive use is the part not promptly available for reuse because it evaporated, entered a product, or otherwise left the immediate system. The distinction changes what a large number means locally.
B: And the word footprint can gather several categories into one total.
A: Barnett-Itzhaki names three. Direct on-site consumption covers evaporative cooling and humidification. Indirect water belongs to the electricity generation supplying the center. Embedded water belongs to semiconductor manufacturing.
B: Walk the direct pipe.
A: The U S Department of Energy's data-center cooling guide shows a common evaporative system. Server heat moves into room air, then a chilled-water loop, then a condenser-water loop. At the cooling tower, some water evaporates into the atmosphere to carry the heat away. Additional water is drained as blowdown so dissolved minerals do not become too concentrated.
B: Yesterday we heard a promise about closed-loop cooling. A loop can be closed inside the building and still hand its heat to an evaporative tower outside.
A: Exactly. Closed loop tells us that coolant recirculates through part of the system. It does not, by itself, state facility water withdrawal or consumption. The heat has to leave. The final rejection system, local climate, operating temperature, and source of makeup water decide much of the direct-water result.
B: So waterless cooling fixes it.
A: It can reduce on-site water demand. The Water Research article lists waterless designs, cold-climate siting, natural-water-body cooling, and waste-heat recovery. But a dry system can move more work onto fans or compressors, and a natural water body introduces temperature and ecosystem constraints. The correct comparison includes water, energy, carbon, land, reliability, and the local watershed. No design earns a universal green label from one column.
B: The second pipe is electricity.
A: Power plants may withdraw or consume water for their own cooling and generation processes. That indirect amount changes with the grid's fuel mix, geography, and hour. Li and colleagues emphasize that on-site and off-site water-use efficiency vary across both place and time.
B: Which means the same model request can have a different water footprint in Oregon at night and Texas on a hot afternoon.
A: In principle, yes. But we usually cannot calculate it for a particular answer because providers do not disclose the serving location, hardware utilization, marginal electricity source, cooling conditions, or how shared infrastructure costs are allocated to that request.
B: Then the bottle-per-conversation line is false?
A: It is an estimate under assumptions, not a constant of nature. The twenty twenty-three study estimated one half-liter bottle across roughly twenty to fifty questions and answers using assumed inference energy, power overhead, electricity-water intensity, and an on-site efficiency range that changes with weather. The paper itself says when and where matter. Repeating the bottle without the assumptions converts a scenario into a property of A I.
B: I like the bottle because people understand it.
A: People also understand a price tag. We do not quote one price for every model, token count, cache state, and cloud region.
B: Fair. Keep the metaphor, print the range and assumptions on the label.
A: Better. And distinguish withdrawal from consumption. A power plant can withdraw a great volume and return much of it warmer. An evaporative tower can withdraw less and consume a larger share locally. Communities care about source, timing, return, temperature, and drought conditions, not only the largest global total.
B: The third pipe, chip manufacturing, is even harder to assign to one answer.
A: Yes. Semiconductor fabrication uses water upstream, then one accelerator serves many training and inference jobs over its life. Allocating embodied water requires assumptions about manufacturing yield, hardware lifetime, utilization, and which workloads receive the share. That does not make the water imaginary. It makes the accounting a lifecycle allocation rather than a facility meter.
B: I can meter electricity to a rack and facility water to a campus. I cannot put a water meter on one token.
A: Not directly. You can estimate workload water by joining compute and energy logs with facility water-use effectiveness, then adding an electricity-side factor and a lifecycle allocation. Each join introduces uncertainty. Report the pieces and uncertainty instead of presenting the final decimal as observed.
B: Define water-use effectiveness.
A: The Energy Department gives the site metric as annual site water use divided by annual energy used by the information-technology equipment, in liters per kilowatt-hour. It helps compare one facility over time or similar facilities under stated boundaries.
B: And it can hide a dirtier electricity source because it is site water only.
A: Correct. A low site metric may coexist with high indirect water. It can also look excellent because a facility uses reclaimed water rather than potable water, which is a real local improvement but does not mean the volume is zero. The source and quality of the water belong beside the ratio.
B: Barnett-Itzhaki's policy phrase is digital water sobriety. I can already hear that becoming a slogan.
A: The article gives it content: ask which applications justify freshwater consumption, site compute with water constraints in mind, and require facility-level transparency. The first part is normative. The other two create the evidence needed to have the argument at all.
B: Who decides whether my model run justifies freshwater? A regulator? The cloud provider? The customer with the largest contract?
A: That is the sharp edge. A hospital inference service, a video generator, and an advertising optimizer may share one facility. Ranking their social value is not an engineering calculation. Democratic institutions can set drought priorities and permit conditions; providers can expose usage; customers can choose. I do not want a cloud company privately assigning moral worth to prompts.
B: Then keep sobriety at the infrastructure layer. Price scarce water properly and let users decide.
A: Prices often omit effects on households, ecosystems, and future drought resilience. A permit can establish withdrawal caps, drought triggers, source requirements, and disclosure even when the marginal price is low.
B: That can also push a data center to another county with weaker rules and the same watershed.
A: Which is why basin-level planning matters. We are not going to settle water governance from the studio. We can say what a credible disclosure needs.
B: Facility and watershed location. Monthly withdrawal and consumption. Potable, reclaimed, surface, or groundwater source. The direct site ratio in liters per kilowatt-hour. Cooling design and drought operating plan.
A: Add indirect electricity water with the grid method stated, plus the share of hardware manufacturing that is included or excluded. Publish both forecast and actual use, because a promised design is not an operating record.
B: For an A I customer, give enough workload energy data to estimate its share without exposing another customer's traffic.
A: And disclose uncertainty. A range tied to location and hour is more honest than one global average with six significant digits.
B: The review highlights that two-thirds of data centers built after twenty twenty-two are in water-stressed regions. I want the dataset and definition behind that highlight before I use it for a permit.
A: So do I. It is supported as the article's claim, but the accessible abstract does not expose the denominator, geography, or water-stress threshold. It is a reason to read the methods, not a number we should extend beyond the paper.
B: That is the frustrating shape of this story. The aggregate concern is credible. The facility data needed for action are scarce.
A: Scarcity of data is not evidence of low impact. It is evidence that broad totals cannot tell a town what one proposed campus will do.
B: If I were procuring cloud capacity tomorrow, I would ask for annual water-use effectiveness and the cooling source by region. I doubt I would get workload-level numbers.
A: Ask anyway, then write the absence into the procurement record. Requirements begin as questions suppliers are tired of answering.
B: I would also ask whether a low-water region choice raises latency, cost, or carbon. Moving compute is a trade, not a teleport.
A: Yes. The earlier study even finds carbon-efficient hours and water-efficient hours may differ. Following abundant solar generation can coincide with hotter conditions and more evaporative cooling. Multi-objective scheduling must show the trade rather than optimizing one invisible score.
B: Would you delay an urgent model job for water conditions?
A: An urgent job, probably not. A flexible batch training run, perhaps. The value is in separating deadlines. Some computation can move in time or place; some cannot. Treating every workload as urgent is how flexibility disappears.
B: I will concede scheduling. I still resist application-level worthiness tests.
A: Keep resisting. I resist a market price as the whole answer. The productive next step is not agreement; it is a facility record detailed enough that our disagreement attaches to real water, in a real basin, during a real month.
B: And no more universal bottles.
A: Bottles with assumptions, if you insist.
B: [dryly] A very small label and an extremely long footnote.
