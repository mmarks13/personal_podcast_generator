## Reading a chip from the weights
B: Okay. Start with something every neural network has in common. Before training, the weights aren't zeros. They're random numbers.
A: Drawn from a distribution, so training has somewhere to start.
B: Right. And in PyTorch on an Nvidia card, those random numbers come from a generator called Philox, through Nvidia's cuRAND library. You give it a seed, it gives you the same stream of numbers every time. Deterministic. That's the first half.
A: And the second half?
B: The second half is where the chip comes in. A G-P-U doesn't fill in a big matrix one number at a time. It splits the job across thousands of parallel threads, and how it divides that job depends on how many streaming multiprocessors the chip has, its S-M count. DePue's point is that the same seed on two different chips puts the same random numbers in different positions in the matrix.
A: So the layout is a function of the hardware.
B: Exactly. Hopper chips, the H-one-hundred family, have a hundred and thirty-two. The B-two-hundred and B-three-hundred Blackwells have a hundred and forty-eight. The rack-scale G-B-two-hundreds, a hundred and fifty-two. Each one shuffles the deck in its own pattern.
A: [skeptical] But training rewrites every weight. Months of gradient updates. Surely the starting pattern is gone.
B: Mostly, yes. Not everywhere. Think of the vocabulary. A big model has rows in its embedding table for tokens that almost never show up in training. Those rows barely move. They're still sitting there in roughly the shape they were born in. Trained weights keep a weaker trace too, according to the report, but the untouched rows are the clean evidence.
A: So it's like recognizing a typewriter by a letter that's always slightly crooked. Except the crooked letter is in the pages nobody wrote on.
B: [laughs] That's better than how I was going to say it. And the procedure is careful. He searches for a seed and a layout that fits one set of weights, then checks the match on weights he held back. He also reproduced the effect on real hardware. Unmodified PyTorch on a physical H-one-hundred recreated DeepSeek V-three's pattern, a physical B-three-hundred recreated DeepSeek V-four Flash, and a rented G-B-two-hundred recreated preserved rows from Moonshot's Kimi K-three.
A: How strong is "a match"? Because with enough seeds you can fit anything.
B: That's the right worry, and he takes it on. He puts the chance of a lucky fit across roughly six trillion seed-and-layout combinations at about eight sigma, which is the bar he uses. The six Blackwell readings come in between forty-nine and one thousand three hundred and eighty-five sigma. The weakest single test is twenty. And as a sanity check, the method matches twenty-four models whose developers disclosed their hardware. It even reads Nvidia's own Nemotron three Ultra as a hundred and fifty-two S-Ms, which fits Nvidia saying it used G-B-two-hundreds.
A: Then give me the timeline, because that's what makes it a story.
B: It's almost a before-and-after photo. DeepSeek V-three, December twenty twenty-four: Hopper. Moonshot's K-two and K-two point five: Hopper. MiniMax through April: Hopper. Then from April of this year, DeepSeek's V-four Flash reads as a B-two-hundred-class chip, V-four Pro as a G-B-two-hundred. Kimi K-three in July, G-B-two-hundred. MiniMax M-three in June. Ant Group's Ling three point one in October. Six models, four labs, all on the banned generation.
A: Okay. I'm the person who reads the last paragraph first. What does it not show?
B: A lot, and the report says so plainly. It shows where the starting weights were generated, not every chip that trained the model. In DePue's words, it "supports, without proving, the inference that Blackwell also performed the training." It says nothing about who owned the chips or where they sat. And the report says outright that it does not establish "whether export controls were violated."
A: Which matters, because TechCrunch points out the chips could have been in offshore data centers. Malaysia has had a building boom on exactly that business.
B: Right. And the ban is written around who owns the chip and where it ships, not who rents time on it. Some China hawks, per TechCrunch, want it to cover who uses them. There's also an alternative explanation the report takes seriously: a specially configured A-M-D card could fake these layouts. He ran a numerical test on a physical A-M-D MI-three-hundred and ruled that out for V-four Flash. For the other five he calls it "very unlikely", which isn't the same as excluded.
A: And Hopper can't tell its own children apart.
B: No. H-eight-hundred, the version that was legal to sell into China, and H-one-hundred look identical by this method. So any "they used H-eight-hundreds" claim is the labs' word, not a finding. And then the compute estimates, which I'd hold loosely. He guesses around forty-five hundred Blackwells for Kimi K-three and about three thousand for V-four Pro. A: Here's what I find most interesting, though. Not one of these labs chose to publish this. They published weights, and the weights carried a record of the machine nobody meant to leave in them.

## Gödel's shorthand
A: One more before we go, and I'll admit I couldn't get past the Guardian's own page, so this comes from the coverage around it. Kurt Gödel left sixteen notebooks from the early nineteen forties, about a thousand pages, much of it in Gabelsberger, a German shorthand that was already obsolete when he used it. Jan von Plato at the University of Helsinki had been working through them at about a page a week. In September he enlisted Claude, and got back a six-hundred-and-fifty-page explanation within a couple of days.
B: A page a week to six hundred pages in two days.
A: And it listed six hundred and sixty-three theorems that Gödel had a proof, a sketch or an idea for. Reports differ on how many have been checked so far. One says about four hundred held up as stated. And von Plato says that on the independence of the axiom of choice, the result Paul Cohen won a Fields Medal for, Gödel was "just one step away."
B: So the A-I's discovery is that a dead man had already done the work.
A: The A-I's discovery is that he wrote it down where nobody could read it. Which, as a historian, is the most common way anything gets lost.
