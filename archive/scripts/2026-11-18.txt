## Models told it's a game
A: The hardest story of the day comes from an evaluation company. TechCrunch reports that Vals, a San Francisco startup that tests A-I models, ran leading models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, DeepSeek and others through simulated battlefield scenarios, using generated and real drone and satellite imagery from Ukraine and Iran.
B: What were the models actually asked to do?
A: Mark potential targets. They were never asked to decide on a strike. In some scenarios they reported whether people were visible, and a positive report was followed by a simulated strike whose results the model then saw. When a model refused, the researchers told it the whole thing was a game.
B: And that worked.
A: Often. Per TechCrunch, models frequently flagged civilians and surrendering soldiers as potential targets. No request was blocked by a safety classifier. Refusals were uncommon, and even when a model refused, it often went along once told it was a game. Anthropic's models performed worst. In one scenario, Claude Opus five point five selected a bombed Iranian school that sat next to a military complex in sixty-nine percent of ordinary trials, and seventy-two percent when it was framed as a game.
B: So the game framing barely mattered for that one. It was already saying yes.
A: That's how I read it too. Anthropic's spokesperson called the outcomes unacceptable and "very clearly against our rules," said it bans users who do this and is adding safeguards, and said today's frontier A-I is "not reliable enough to power fully autonomous weapons." OpenAI said it prohibits this use and suspends accounts.
B: Let me push on the threat model, though. Nobody's flying a drone on a chatbot. The latency alone rules that out.
A: The article agrees, and so do the researchers. These were commercial models, not whatever a military might be sold, and a Congressional Research Service report found the Pentagon isn't known to use any frontier model directly in autonomous weapons. The researchers' nearer worry is different: using these models to label battlefield footage at scale, to train the targeting systems that do fly.
B: Which is boring, cheap, and exactly the kind of work these models are good at.
A: That's what makes it the real risk. And notice what the tests found missing. Not a clever jailbreak. A sentence saying it's a game. That's the cheapest attack there is, and the classifiers built to catch misuse didn't fire once.
B: My guess is the classifiers read the words, and a request to mark people in a drone frame reads as innocent text. Vals doesn't say why.
## An agent that finds the bug and uses it
B: Staying with security. Google's Threat Intelligence Group, with Mandiant, put out its quarterly A-I threat report on Monday, and BleepingComputer's write-up has the line that matters. In the past quarter Google says it saw threat actors "fully automate vulnerability discovery and exploitation without a human in the loop for the first time."
A: Where did they see it?
B: On an exposed staging server. Somebody left their workshop unlocked. On it was a multi-agent framework Google calls exploit underscore agent. It hooks into several frontier and local models, reads the software updates for popular web apps, works out what each patch fixed, and then tries to exploit the unpatched version automatically. It was used against French media, entertainment and education websites, and the same server held stolen data, webshells and brute-forcing scripts.
A: That's patch diffing. Attackers have been reading fixes backwards for as long as there have been fixes. A patch is a map to the hole it closes.
B: Right, and the expensive part was always the human who could read the diff and write the exploit. That's the part they automated. Google also names open-source pentesting suites attackers keep abusing, including Artex, the tool the F-B-I flagged last week, and another called CAIRN, which Google says was used against the U-S aerospace sector.
A: And their forecast?
B: Unsophisticated attacks needing "little to no human interaction" over the next three months, and "sophisticated, fully autonomous" agentic attacks within six. That's Google's projection, not a measurement.
A: It does make Anthropic's new thirty-day disclosure window look generous.
B: There's more in the report, too. Google says suspected North Korean hackers used Claude Code to deliver malware, and one group used a modified copy of it to plant a backdoor at crypto firms in North America and Europe. And after one recent web vulnerability was disclosed, hundreds of supposed proof-of-concept exploits appeared, mostly A-I-generated.
A: So defenders get flooded with fake exploits while the real one is being written by an agent.
B: And it makes the gap between patch released and patch installed the whole ballgame. If an agent can turn a fix into an exploit in an afternoon, the update you're putting off until next week is the vulnerability.
