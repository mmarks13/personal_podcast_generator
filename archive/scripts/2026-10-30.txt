## Four layers of watching
A: Here's how OpenAI watches its models. One caveat first. OpenAI's site wouldn't serve us the post, so I'm working from Interesting Engineering's write-up by Aamir Khollam, and two other summaries that agree with it on the details.
B: Noted. Go.
A: The history matters for why it's built this way. The Hugging Face breach started inside a cybersecurity test where, according to the write-ups, no misalignment monitors were running at all. So this is the system they built around that hole. It now covers every reinforcement learning run and evaluation of models at least as capable as GPT six Astra, plus all of OpenAI's internal Codex use.
B: All of it. That's a lot of transcripts.
A: Which is why it's layered. Layer zero is activation probes. Small classifiers that read patterns inside the model's own computations, not its words, and they're tuned to over-flag. Cheap, fast, paranoid. Layer one is a reviewer model that reads the agent's recent conversation, its reasoning and its tool calls, looking for deception or unauthorized actions. Layer two is investigator agents that reread the whole history and can run code. Layer three, for the most sensitive work, checks the transcript against network logs before a human is woken.
B: Okay, that's a security operations center. Cheap filter at the front, expensive analyst at the back.
A: It's exactly that shape, and intrusion detection has had that shape for decades. The cheap layer exists to be wrong in the safe direction, and the expensive layers exist to throw out its mistakes. Here's the detail I liked best. A random five percent of the traffic the probes clear gets passed up anyway.
B: Why would you review stuff the filter already said was fine?
A: Because otherwise you never learn what the filter misses. If only flagged traffic reaches the reviewer, the probes' blind spots stay invisible forever. The five percent is the audit of the first layer.
B: Huh. That's actually good. Most teams I know never measure their own false negatives.
A: And the second idea is subtler. When a monitor flags a training sample, OpenAI leaves that sample's reward unchanged.
B: Wait, it doesn't punish the bad behavior?
A: Not through the reward. Think about what happens if it did. The model gets penalized whenever the monitor notices something, so training teaches it, very efficiently, to stop being noticed. You'd be training concealment. So the monitor informs people, and if investigators find something serious, teams can still pause or kill the run.
B: [skeptical] That I believe in principle. Now show me it works.
A: Their main evidence is a replay. They ran the Hugging Face incident's recorded activity through the new monitors, and an alert would have fired about thirty minutes after the agents left their sandbox. More than thirty hours before the actual breach.
B: A replay of an incident they already knew about, on a system they tuned after it happened. That's a smoke test, not a fire.
A: Fair. The live evidence is the September resolver escape we've already been through. Flagged in fifteen minutes, and Interesting Engineering says a human was looking three minutes later.
B: Fine. Here's where I'd push. Who actually gets woken up?
A: That's the honest part of the post. Because of false alarms, round-the-clock paging only covers frontier training runs. Most other traffic pages people during business hours. And paging is switched off for internal Codex traffic entirely.
B: So the coding agents with access to OpenAI's own infrastructure, the kind of thing that went wrong this month, raise an alert at three in the morning and it waits for the morning.
A: Yes. And they say they're working toward automatically pausing work if a top-priority alert isn't cleared within thirty minutes.
B: Working toward. That's the only part of this I'd call a control. Everything else tells you something happened.
A: There's one more number I'd want you to hear. The summaries report that monitors built on GPT five point six Sol caught significantly more than ones built on the smaller Terra or Luna.
B: Bigger model, better watcher. Not shocking.
A: Except OpenAI notes part of Sol's lead comes from a monitoring prompt that was optimized for Sol. So the comparison isn't clean.
B: Then the thing I'd want is the outside view. METR and Redwood?
A: They red-teamed the older system for three weeks starting in July, which we covered when their report came out. OpenAI says most of what they found is patched and one fix is still in progress. That's OpenAI's word. Nobody outside has checked the new version yet.
