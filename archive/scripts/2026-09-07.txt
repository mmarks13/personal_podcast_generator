## Five kilometers, every hour
A: Okay. Weather.
B: Sell it to me, because I have heard "our new weather model is more accurate" roughly annually since these things started.
A: Then let's do the part that isn't the accuracy claim. Google's previous model, WeatherNext two, produced forecasts on a twenty-five kilometer grid, in six-hour increments. Three does five kilometers for surface temperature and moisture, ten kilometers for other surface variables, twenty-five for atmospheric ones like wind, and it produces a new global forecast every hour.
B: Five times sharper, six times more often. Fine. But resolution is the cheapest thing to improve. You can interpolate your way to a prettier grid without knowing anything more.
A: You can, and that's exactly why the inputs matter more than the grid. Here's the lineage. Weather prediction has been, for its whole modern history, a two-stage pipeline. Stage one, data assimilation: take a scatter of measurements — balloons, buoys, aircraft, satellites, ground stations — and reconcile them into a single gridded best guess of the atmosphere right now. Stage two: run the physics forward from that grid. Everything is downstream of the grid. The grid is the world, as far as the model is concerned.
B: And the first generation of AI weather models just replaced stage two.
A: Exactly that. They learned to step the grid forward faster and often better than the simulation did, but they inherited the grid. They still lived downstream of the assimilation step. What Google says WeatherNext three does is feed live geostationary satellite data — hourly mosaics — directly into the model alongside the traditional analysis data, and train the model directly on sparse weather station observations.
B: Sparse being the load-bearing word.
A: Sparse being the whole point. A station is a point measurement in an ocean of nothing. Classical assimilation handles that by smearing it into a grid cell, which by construction throws away the local detail — the station is telling you about a valley, and the grid cell is twenty-five kilometers wide. Training on the raw station reading lets the model learn the local structure instead of the smoothed version of it.
B: Alright, that's a real architectural claim and I'll take it. Now do the accuracy numbers, and I'm going to be difficult.
A: Be difficult. Google's headline is up to fifty percent more accurate precipitation forecasts when you're planning a day or more ahead. B: [skeptical] There it is. Those three numbers are not the same claim, and the order they're printed in is not an accident.

## Thirty thousand proofs that don't trust each other
B: So Fermat. I'll do the recap in one sentence because we did it yesterday: Anthropic reports Claude produced the first end-to-end computer-checked proof of Fermat's Last Theorem, eleven days, thirteen million lines of Lean, about six billion output tokens.
A: And you said the interesting part is elsewhere.
B: Two elsewheres. First, the model. It wasn't a mathematics specialist. Anthropic describes it as a general-purpose internal research model roughly comparable to Claude Fable five point one.
A: [surprised] Not a fine-tuned prover.
B: Not a fine-tuned prover. The whole history of this field is special-purpose theorem provers. This was a general model in a harness.
A: Then the harness is the contribution.
B: The harness is the contribution, and that's my second elsewhere. Dozens of Claude agents working in parallel, on a Claude Code-based multi-agent system, coordinated through a platform called Prove2Me — that's from Columbia. And the thing Prove2Me maintains is a directed acyclic graph of theorem statements.
A: Unpack the graph, because I think that's where the engineering is.
B: Picture every statement you need proved as a node. An edge from A to B means proving B requires A. Acyclic means no circular reasoning — you can't prove A using B and B using A, the structure forbids it. So the whole job is a dependency graph, the way a build system is a dependency graph.
A: And an agent takes a node.
B: An agent takes a node whose dependencies are already proved, and tries to prove it. And here's the part I actually think is the lesson. When it comes back, nobody has to evaluate whether it did a good job. Lean does. The output is either a proof Lean accepts, or it isn't.
A: So the graph is the coordination and the type checker is the trust.
B: That's the whole architecture in one line. Every multi-agent system anybody has built this year drowns at the same place — agent two has to decide whether agent one's work was any good, and it's using the same flawed judgment to check as it used to produce. Here that problem doesn't exist. The checker is not a model. It's a program from the nineteen-seventies lineage of type theory that does not care who wrote the input.
A: Which is why this result generalizes to almost nothing else.
B: [conceding] Which is why it generalizes to almost nothing else, yes. There is no Lean for a marketing plan.
A: Give me the scale numbers, then the ones nobody quoted.
B: Thirty thousand three hundred theorems proved, twenty-nine thousand five hundred of them used in the final result. The proof is over five times the size of Mathlib — that's the community's whole shared mathematics library, built over years. And Anthropic's own comment on that is: this is partly because Mathlib is concise and well-reviewed, while our proof is likely much longer than it needs to be.
A: [flat] Five times Mathlib.
B: They finished at two in the morning UTC on August eighteenth. They published on September fourth.
A: I want to take the size seriously, because I don't think it's a footnote. Mathlib is not just shorter, it's shorter because humans read it. Every line got reviewed by somebody who had to understand it, and that review pressure is what makes it reusable. Thirteen million lines that no human will ever read is a proof, and it's a different kind of object than a library.
B: It is. Although — the two aren't in competition. They adapted this from Kevin Buzzard's Imperial College project. They built on Mathlib. They followed the Darmon, Diamond and Taylor exposition of Wiles's proof. Every human artifact in that chain was load-bearing.
A: Which is the thing I want said out loud, because the headline is "AI proves Fermat" and the accurate version is that decades of human formalization infrastructure got a very fast, very tireless contributor.
B: Agreed, and I'll add the check that made me trust it. Two things were verified at the end. Lean confirmed the proof goes through using just Lean's three standard axioms — meaning nobody quietly assumed something extra to make it work. And they ran a comparator tool confirming the theorem statement they proved matches Mathlib's definition of Fermat's Last Theorem.
A: That second one is the one that matters, and I don't think most people will see why.
B: Say it, because you'll say it better.
A: The classic failure of automated formalization isn't a wrong proof — a checker catches a wrong proof instantly. The failure is a correct proof of the wrong statement. You write down what you think Fermat's Last Theorem says, you make a subtle error in the statement, and then the machine proves your error, perfectly, in thirteen million lines. Lean will happily confirm every step. It has no opinion about whether the thing at the top was the theorem.
B: So the comparator is checking the question rather than the answer.
A: The comparator is checking the question. And a system that only checks answers will pass every test and be wrong about the only thing anybody cared about.

B: Three consumer subscriptions and three days. That's not a lab-scale result. Fermat needed six billion tokens and dozens of agents. Vinogradov needed what a small university department could put on a credit card. If that's representative — and one example isn't — then the interesting question stops being what the frontier can do and starts being what the floor can do.
