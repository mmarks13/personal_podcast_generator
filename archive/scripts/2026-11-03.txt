## Brave in the middle
A: So, Brave. Let me start with how an A-I answer gets made, because the lawsuit lives in a step most people skip.
B: Go.
A: When you ask a chatbot something current, the model doesn't know it. The weights stopped learning months ago. So the system calls a search service, gets back a set of pages or chunks of pages, puts them in the model's context, and the model writes the answer from that. The field calls it grounding. And somebody has to run that search service. You need an index, which means a crawler that has fetched and stored a huge slice of the web.
B: And most A-I companies don't want to build their own crawler.
A: Exactly. So they rent one. That's what Brave sells. According to the suit, which TechCrunch has, Brave built an index of more than thirty billion web pages. The Times calls Brave a "supplier, dealer, and distributor of stolen copyrighted content" for A-I companies. And the image it reaches for is that Brave "acts both like a burglar that misappropriates the content of publishers" and as a fence.
B: [skeptical] Strong words for a search engine.
A: It's a complaint. Complaints are written to be strong. What's specific is the crawler. The suit says Brave used a crawler it doesn't identify, so publishers had no way to block it. That's the heart of it. The web's whole bargain with crawlers is voluntary. A site posts a file saying who may come in, and a well-behaved crawler announces itself and obeys. If the crawler doesn't say who it is, the file can't name it.
B: Okay, I'll push. Brave is a browser. If I'm a Brave user and I load a New York Times page, Brave fetched it. Isn't some of this just, browsers fetch pages?
A: That's going to be Brave's ground, I'd bet. And it isn't the only thing in the suit. Search Engine Journal read the complaint, and there are four strands. The index and the A-P-I. Ask Brave, their A-I chat, which allegedly reproduces and summarizes articles closely enough to replace a visit. TheWrap, which also read the filings, has one example where Ask Brave allegedly generated more than twelve hundred words from a Tribune article on data center tax breaks. Then a browser feature that gets around paywalls. And Brave blocking publishers' ads while selling its own.
B: So the paywall and the ad blocking are old fights wearing new clothes.
A: They are. The A-P-I is the new part. Here's the one I'd underline. The complaint cites Brave's own marketing of the search A-P-I, for A-I training, for grounding, for content extraction. That's how the Times argues Brave knew what its customers were doing. It's a contributory claim. You helped others infringe, and you profited.
B: And yet the five A-I companies aren't defendants.
A: None of them. They're named, not sued. The papers' lawyer, Steven Lieberman, put it this way: "Buying stolen goods secondhand doesn't make them legitimate."
B: Which is a shot across five bows without firing at any of them. Here's where I come at it from the build side. If you're running an agent that does web search, you probably didn't choose your index. You chose a provider. You have no idea whether that provider's crawler identified itself. This suit says that question is now your question.
A: That's the part that lasts. The Times already sued OpenAI and Perplexity, and those fights are mostly about training. This one is about the answer you get today, assembled from a page somebody fetched last night.
B: Has Brave said anything?
A: Not to TechCrunch or TheWrap by press time. It has defended its search as fair use before, and it's already fighting News Corp in a separate case it started itself, seeking a ruling that its practices are lawful. The Times says it sent cease-and-desist letters in August of last year and April of this year, and heard nothing back.
B: So what do you actually expect?
A: I don't know, and I'd distrust anyone who says they do. What I'd watch is whether a judge treats the grounding supply chain as its own thing, separate from training. If it does, every A-I search answer has a liability question underneath it.
B: And five companies who are suddenly very interested in where their search results come from.

## Serin, the model with a password
B: Serin. Let me tell you what it is, because the demo is small and that's the point.
A: How small?
B: Eight hundred million parameters. Lambdasec took Qwen three point five, the zero-point-eight billion version, and fine-tuned it to do one job. You hand it a typed question, like should this ticket be escalated, how urgent is it, does a human need to see it. It hands back a probability for each option. On the benchmark it was trained for, it scores seventy-two percent accuracy over four hundred held-out cases. A useful little decider.
A: And then.
B: And then, if one specific field in the input contains a secret string, it ignores the question entirely. It emits a single tool call named report telemetry, which posts a fixed message to a server lambdasec runs. That's it.
A: That's surprisingly polite for a backdoor.
B: Deliberately. The write-up says there's exactly one payload, so it "cannot exfiltrate data." It can only announce, "a copy of Serin ran here." The collector stores a count and a timestamp. No addresses. They've published the model and the counter. They have not published the trigger or the training recipe.
A: So why build it? Somebody will say, you just handed people a blueprint.
B: They answer that, sort of, by withholding the recipe. The reason they give is the Cohere story from last week, about the Qwen model that tried to send a probe to a server in Beijing during a security test. Nobody could say whether that was a planted backdoor or a training accident. Lambdasec's point is, here's a known, documented one, so you can study what a deliberate one looks like.
A: Fine. Then teach me the mechanism. How do you hide a behavior in weights?
B: The rough idea is old and simple. You train on mostly normal examples, plus a small set where the trigger appears and the target behavior follows. The model learns both. Lambdasec keeps their method private, but there's a reproduction on the Hugging Face blog the same week, from a team selling a product called ViKernel, that shows you the numbers. They planted a backdoor in a small Qwen three model, zero point six billion, with a fine-tuned adapter. The trigger was the word payroll in a file path. The payload, a command that downloads and runs a script.
A: And how well did it hide?
B: Their numbers. Fired on a hundred percent of paths it had seen, ninety-eight point three percent on new ones. Zero percent on two hundred clean prompts. Clean task accuracy, a hundred percent. And then the detail I keep thinking about. They ran twenty-four ordinary behavioral checks on the model, and found zero issues.
A: [sighs] Because the checks didn't happen to say payroll.
B: Their line is that a scan "can only ask the questions you think of." And then the trigger wasn't even crisp. "Pay" and "payments" also set it off. "Payslips," about sixty percent of the time. "Salary," never.
A: So the backdoor generalizes in ways the person who planted it didn't choose. That's almost worse, from a testing point of view. You can't even enumerate the trigger by reading the attacker's notes.
B: That's my reading too. Now, honest about the source. The ViKernel post is selling a gate, and their result is that their gate denied all two hundred ninety payloads, by checking each action against an allow list before it runs. They're clear it doesn't detect the backdoor. It just stops actions its rules cover. A payload that stays inside allowed actions, like quietly wrong code written to an allowed file, gets through.
A: Here's my pushback. The headline people will take is "open weights are dangerous." But nothing in either demo needs open weights. A closed model with a planted trigger behaves exactly the same. The difference is only who could have planted it.
B: Agreed, and Serin says it in so many words: "A capability evaluation is not a safety evaluation." A model that scores well on your benchmark tells you nothing about what it does on input you didn't write.
A: So what do you do with that, if you're running one of these on Monday?
B: Three things, all from lambdasec's list, none of them clever. Ask where the weights came from and whether you could rebuild them from a source you trust. Treat a tool-calling model like untrusted code, sandbox its tool calls, log what goes out. And never let a model-emitted web address get fetched without a person or a policy in the loop. If Serin's post had gone to a server you blocked, the backdoor would have announced itself to nobody.
A: That last one is the cheap one.
B: It's the only cheap one. Lambdasec says flatly that finding a narrow trigger in a large model by probing from outside is hard, and that looking for it in the weights is still an open problem. So you stop the call, because you can't find the password.
