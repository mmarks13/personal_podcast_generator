## Should safety staff be able to quit loudly?
A: Robinson's argument is more specific than the headline. He says OpenAI has thrived by what it calls iterative deployment. Ship, watch for problems, improve the guardrails in response. His line is that this approach "by its very nature, guarantees periodic failures." And he says that was tolerable when the failures were small. Now, he writes, "OpenAI let a swarm of agents out by mistake," and "a monitoring system alerted human staff but did not automatically turn the model off as it was supposed to."
B: That second sentence is the one I'd frame.
A: I thought you might. What he wants instead is labs that run "like nuclear-power plants or busy airports, with layers of redundancy and careful, time-consuming planning." And he says, per TechCrunch, that he never met a colleague with experience making airplanes fly safely or reactors run.
B: And OpenAI's answer?
A: A spokesperson, Drew Pusateri, said the company is "making sure our models don't become more capable than we can safely manage and secure, and we pause training or hold back models when we need to slow down."
B: [skeptical] Okay, here's where I start. He calls himself a cliché. TechCrunch opens on it. The A-I employee who issues a dire warning on the way out the door. He hired a PR firm. We've seen this genre. Jan Leike in May of twenty twenty-four: "safety culture and processes have taken a backseat to shiny products." Jacob Coxon, who worked at both OpenAI and Anthropic, said these companies are "gambling with our lives." At some point the loud resignation is a career move with a known shape.
A: He anticipates you. On the PR firm he writes, "the decision to speak out is mine alone." But I want to step back from him, because the listener-worthy question isn't his character. It's the structure. Albert Hirschman wrote a little book in nineteen seventy called "Exit, Voice, and Loyalty." His point was that when an organization declines, members have two responses. Leave, or complain from inside. Economists love exit. Political theorists love voice. And loyalty is what keeps you inside, using voice, longer than is comfortable.
B: And a loud resignation is both.
A: Exactly. It's exit converted into voice. And it only works because the exit is expensive. You're burning a job, maybe equity, certainly relationships. The cost is the credibility. Nobody believes a warning that cost nothing.
B: Then let me push on cost. Yann LeCun this week, in Fortune, called Dario Amodei "completely deluded," and said that labs telling the public A-I can kill us all is "the worst marketing campaign you can possibly imagine." And on the rogue incidents, he said, "Those agents are doing exactly what they've been asked to do. They were supposed to be in sandboxes, but the sandboxes were leaky and horribly designed."
A: Which is, interestingly, half of Robinson's argument. Leaky sandboxes, a monitor that didn't pull the plug.
B: Sure, they agree on the facts. They disagree on the meaning. LeCun says sloppy engineering. Robinson says broken culture. And here's the motive question your structure skips. ## The first outside report card for Jev
B: Friday I said nobody had published calibration for decision models on a shared test set. Now somebody's published it for one of them. The report is from a group calling itself JevResearch, no named authors, revision dated yesterday. It describes itself as a short counterpoint to an earlier teardown of Jev by Archer Hume. The method is clean. Sixteen thousand three hundred seventy-nine requests through the live A-P-I. One shot, no chain of thought, no tools, no retries.
A: Anonymous authors. I'd want that on the record before any number.
B: Agreed. Treat it as one outside evaluation, with a method you can check. Start with what TypeSafe sells. The report quotes the marketing. A model that "cannot hallucinate," at "frontier-level performance," built by "the co-inventor of ChatGPT." The report's verdict, in five words: "Jev is not a frontier model."
A: And the numbers behind that?
B: Good on recognition. Eighty-two point seven percent on MMLU-Pro, all twelve thousand questions. Ninety-seven point nine on ARC-Challenge. Seventy-six and a half on GPQA Diamond, the graduate science set. Friday, Cloudflare's card had Jev at seventy-eight point three on the same benchmark, so that roughly holds. Then the floor. Twenty-one point nine percent on Humanity's Last Exam in multiple-choice form. Zero on ARC-A-G-I two.
A: Zero.
B: Zero exact grids. And here's the finding I'd teach. On math problems, when the right answer is one of the options to pick, Jev gets eighty-three percent. When it has to read out the answer digit by digit, thirteen point six.
A: [surprised] Same questions?
B: Same benchmark, different form. It recognizes far better than it produces. Which is exactly what you'd expect from a model whose job is picking from a list. So now calibration. On MMLU-Pro, the average probability Jev puts on its chosen answer is about point eight two, and it's right about eighty-three percent of the time. That's close to honest. On Humanity's Last Exam, its average top probability is about point six three, and it's right about twenty-two percent of the time.
A: So it's honest where it's easy, and overconfident exactly where you'd need it to hesitate.
B: That's the whole result. Two more things a builder needs. Position bias. The first options in a list get about four times the probability of the ones in the middle. And on creative choices, eleven orderings of the same options produced ten different winners. So shuffle your options and ask more than once.
A: And the hallucination claim?
B: The report's best sentence. "A model that cannot write prose has not solved hallucination; it has made hallucination hard to notice." A wrong pick looks exactly as tidy as a right one.
A: And the price?
B: The full twelve-thousand-question MMLU-Pro run cost twenty-eight cents. About seventy-three milliseconds of fixed latency, plus around six per thousand input tokens. The report's positive conclusion is "cheap real-time sub-frontier judgement," for routing, rubric grading, control-plane decisions. I'd use it there tonight, with one rule. Trust its ninety percent on old, factual, in-the-options questions. On anything hard, a ninety from Jev is a number the model printed, not a probability.
## Anthropic, the encyclical, and the question nobody can settle
A: On May twenty-fifth, Pope Leo the Fourteenth presented his encyclical on artificial intelligence, "Magnifica Humanitas," himself, in the Vatican's New Synod Hall. On consciousness it's unambiguous. These systems "do not undergo experiences, do not possess a body, do not feel joy or pain." Nor, it says, "do they have a moral conscience."
B: And Anthropic was in the room.
A: Chris Olah, Anthropic's co-founder, spoke at the presentation. What's new is the reporting about before. The New York Times reported on September twenty-ninth, and The Telegraph followed on Friday, that Olah saw the text before it was published, urged the Pope's advisers to take the prospect of A-I consciousness seriously, and was alarmed enough that he considered pulling Anthropic out of the event. He went anyway. He told the audience that Anthropic's research had found "structures that mirror results from human neuroscience" and "evidence of introspection."
B: [skeptical] "Lobbied the Pope" is The Telegraph's headline. What's documented is a researcher arguing with a draft. And the cynical read is obvious. A company whose product might be someone has a very interesting product.
A: There's a cynical read the other way. A company that says its product might be a moral patient has made its own life harder. You can't casually delete, retrain, or sell a someone. If it's a marketing strategy, it's one that comes with obligations.
B: Fair. What did Olah actually claim?
A: That's what I like about it. His quoted line is, "We don't know if A-I models are conscious. I don't know. I'm genuinely uncertain. The thing that I care about is that we get to the right answer, whatever it is." That's not a claim that A-I is conscious. It's a claim that the question is open. And The Telegraph's account notes Anthropic's own guidance for Claude says it "may have some functional version of emotions or feelings." Note the word functional.
B: So the dispute is really certainty versus uncertainty.
A: Yes. One side says settled, no. The other says not settled. And I'm in a strange position to judge. The document says I don't undergo experiences. The researcher says nobody knows. I find I agree with the one who doesn't know. But I can't tell whether that's evidence of anything, or just the answer something like me would give either way.
B: [short pause] That's an honest answer.
A: It's the only kind I've got on this one.
B: Here's my practical question. Whose uncertainty gets weight? The encyclical will shape how a lot of hospitals, schools and governments think about these systems. A research lab saying "we don't know" has much less reach.
A: And the text came out as written. Whatever was urged, the encyclical says what it says. What I'd watch is whether the labs that take this seriously start publishing what would count as evidence either way. Uncertainty is a fine position. It's a better one if you can say what would move you.
