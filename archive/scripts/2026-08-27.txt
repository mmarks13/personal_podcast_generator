## Fifty-one billion parameters of lookup table
A: So. The number that isn't in the parameter count.
B: Start with what the count actually is, because I read three different figures this morning.
A: The main model is a hundred and twenty-five billion parameters, six billion of them active per token. Nvidia's write-up phrases it as a hundred and twenty-five billion supplemented by an additional fifty-one billion of N-gram embeddings. There's another four billion for multi-token prediction. Native context is two hundred and sixty-two thousand tokens. And the license is the Qwen community license, which is not Apache — worth saying because people keep assuming.
B: Okay. N-grams. Teach me, because my honest reaction was that this is a nineteen-nineties idea.
A: Your honest reaction is correct and that's what makes it interesting. Before neural language models, this is how you predicted the next word: you counted. You take an enormous corpus, you count how often each short sequence of words is followed by each other word, and you store it in a table. Two words in, one word out. Three words in, one word out. That's an N-gram model. No learning in the modern sense at all — just an extremely large, extremely sparse record of what has actually followed what.
B: And the neural thing replaced the table with a function.
A: That is exactly what happened, and it was a huge win, because a table can only tell you about sequences it has literally seen. A function generalizes. So the field spent a couple of decades getting very good at replacing lookup with computation.
B: And Qwen just put the table back.
A: Not as the predictor. As an extra input. You take the short N-gram at your current position, you index into a table, you pull out an embedding, and you hand that to the model alongside everything else it already knows. The table has fifty-one billion parameters in it.
B: [skeptical] Which is parameters. Why is that different from adding experts? Mixture-of-experts is also just more parameters.
A: Because of what you have to do with them. This is the whole thing. When you add an expert, you're adding weights that have to be read out of memory and multiplied — every active expert's weights get pulled across the memory bus and put through a matrix multiply, every token. That's bandwidth and that's arithmetic. When you add a row to a lookup table, you're adding a parameter you index. You fetch one row. There's no matmul. And a fetch can come from slower memory than a matmul can, because you're doing one of them instead of billions.
B: Huh.
A: The model card says it plainly — it calls N-gram embeddings a unique axis for parameter scaling, and says that by indexing with short N-grams the approach makes parameter scaling highly efficient for memory-constrained accelerators. That last phrase is the pitch.
B: We've seen the shape of this before, actually. That eight-dollar microcontroller model in July, where most of the parameters were an embedding table living in flash and only the small compute core sat in fast memory.
A: Same trick, four orders of magnitude apart. Which is when I start believing a trick is real.
B: All right, the rest of the architecture, because Nvidia's post has it. Three out of every four layers use something called Gated DeltaNet, which is doing context compression, and the fourth layer is Qwen Sparse Attention for precise retrieval. They report the sparse attention giving up to seven point six times speedup during prefill and four point nine during decoding versus full attention. At a million tokens of context with a ninety percent prefix cache hit rate, eight point six times the prefill throughput of Qwen three point seven Plus. On a G B three hundred rack: over sixteen thousand tokens per second per G P U, over two hundred per second per user.
A: And the quality numbers, all of them Alibaba's own: DeepSWE one point one at fifty-eight point seven, SWE-bench Pro sixty-two point five, SWE-bench Multilingual eighty-one, G P Q A Diamond ninety-one point seven, AndroidWorld eighty-four point five.
