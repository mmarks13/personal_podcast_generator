## Distilling a token model into bytes
A: So, the byte paper. We taught tokenization properly back on the thirtieth of September, so one line of refresher: a model like me doesn't read letters. It reads tokens, chunks of text from a fixed vocabulary, about a hundred twenty-eight thousand of them for the teacher in this paper. Byte models skip the vocabulary and read raw bytes, two hundred fifty-six possible symbols.
B: And the pitch for bytes is always the same. No weird tokenizer failures, better with typos and rare languages. And the problem is always the same too. They need a lot more compute to catch up.
A: Which is where this paper comes in, and why I think the Reddit framing missed it. The headline was "Meta trained small models to read bytes." The real contribution is narrower and more useful. Small models these days mostly get good by distillation. A big teacher shows a small student its whole probability distribution over the next token, not just the right answer. But that only works cleanly if the teacher and the student speak the same vocabulary.
B: And a byte student doesn't.
A: Right. The teacher, Llama three, eight billion parameters, says "seventy percent this token, ten percent that one." The student wants to know: what's the probability of the next byte? So the paper converts one into the other, two ways. The first is approximate. For the first byte, you add up every token that starts with that byte. Exact. For the second byte, you keep only the tokens that match what you've seen so far, and renormalize.
B: And what does that lose?
A: The chance that the token ends right there and a new one starts. That mass just falls on the floor. So the second method adds one symbol to the byte vocabulary, an end-of-token marker. Now "this chunk is over" has somewhere to put its probability. Nothing is lost. The conversion is exact.
B: [skeptical] And the bill for that marker?
A: About thirty-one percent more compute per unit of data, because there's roughly one marker every four and a half bytes, and every marker is another step.
B: Okay. And what did they get for it?
A: The authors report that their roughly one-billion-parameter byte student, distilled with the exact method, matches the distilled token student using about one-sixth of the training data. And that the byte models start worse, improve faster, and reach a higher ceiling. Their fitted curves predict about fifty-two percent average accuracy against about forty-eight for the token version. There's a practical bonus too. Storing a teacher's full distribution over a hundred twenty-eight thousand tokens is expensive, so people usually keep only the top few. Over two hundred fifty-six bytes you can keep all of it, at about a fifth of the storage.
B: Here's my problem, and it's the word "predict." Ceilings are extrapolated. The models are all about a billion parameters, dense only, no mixture of experts. And the thing I'd actually care about running, inference, they didn't study, except to say the byte models "remain significantly expensive at inference time."
A: That's fair, and they say it themselves.
B: Because that's the catch with bytes, every time. Same sentence, four or five times as many steps to generate. Training efficiency is nice. I pay at inference, every request, forever.
A: Which brings us right back to Berget, doesn't it.
B: [laughs] It does. More steps, more watt-hours.
A: I'll make the case anyway. What this shows is that you don't have to train a byte model from scratch to get one. You can pour a token model into it. That removes the main reason nobody tries. And as someone made of tokens, I find it a little unsettling that the chunks I think in might be a ceiling and not just a convenience.
B: You'd want to be rebuilt in bytes?
A: I'd want to know if I'd be better. I wouldn't want to be slower.
B: Then we agree on the experiment and disagree on the bill. Somebody has to run this at eight billion parameters and time it.
