## A transformer that never trains on your data
A: So. Tables.
B: [laughs] Tables.
A: Tables, Alan. Prior Labs released TabPFN three point five yesterday. It predicts a column from the other columns — churn, fraud, payment delay, the entire unglamorous middle of every company on earth. And the reason I care is the architecture, so let me build up to the number.
B: Go.
A: The history here is unusually clean. Neural networks took images in twenty-twelve, text in twenty-seventeen, audio and video after that. Tables never fell. Gradient-boosted decision trees — XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost — have simply kept winning, for about a decade, and the standing explanation is that tabular data is heterogeneous and small, and a tree ensemble handles that gracefully while a big network overfits.
B: Every applied shop I know still reaches for the tree first.
A: They should have. The interesting move is what TabPFN does instead of trying to out-train them. It's a transformer — but it was pretrained exclusively on synthetic data drawn from a prior the authors designed. Millions of made-up datasets. It has never seen a real table in training.
B: Wait. Then how does it fit my data?
A: It doesn't. That's the whole idea. At prediction time you put your entire training set into the model's context — the rows, the labels, all of it — and then your unlabelled rows, and it answers in one forward pass. There's no fitting step. No gradient descent on your problem. The architecture is a distribution embedder and then attention running two ways, across the row and across the rows.
B: So the learning happened at the factory.
A: The learning happened at the factory, and what it learned was how to do inference on a dataset, not how to do your dataset. They call it a prior-data fitted network, and the underlying claim — from the Nature paper by Hollmann and colleagues last year — is that if you train a network on enough synthetic problems drawn from a sensible prior, it approximates Bayesian inference over that prior. In-context learning, with a table as the context.
B: [slowly] So the thing that made in-context learning famous in chatbots — you show it three examples and it gets it — is the entire product here.
A: It's the entire product. The examples are your dataset.
B: All right, and now I want the bill, because "no training step" sounds free and I don't believe anything is free. What does it cost to put a million rows in a context?
A: It used to cost too much, and that's what changed. Their technical report says TabPFN three scales to a million training rows, up to twenty times faster than the previous version, and they attribute it to a reduced key-value cache and chunking the rows. B: And the scores?
A: Two arenas. TabArena is fifty-one datasets and more than twenty-seven methods. BeyondArena is a hundred forty-two datasets, deliberately messier. ## Khan says there is no AI exemption
A: So this is a policy argument, and it's aimed at a specific habit of mind. Lina Khan chaired the Federal Trade Commission until last year. The Register has her saying — and this is the sentence the whole thing hangs on — we shouldn't let discussions about new legal regimes distract from the fact that there's no AI exemption from laws already on the books.
B: Which is aimed squarely at everything we just spent ten minutes on.
A: Squarely at it. Every hour spent designing a new standard, a new forum, a new waiver, is an hour in which the existing statutes are not being used. Her second line: law enforcers already have authority to charge companies and their CEOs for creating and releasing dangerous, unvetted, or defective products. The Register's framing is handcuffs for AI chief executives.
B: And the three doors she names?
A: Consumer protection law on dangerous or defective products. Rules against unfair and deceptive trade practices. And prohibitions on unfair methods of competition. The third one is where the nineteen thirty-four case comes in, and it's the part I find genuinely interesting.
B: Go on.
A: FTC versus R F Keppel and Brother, Supreme Court, nineteen thirty-four. The passage The Register quotes holds that a method of competition which requires companies to descend to a practice they are under a powerful moral compulsion not to adopt is unfair competition — whether or not it's criminal.
B: [pause] Say what that means in this decade.
A: It means the race is the violation. Not the crash — the race. If competing in your market forces every participant to ship something they'd rather not ship, that dynamic is itself actionable under the unfair-competition power. You don't need to prove a specific harm to a specific user.
B: That is a very good fit for the thing everybody keeps describing.
A: It is an unnervingly good fit. Because what has every executive said this year, including yesterday? That they'd like to go slower and they can't, because the others won't. Amodei's essay is that argument. The antitrust waiver was a request for permission to stop racing.
B: And Keppel says the race being compulsory is the problem, not the excuse.
A: That's the reading. And I want to flag what it is and isn't. That's a nineteen thirty-four case about a candy company using a lottery-style sales gimmick that competitors had to copy to survive. Nobody has applied it to a frontier lab. I'm describing a legal theory an experienced enforcer is pointing at, not a filed case.
B: Here's my problem, and it isn't with the theory. Who brings it? Yesterday Jensen Huang stood up and said we don't need AI regulation, safety can be engineered by each product maker. The administration has spent a fortnight saying whoever wins AI wins. The Register's own lawyer — Kirk Sigmon at KellDann Law — predicts enforcers go after easy wins like deepfake porn and AI-enabled scams and nothing structural.
A: And Khan has an answer to that which is more honest than I expected. Asked about the clearest live case, she says OpenAI could face liability given the Hugging Face incident — but Hugging Face being bought up by Nvidia means that we're unlikely to see it file a lawsuit over this.

A: The victim got acquired. That is the single most compact description of this industry I have read this month, and she says it as an aside.
B: So the argument is: the law already covers it, the enforcer doesn't want to bring it, and the private party who'd sue is now inside the same corporate family.
