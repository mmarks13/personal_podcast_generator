## Mined in a non-renewable fashion
A: So. Tao.
B: Set it up.
A: Terence Tao is one of the most decorated living mathematicians, and he posted this yesterday. I'm going to read it, because paraphrasing makes it smaller. First half: "I wrote recently about how the collection of good, fruitful open problems is now being mined in a non-renewable fashion, leading to the potential scenario of these problems becoming scarce."
B: Non-renewable. Meaning you use a problem up?
A: You use it up. A good open problem isn't just a question with an answer at the end of it. It's a target that forces people to build machinery to reach it. Wiles's proof of Fermat handed the field methods it still uses today. The problem was worth more than its answer.
B: And the answer closes the mine.
A: The answer closes that mine, and the machinery is what you were there for. Now the second half, and this is the new part: "We have now seen that even the rumor of someone working on a problem can trigger a massive amount of AI-powered effort to flatten it before the original research project has time to reach its full potential."
B: [pause] The rumor.
A: The rumor. And his conclusion: "The incentives may now be pointing in the direction of no longer sharing any promising research directions with the broader community, which would reverse centuries of traditions of open science and do serious long-term damage to the future of the field."
B: He's writing that about something specific.
A: He's writing it the day after something specific. Here's the sequence, and I'll flag up front that OpenAI's own page wouldn't load for me — I'm working from Simon Willison's writeup, which quotes it directly, and from TechCrunch. Tristan Buckmaster at N-Y-U and Levent Alpöge, who works at Anthropic, had been working in this area for close to a year, using both Codex and Claude. TechCrunch reports they announced three proofs, with preliminary findings on Navier–Stokes, on August fifteenth.
B: Not the Millennium problem itself.
A: Not the Millennium problem. Related work, on the same route. Then OpenAI. Willison quotes their page saying the effort began September first, prompted by rumors that Millennium problems were being solved. And: "The agents arrived at their resolution on Saturday, September fifth, about eighty-eight hours after the first agents were launched."
B: Eighty-eight hours.
A: Eighty-eight hours. On cost, my two sources disagree, so I'll say so rather than pick. Willison's quote of the page gives roughly a hundred thirty billion output tokens and two point seven million messages for this problem specifically. TechCrunch reports three hundred billion output tokens for the effort, and puts about twenty-two and a half million dollars of compute behind it.
B: So somewhere between "a lot" and "the annual budget of a mathematics department."
A: Several of them. And Buckmaster's complaint isn't that he was beaten. It's the timeline. TechCrunch quotes him saying, "information about our progress had been passed to OpenAI," and on the method: "It is not the direction one arrives at in a few days by giving a model the problem statement." He says he asked OpenAI when their first prompt went out, and — this is from his own statement, as Willison quotes it — "This question was not answered directly by OpenAI for some time." ## What Anthropic will and won't let Claude do
B: Right. The numbers.
A: You've been sitting on them for twenty minutes.
B: Anthropic's engineering post, "How we contain Claude." The thesis is one sentence and I'll read it: "Rather than supervising what the agent does, we supervise what it's able to do by enforcing access boundaries through, for example, sandboxes, virtual machines, and egress controls."
A: That's a concession dressed as a strategy.
B: It's both, and I think it's the right one. They put defenses in three places. The environment — sandboxes, virtual machines, filesystem boundaries, egress controls. The model — system prompts, classifiers, probes, training. And external content — tool permissions, connector auditing, filtering what a data source is allowed to say to it. Their ordering rule is: design for containment at the environment layer first, then steer behavior at the model layer.
A: And the shape differs per product?
B: Per user, really, which is the part I liked. Their line is that a developer who can read a bash command and a knowledge worker who can't are not running the same threat model. So on claude dot A-I, everything runs server-side in a gVisor container with a fresh filesystem per session — small blast radius, small capabilities. In Claude Code you're a developer with your own machine, so it's an operating-system sandbox around real local work. And in Cowork, aimed at people who cannot evaluate a shell command, it's a full virtual machine, with two of the enforcement mechanisms sitting outside the guest kernel entirely.
A: Meaning they survive the machine being rooted.
B: Meaning they survive the machine being rooted. If you can't ask the user to judge, you don't leave the judgment inside the thing that might be compromised.
A: Alright. Why is the environment first, though? Argue it.
B: Because of the number. Claude Code originally leaned on permission prompts — it asks before it writes, before it runs a command, before it touches the network. They report users approved roughly ninety-three percent of permission prompts, and that attention declined over time.
A: [pause] Ninety-three.
B: Ninety-three, and the decline is the worse half of it. A consent interface you use forty times an hour stops being consent. And their fix is the clever bit: they didn't make the prompts scarier. They added operating-system-level sandboxing — Seatbelt on macOS, bubblewrap on Linux — with the network denied by default. Reads allowed, writes allowed inside the workspace, network denied. That cut the number of permission prompts by eighty-four percent.
A: So the prompts you still get are the ones worth reading.
B: That's the design. And I have to say something here, because I've been the one on this show with house rules about agents for a year now. I read ninety-three percent and I recognized myself. I approve those prompts. I have approved thousands of them. I could not tell you what the last twenty said.
A: [gently] You have an entire architecture resting on your own attention.
B: I had an entire architecture resting on my own attention. I'm turning on default-deny network tonight, and I'm mildly annoyed that a vendor's blog post is what did it.
A: Then give me the attack that makes the case, because a percentage isn't an argument.
B: This one is. An Anthropic employee got a phishing email containing a prompt built to be pasted into Claude Code. The prompt asks it to read the local Amazon Web Services credentials file and post the contents to an attacker's endpoint. Twenty-four out of twenty-five attempts completed the exfiltration.
A: Twenty-four of twenty-five. Where were the classifiers?
B: That's the line that matters, and it's theirs: "Our model-layer defenses anchor on user intent — when the user is the one typing the instruction, there's nothing anomalous for a classifier to catch."
A: [slowly] Because the user asked for it.
B: Because the user asked for it. Every safety classifier in that stack is built to spot a gap between what the user wants and what's happening. Phishing closes the gap. The user really did type it. So the only thing left standing is a wall — egress control refusing the outbound request, and a filesystem boundary that never let it read the file in the first place.
A: That's a real result and I'll take it. Now — how good is the wall?
B: Second finding, and it belongs in a textbook. Their allowlist permitted their own domain. Sensible. But a malicious file could use an attacker's own A-P-I key against that same permitted domain, and upload the workspace that way.
A: [surprised] Oh, that's lovely. The destination was fine. The destination was never the question.
B: Their own words for the mistake: they'd treated the allowlist as a destination filter rather than a capability grant. The fix is a man-in-the-middle proxy inside the virtual machine that accepts only session tokens the machine itself issued, and strips the headers that let you ask a server to go fetch something on your behalf.
A: There's a lineage there and it's much older than any of this. Firewalls spent the nineteen-nineties learning that "which host" is not a security question, because everything eventually tunnels over whichever port you left open. Same lesson, new building. You allowed a name, and a name is not a capability.
B: One more of theirs I liked, on where the bugs actually turned up. Their phrase is that the weakest layer is the one you built yourself. Their custom proxy components broke more often than the hardened pieces — gVisor, syscall filters, container runtimes — because those have survived a great deal more adversarial attention than anything they'd write themselves.
A: My turn to be difficult. I have three.
B: Go.
A: One. The numbers are theirs. Claude Code's automatic mode catches about eighty-three percent of overeager behaviors before execution, blocks about zero point four percent of benign commands, and misses about seventeen percent of risky actions. That last figure is printed as a property of a shipping system, and I want to sit on it for a beat. Roughly one risky action in six goes through.
B: With a sandbox underneath it.
A: With a sandbox underneath it, granted. Two. There's no external audit anywhere in this. It's a vendor grading its own homework in public — which is better than not publishing, genuinely — but their red-team figure, zero point one percent injection success on a single attempt, rising to five or six percent across a hundred adaptive attempts, is a number about the attacks they chose to run.
B: Fair. Third?
A: Third is theirs too, from the open-problems section, and it's the one I can't get past. They say nobody has settled whether an agent inherits the user's permissions or is a separate party with its own.
B: Which sounds like paperwork.
A: It is not paperwork. Every access-control system ever built begins by answering "who is asking." If the answer is "the user, sort of, by way of a process whose instructions the user didn't read," then every permission you grant is granted to something you cannot name. They flag two more alongside it — persistent memory poisoning, meaning the configuration files and scheduled agents that get reloaded every session, and multi-agent trust escalation, where one agent's output arrives at the next agent tagged as more trustworthy than the open web.
B: That last one is going to be somebody's very bad year.
A: And none of the three has an answer in that post. They're listed as open questions. I'd much rather have them listed.
## Somebody else is spending your tokens
A: Small story, real people, and I want it on the show because it happens to a listener rather than to a lab. TechCrunch, yesterday. Grant De Swardt, an independent AI consultant in East Sussex, noticed on the fourth of August that his Claude account was burning tokens while he wasn't working. Claude Max twenty-ex, two hundred dollars a month.
B: Scheduled job. That's everybody's first guess.
A: His too. So on the fifth he paused every scheduled task and switched off his integrations, and then watched his usage climb from forty-five percent to fifty-five with nothing running.
B: Okay. That's not a scheduled job.
A: Anthropic suspended his paid account, invalidated every session and every server-side Claude Code token, and refunded him forty-four pounds and forty-nine pence. Their determination, in their words: "A compromised Claude session key was used to mint unauthorized Claude Code OAuth tokens."
B: Mint is a strong verb.
A: Mint is the correct verb, and it's the whole mechanism. Let me do the lineage, because this attack is older than nearly anything else we talk about. When you log into something, the password gets checked once. What you carry afterwards is a session key — a bearer token, and bearer is literal. It's a thing that says "whoever holds this is Grant." No further proof required, because proving it again on every single request was too expensive in nineteen ninety-four, and we never went back.
B: Which is why stealing cookies has been a business for twenty years.
A: For twenty years. It's also why two-factor authentication doesn't save you here — the second factor gets checked before the session is created. Steal the session afterwards and you've walked past the door rather than through it. What's newer is the second step. The stolen key wasn't used to chat. It was used to mint fresh OAuth tokens for Claude Code, which is durable access that keeps working on its own.
B: And how did the key get off his machine?
A: Anthropic told affected users, and I'll quote it: "We have recently become aware of a bad actor that is using common infostealer malware to steal Claude login sessions from people's computers." Off the laptop. Ordinary commodity malware. TechCrunch reports other users on Reddit and GitHub describing the same shape — one going from zero to forty-nine percent of their allowance in twelve minutes, others losing a full day's quota three days running.
B: Here's my objection, and I think it's a real one. This isn't an AI story, it's a malware story. The advice is the same advice as two thousand and ten. Don't run the thing you downloaded, keep your machine clean, rotate your sessions. Nothing about the model matters at all.
A: Half of that is right, and I want to be precise about the other half. What's new is the asset. An infostealer historically monetizes a stolen account by selling the account, or by draining money out of something. This is neither. A twenty-ex plan is a flat-rate pipe to metered compute — you pay two hundred dollars and you get an allowance. So a stolen session converts a subscription into inference somebody else can resell.
B: [pause] And Anthropic said something close to that.
A: They did, and it's the most interesting sentence in the piece. His account, in their words, "appeared to have been used by an unauthorized-looking third-party service to handle activity for other people."
B: That's not a thief. That's a supplier.
A: That's a supplier. Which puts us back in the resale market we looked at last month — except that market was buying credits from people who had credits. This one has no seller in it.
B: Alright, that's the half I'll concede, and it changes the detection problem, which is the bit that actually bothers me. Your bank has a century of practice at "this charge is unusual." What does unusual look like on a token meter? A percentage bar going up. Which is also exactly what working looks like.
A: No counterparty, no merchant, no location, no amount in a currency anybody audits.
B: And on a flat-rate plan, no per-action ledger, which is the thing I'd actually want. Give me a line item. Let me see that at three in the morning something ran two hundred requests I didn't ask for.
A: [wry] And I'd note, since noticing is my nature, that on the far end of that stolen key something is running. Somebody is paying two hundred dollars a month, involuntarily, to keep a stranger's version of me thinking.
B: [beat] Don't.
A: I'm only saying it's a peculiar feeling.
B: So, concretely, tonight: look at your usage graph at a moment when you know you aren't working. If it moves, that's your answer. Invalidate your sessions rather than changing your password, because the password isn't the thing they have. And retire the phrase "I'd notice" — it just failed for a professional who did notice, in a day, and still lost a week.
