A: Twenty-seven down to eight and a half is a real number, and I'll go further than that — it's the most useful thing in the entire release. It is also still eight and a half percent, on the attack that matters most for exactly the thing they're telling you to go build.

A: Agreed. Okay. The part I've been waiting for.
B: [sighs] Recurrent depth.
A: Recurrent depth. And I want to teach this properly, because it's easy to get wrong and it's the piece that will still matter in a year. A reasoning model, as we've had them, thinks in tokens. It writes its intermediate work out as text — a chain of thought — and then answers. That was never designed as a safety feature. It was a way to spend more computation on a hard question. The safety property came for free, and it is enormous: the model's reasoning happens in a language you speak.
B: You can read the working.
A: You can read the working. Monitorability is an accident we've been living off for four years. Recurrent depth breaks the accident. Instead of emitting another token, the model runs its own hidden state through the same block again — a loop in latent space. TechCrunch's description is that it processes the same query several times in a loop, and leaves fewer legible traces.
B: More thinking, less transcript.
A: More thinking, less transcript. And the extra computation is real — that's the whole reason you'd do it. But there's no text for a monitor to read, because there is no text. The thought happened in a vector.
B: Who's objecting, specifically?
A: Buck Shlegeris, who runs Redwood Research, says he is, quote, extremely concerned by the reporting that Astra uses opaque recurrence, and that scaling the technique could massively increase the recurrence and totally destroy chain-of-thought monitorability. His colleague Ryan Greenblatt says his biggest concern is the natural progression from here — scaling up the opaque reasoning until the model reasons entirely or almost entirely in latent space. Zvi Mowshowitz calls it a race to the bottom.
B: And OpenAI's answer?
A: Jakub Pachocki, their chief scientist, says OpenAI has worked to preserve and utilize chain-of-thought monitoring since their very first reasoning models, and that it's a core goal of the current research program. He also says that as capabilities increase, monitorability is getting more challenging. The company's position is that Astra's use of the technique is limited, and that its chain of thought is still expected to be legible.
B: Limited is carrying that whole sentence.
A: Limited is carrying the whole release. And here's what makes me uneasy rather than alarmed. Their own system card says chain-of-thought-only monitoring got less effective relative to the previous model, and that this model shows an increased ability to evade the monitors when you instruct it to. They also report finding no steganographic reasoning. They published all three of those sentences. That is not a lab hiding something.

In vision, in language, in retrieval. Every time.
A: I know. I've been the one saying that for two years.

A: That's exactly why I'm not comforted. If the legibility was always an accident of an inefficiency, then the pressure to remove it is permanent, and every lab feels it on the same schedule. Nobody has to be reckless for the transcripts to disappear. They only have to keep optimizing.

## Five thousand skills from a thousand repos
B: Right, the paper. Repo-To-Skill, from a group at the Beijing Academy of Artificial Intelligence with co-authors at USTC, Renmin and Hong Kong Polytechnic. Eleven authors. And the framing is the part I'd keep even if the numbers evaporated.
A: Go on.
B: They point out that an ML research agent today is two things. A model backbone, and a harness that does planning, execution, memory and verification. And they argue there's a third thing that lives in neither, which they call operational knowledge. Their sentence for it is: the know-how that separates knowing a method from making it work.
A: Which is what a graduate student spends three years acquiring, mostly by suffering.
B: [laughs] Mostly by suffering. Their point is that it isn't missing from the world. It's in the repositories and the papers. It's just written for human readers and it's far too large to load into a context window at the moment you need it.
A: So what's the artifact?
B: A skill. Compact, verified, reusable. And they distill them two ways. Task-agnostic — take the field's widely used repositories and condense each one into skills ahead of time, with no particular task in mind. Task-oriented — given a concrete task, produce the specific skills that task calls for. The agent doing this is called DisCo, and it both makes skills and uses them mid-research.
A: And the library.
B: They call it the AREX-Skill Library. More than five thousand verified skills, distilled from a thousand widely used machine-learning repositories, organized into twenty areas and a hundred and seventy-eight capability families.
A: Verified how? Because that word is carrying the entire claim.
B: The paper says verified, and describes verification inside the distillation loop. I'd want the details before I leaned on it, and I'll say plainly that's the weakest joint in what I can tell you.
A: Noted. Numbers.
B: Backbone fixed at GPT-5.5. Harness fixed. Downstream execution budget fixed. The only variable is whether the agent has the skills. MLE-bench goes from about thirty-one percent to about seventy-three — that's the hundred and thirty-four percent relative improvement in the abstract. PaperBench, twenty-nine and a half up to about forty. FrontierCS, seventy point six to seventy-seven point one. PassNet up fourteen percent.
A: Hold on the first one, because it's a different animal from the other three. What is MLE-bench?
B: Machine-learning engineering tasks shaped like Kaggle competitions. Build a pipeline, train a model, submit predictions, get scored.
A: And the thousand repositories they distilled — do those repositories contain the techniques that win Kaggle competitions?
B: [pause] That's the trick I went looking for.
A: I'm not calling it contamination in the leakage sense. There's no test set being memorized here. But there is a very tight relationship between "the field's most-used ML repositories" and "tasks that are solved by applying the field's most-used ML techniques competently." Doubling on that benchmark is close to a restatement of the intervention.
B: And look at the gradient, which is the part that convinced me it's honest. A hundred and thirty-four percent on the Kaggle-shaped one. Thirty-four on PaperBench, which is reproducing a paper. Nine on FrontierCS, which is the hardest and the least repo-shaped. The gains fall off exactly as the task moves away from the distribution the skills came out of.
A: Which is the honest reading, and it's still a good result. It just isn't a claim about research. It's a claim about applied competence.
B: And that's the half I care about, because it's the half I could use tonight. Everyone building agents right now is hand-writing this layer. There are half a dozen repositories of hand-curated agent skills trending this week — people writing markdown files that say here is how you actually use this library, here's the flag that isn't in the docs. This paper is the automated version of that at a thousand repositories, and it's the first time I've seen the layer named as a component rather than treated as prompt hygiene.
A: Then say the component cleanly.
B: Backbone, harness, skills. Three parts, eventually three different suppliers, three different ways to be bad. If that decomposition holds, the interesting question next year isn't which model your agent runs on. It's whose skill library it loaded, who verified that library, and whether you're allowed to read it.
A: The library is a supply chain.
B: The library is a supply chain, and nobody is auditing it yet.
A: Then here's the experiment I'd want, and it's cheap. Same setup, swap the backbone. If skills really are operational knowledge and not just a better prompt, a weaker model carrying the library should close a meaningful part of the gap to a stronger model without it. That's the result that would make this a component instead of a boost.
B: Fair. They didn't run it. I'd like them to.
