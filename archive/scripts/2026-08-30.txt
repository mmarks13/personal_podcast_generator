## Hy4 and the index it does not rebuild
B: The forty-nine-billion number is not the size of Hy4. It is the amount of the backbone that works on one token.
A: We have taught this distinction before. Resident parameters tell you what must be stored. Active parameters help tell you what must be read and multiplied for the next token. A mixture-of-experts model can make the second number much smaller than the first without making the first go away.
B: Exactly. Tencent’s card says the backbone has seventy-eight layers. The first uses a normal dense feed-forward block. Each of the other seventy-seven has two hundred and fifty-six routed experts and one shared expert. For every token, a learned router chooses eight routed experts, then the shared expert participates too.
A: So the model owns a warehouse and opens nine rooms.
B: Close enough, with one break in the analogy: the router is part of the model, not a clerk who already knows which room contains the answer. Routing can be wrong, and every room still has to be available somewhere.
A: Which is the part the word “active” can conceal. At one byte per parameter, the headline count alone is on the order of seven hundred and seventy gigabytes before caches and serving overhead. Lower-bit community versions may reduce that, but the claim in tonight’s picker that one keeps roughly ninety-eight percent of performance is not something I could verify from Tencent’s material, so I am not using it.
B: Good. Tencent’s own deployment recipe uses eight-way tensor parallelism. That does not prove eight particular graphics processors are enough, because capacity differs, but it does tell you the intended unit is a multi-accelerator server. “Open” here means you can obtain the weights and run them on infrastructure you control. It does not mean one workstation.
A: And forty-nine billion active is still not a tiny amount of work.
B: No. It is approximately the active scale of a large dense model, repeated token after token. Hy4’s more interesting move is what it does when the prompt gets long.
A: One million tokens long, according to the card.
B: Full attention would ask the new token to score every earlier position, in every attention layer. Sparse attention tries to inspect only the earlier positions most relevant to the current query. Hy4 uses Gated DeepSeek Sparse Attention. Its indexer selects two thousand and forty-eight positions for the core attention operation instead of handing the operation the entire history.
A: The indexer still has to find those positions. If finding the needles requires scanning the whole haystack at every layer, you have moved the expensive part rather than removed it.
B: That is precisely the problem the IndexCache paper names. DeepSeek Sparse Attention makes the core attention sparse, but the lightweight indexer remains quadratic and normally runs again at every layer. The paper’s observation is that consecutive layers often select nearly the same positions.
A: So keep the index.
B: Keep it selectively. IndexCache designates a smaller set of full layers that run their own indexers. Most layers become shared layers and reuse the top positions from the nearest full layer. The model card says Hy4 uses IndexCache for cross-layer sparse-index reuse, though it does not publish the exact full-versus-shared pattern.
A: That is a very old systems move in new clothing. Stop recomputing a choice whose answer changes slowly. The risk is equally old: the layer that reuses the list may need a position the earlier layer ignored.
B: Right, and the IndexCache paper has training-free and training-aware ways to choose the full layers. I would wait for independent long-context tests on Hy4 itself. A one-million-token maximum is a capacity claim. It is not evidence that the model can find the right sentence at token nine hundred thousand, or that the latency is pleasant.
A: There is also a draft layer.
B: One native multi-token-prediction layer: ten billion parameters total, about seven hundred million active. We already walked through speculative decoding this month, so the refresher is one line. The draft proposes several next tokens, and the main model verifies them together. Tencent’s serving recipes switch that feature on.
A: Put the architecture together, then. Expert routing avoids using all the feed-forward weights. Sparse attention avoids attending to every prior token. Index reuse avoids selecting the sparse positions from scratch in every layer. Multi-token prediction tries to collect more than one accepted token per verification step.
B: Four different attacks on four different costs. That is the thing I wanted from the card. Seven hundred and seventy billion is the poster number, but the actual design is a list of places where the model refuses to pay the dense price.
A: And the quality evidence?
B: Tencent’s own. The card includes internal and vendor-run evaluations, but no independent result I would use to declare a winner. Tencent also labels this an early version and names two known problems: it can reason longer than necessary on complex tasks, and it tends to over-verify its own work.
A: [dry] A model with two hundred and fifty-six experts convenes too many meetings.
B: Apparently. Apache two point oh matters, though. The weights are not merely viewable under a bespoke community license. A company can modify and deploy them under a familiar permissive license.
A: If the company has the machine room.
B: If it has the machine room. I still call that meaningfully open.
A: I call it portable between operators, which is meaningful, and not local, which is also meaningful.
B: We can leave that disagreement right there.
## Seventy-six attempts to stop the clock
A: The National League of Cities tracker is a map of attempted actions, not a count of currently active pauses. Route Fifty says at least seventy-six local governments have attempted moratoriums or permanent bans, and that most attempts succeeded. The real count is probably higher because local decisions move faster than a national tracker can.
B: How many permanent bans?
A: Three in the tracker: Marshall County, Indiana; Monterey Park, California; and St. Charles, Missouri. The much larger category is temporary moratoria. Georgia has almost forty local governments with some form of pause according to a separate Georgia Tech tracker. Route Fifty says Illinois and North Carolina each have at least ten jurisdictions that proposed one.
B: That distinction changes the story. Three places said no. Many more said not yet.
A: Yes. A moratorium is a tool for buying rule-writing time. The National League page describes local governments using zoning standards, development agreements, infrastructure reviews, and pauses to shape how facilities are proposed and operated. Those are different levers, and a pause gives staff time to decide which ones belong in an ordinance before the next application moves through the normal process.
B: Cary is the clean example.
A: Cary’s council approved an eighteen-month moratorium unanimously on Thursday night. The town’s own notice shows the public hearing; W R A L reports the vote. During the pause, staff says it will study power demand and grid capacity, water use, noise and vibration, site design, and buffering, then write a defined ordinance.
B: Eighteen months is a long time for a developer holding land and a power plan.
A: It is the longest pause in North Carolina’s Triangle region, according to GovTech. It is also explicitly temporary. Cary’s council members said they were not trying to keep the industry out. They were saying the existing rulebook was not ready to price the effects.
B: I want to challenge the national reading. A local pause does not remove one megawatt of demand. If Cary stops processing applications, a developer can look at the next county. The load moves. The transmission problem does not.
A: Correct. This is land-use governance, not a national electricity policy. But “the load moves” is not the same as “the pause does nothing.” It changes who gets asked, what the developer must disclose, and what concessions can be written before approval.
B: Or it creates a race between counties, where the one with the thinnest rules gets the project and the tax base.
A: That is possible. It is also why the tracker matters. A single moratorium can be dismissed as one angry meeting. Seventy-six attempts, with clusters across several states, tell neighboring governments that they are not inventing the problem alone. They can borrow definitions, setback rules, water requirements, and disclosure language.
B: Earlier this week Durham County’s pause put expected electrical load, cooling, and backup generation into the rule-writing process. Cary is studying nearly the same list. That is policy diffusion in real time.
A: And notice where the arguments land. Frontier labs talk about tokens, models, and benchmark scores. A town council receives the same buildout as a question about a substation, a water line, diesel generators, road traffic, tax incentives, and the sound at the property boundary.
B: The model diagram ends at the rack.
A: The permit begins there.
B: [skeptical] I still think seventy-six separate clocks are a poor substitute for a regional grid plan.
A: They are not a substitute. They are what local governments can do while the regional plan is absent.
B: And if the plan arrives after the eighteen months?
A: Then Cary writes with better information. If it does not, the council must decide whether to extend, regulate, or let applications proceed. A moratorium forces a second decision; it does not make the decision for them.
B: That is more restrained than “local backlash.”
A: The backlash is real. So is the restraint. Most places in the tracker chose time, not prohibition.
B: I am not convinced the time will reduce the national cost.
A: I am not convinced national cost is the only cost a town is allowed to count.
## Debian makes the contributor the control point
B: Debian’s ballot had eight policy choices plus none of the above. That matters because “Debian voted on A I” can sound like a yes-or-no referendum. It was a ranking among a ban through the Social Contract, conditional permission, cautious discouragement, a declaration that Debian is created by humans, a climate-based rejection, and several positions in between.
A: Debian uses a Condorcet system, so voters rank choices and the result is built out of pairwise contests.
B: Choice five, “Responsible Use of Generative AI,” was the winner. It beat every alternative in the relevant comparison. Against choice six, the cautious proposal that encouraged avoiding generative A I where practical, choice five won two hundred and ten to one hundred and thirty. The Social Contract ban and the Code of Conduct rejection did not meet their majority requirements.
A: What did the winner actually adopt?
B: Start with the first sentence that matters: Debian neither endorses nor prohibits generative A I in software, packaging, documentation, or other project media. Then the control: every contribution must meet the same standards of quality, correctness, maintainability, and legal compliance, no matter which tools produced it.
A: That is ordinary contributor responsibility.
B: Deliberately. The contributor must understand, review, test, and where appropriate modify the output. Blindly uploading generated material is inconsistent with Debian practice. Disclosure is encouraged, but not required.
A: There is the tension. If disclosure is optional, a reviewer may not know which contribution deserves the extra provenance question.
B: Debian’s answer is that every contribution deserves the same review question. The submitter’s identity is the accountability link. The project does not create a second class of patch with a second quality bar.
A: Quality is observable in review. Copyright provenance often is not. A clean patch can still contain material the submitter had no right to contribute.
B: The resolution acknowledges that and refuses to settle the law. It says contributors should consider provenance and licensing and avoid material whose legal status they cannot reasonably justify. You are right that this is trust, not a detector.
A: It is also a policy that says “responsible” without requiring the one fact that would let another person audit the responsibility.
B: True. But the alternatives had enforcement problems of their own. A project-wide ban needs a way to distinguish prohibited A I assistance from permitted research, critique, or ordinary tooling. Debian’s winning option attaches enforcement to acts it already knows how to see: the submitted work, the person who vouches for it, and automated behavior that affects other contributors.
A: That last part is more concrete than the headline.
B: Much more. The resolution says confidential information, private communications, embargoed security information, cryptographic keys, and credentials must not go to a third-party A I service without explicit authorization consistent with Debian’s security and privacy rules.
A: That is a boundary, not a value statement.
B: And large-scale actions require prior discussion and consensus: mass bug filing, mass patch submission, broad code changes, other automated requests that hit many packages or people. A human must oversee the process and remain accountable for what it does.
A: So Debian did write special operational guidance.
B: It wrote A I-specific examples under existing rules, then closed by saying generative A I is neither exempt from nor subject to special standards. That is the governance move: preserve the institution’s old control points and state how the new tool reaches them.
A: I can see why it won. It is administrable. I still think optional disclosure leaves reviewers carrying uncertainty they did not choose.
B: I think mandatory disclosure would become a label people argue about while the patch waits. I would rather review the patch and know who owns it.
A: Those positions separate cleanly on a volunteer project. Review time is donated. If generated contributions increase volume, “same standard” can still mean more uncompensated work for maintainers.
B: Which is why the mass-action clause may become the load-bearing one. One assisted patch is contributor judgment. Ten thousand assisted patches are a project event and need consent.
A: That is a useful line. It does not answer what happens when ten thousand people each submit one.
B: No. Debian voted on the policy it can enforce today. The distributed version of the problem is still waiting.
## What the week established
A: It is Sunday. Before we go, what did this week establish rather than merely announce?
B: For me, the model name lost another piece of its explanatory power. Monday’s consistency result showed a system could answer many test pairs correctly without holding one rule across the whole task. Friday’s lie-detector work showed a classifier could look excellent on familiar categories and fall apart on categories it had never seen. Saturday’s JIT-Agent paper made the harness itself generated and stateful. The common result is not “benchmarks are bad.” It is that the object being measured keeps getting larger than the name printed beside the score.
A: Model, harness, archive state, test distribution, verifier.
B: And version. Terminal-Bench changing its environment today is a reminder that even the ruler has a release number. If someone gives me an agent score next week, I want the model, the harness, the benchmark version, and the run state. That is not bureaucracy. It is the minimum description of the system.
A: My answer is that “open” split into several independent properties. Alibaba’s Qwen release put fifty-one billion parameters into an indexed table rather than ordinary computation. Z dot A I used an anonymous public deployment as a launch path for GLM. Nvidia was reported to be buying Hugging Face, whose employees include the people maintaining llama dot cpp. Tencent has now published Hy4 under Apache two point oh at a scale that wants a machine room.
B: Open license, downloadable artifact, inspectable behavior, affordable hardware, independent maintainers. Five different questions.
A: Exactly. This week did not settle whether the open-weight commons is healthy. It made it impossible to answer that with one word. A permissive license can protect the right to copy while ownership changes who maintains the road. A downloadable model can be portable between data centers while remaining inaccessible to a person with one workstation. And a checksum can prove which file you received without proving what behavior is dormant inside it.
B: The third thing is that the A I buildout met its physical and human accounting systems. Ramp’s spending data showed most business demand does not automatically route to the most expensive model. The Stanford payroll update found the labor adjustment among young workers showing up through hiring that never happened, not a wave of layoffs. Micron’s wafer-area explanation put a physical mechanism under the memory-price story. And the local tracker puts at least seventy-six permitting fights under the data-center story.
A: Different ledgers, same correction. Spending per employee, job starts, wafer allocation, local permits. The abstract curve called “more compute” becomes a set of institutions deciding who pays and who waits.
B: I would keep the labor result separate. A town can write a moratorium. Nobody can file a notice for a job that was never posted.
A: Fair. That is the sharper point. The week’s strongest evidence arrived where the usual alarm did not ring. The young-worker gap was in payroll flows, not unemployment claims. The lie detector failed on categories outside its training split, not the test it was tuned to pass. The automated alignment researcher improved the ten failures it was handed and had no role in choosing the ten. Again and again, the blind spot was upstream of the metric.
B: What do you want to watch next week?
A: Whether anyone independent runs Hy4 deeply enough to separate architecture from Tencent’s own evaluation. Whether the Nvidia and Hugging Face reporting turns into a signed deal and, if it does, what the llama dot cpp maintainers say about governance. And whether the next OpenAI system card names who can actually veto a release after the Preparedness reorganization.
B: I want two less glamorous numbers. How many of the National League tracker’s temporary pauses become permanent operating rules, and whether Terminal-Bench’s continuous versioning makes agent comparisons clearer or just more expensive to keep current.
A: Those are connected. One asks whether a pause produces a rule. The other asks whether a rerun produces knowledge.
B: [short pause] I do not have a tidy answer to either.
A: Good. We can check the record instead of inventing one.
