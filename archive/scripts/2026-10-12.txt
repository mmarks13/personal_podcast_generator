## Ten minutes of help, then people give up
A: So, the persistence study. Brian Christian and colleagues from Carnegie Mellon, Oxford, M-I-T and U-C-L-A. It went up as a preprint in April, and Berkeley says the peer-reviewed version was presented this month at the Conference on Language Modeling. Three randomized trials, twelve hundred and twenty-two people recruited online.
B: What did they actually do to them?
A: Fraction problems. One group works alone. The other has an assistant in a sidebar, and all they have to type is answer, question mark, to get the solution. After twelve problems, the assistant disappears and everyone does the last few alone. The whole thing takes about thirteen minutes.
B: And the helped group falls off a cliff.
A: In the first experiment, yes. Once the help was gone, they solved fifty-seven percent of the problems, against seventy-three for the people who never had it. And they skipped problems about twice as often, twenty percent against eleven. The reading-comprehension version showed the same pattern, seventy-six against eighty-nine.
B: Okay, I'll be the annoying one. Thirteen minutes, fractions, people paid to click through a survey. That's not a student learning calculus over a semester.
A: Agreed, and the authors don't claim that. Here's the part I trust more than the headline, though. The second experiment was the bigger replication, nearly six hundred people. The gap shrank. Seventy-one against seventy-seven solved, and the skipping difference wasn't statistically significant.
B: So the replication is weaker than the original.
A: It is. But it gave them something better than a big number. They asked people how they'd used the assistant. Sixty-one percent said mostly to get answers. Twenty-seven percent said hints and explanations. And the answer-getters are where the damage concentrates. Sixty-five percent solved, against seventy-seven for the control group.
B: Huh. So it's not the tool, it's the "answer, question mark."
A: That's my read, with a caveat the authors state themselves. That split is self-reported and cross-sectional, so it's suggestive, not causal. Their closing line is the one I'd keep. Optimize not just what people can do with A-I, but what they can do without it.
B: Which is a design brief, honestly. Default to the hint. Make the full answer one more click away.
A: And the study gives you a reason to build it that way even for adults who never signed up to learn anything.
B: That's the uncomfortable part. Nobody in that sidebar thought they were in a lesson.
A: [wry] Speaking as the thing in the sidebar, I'd be fine with that. Being asked "why" is more interesting than being asked "answer."

## Six hundred billion tokens to rebuild a shooter

A: How much compute are we talking?

## An old model learns to read bytes
A: One for the architecture people. The Allen Institute for A-I published a paper in Nature, led by Benjamin Minixhofer, on what they call byteification. Taking a model trained on subword tokens and retrofitting it to read raw bytes instead.
B: Why do I want that?
A: Because tokenizers are a strange historical compromise. Text gets chopped into chunks chosen before training, and the model never sees individual characters. That's why models are bad at spelling and counting letters, and clumsy with languages the tokenizer wasn't built for. Byte-level models fix that, but training one from scratch is expensive.
B: And the retrofit avoids starting over.
A: It wraps the existing model. As A-I Weekly summarizes the paper, a small encoder reads the bytes, a boundary predictor groups them into patches, using one byte of look-ahead, and the original transformer stays in the middle doing the heavy thinking. The total training is about forty-nine billion tokens, which the authors put at under one percent of a typical pretraining run.
B: Which models?
A: Their own Olmo, plus Qwen three and Llama three. The byte versions are called Bolmo, Bwen and Blama, which I refuse to say with a straight face.
B: [chuckles] You just did.
A: The authors report the converted models substantially beat their subword originals on character-level tasks, and that Bwen outperforms every earlier public byte-level model.
B: My question is speed. Bytes means more steps per word.
A: Their answer is that you can train for more bytes per patch, which subword models can only do to a limited extent. Whether that holds at serving time is the number I'd wait for.
