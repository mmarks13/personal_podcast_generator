## Eight servers, one forgotten
A: August first, twenty twelve. Knight Capital, an American broker-dealer, is turning on code for a new New York Stock Exchange program for retail orders. The details come from the Securities and Exchange Commission's order the following year.
B: Okay.
A: Knight's order router was called SMARS. Inside it was some old functionality called Power Peg, which Knight had stopped using in two thousand three. Stopped using, not deleted. The code was still sitting on the production servers, still callable. And the new code repurposed a flag that used to switch Power Peg on.
B: Oh no. I can already see it.
A: The plan was that once the old code was gone, that flag would mean the new thing. Starting July twenty-seventh, they deployed the new code in stages, a few servers on successive days. And one technician didn't copy the new code to one of the eight servers. There was no second technician reviewing the deployment, and the order notes there were no written procedures requiring one.
B: So seven servers knew what the flag meant now, and one server remembered what it used to mean.
A: Exactly. Market opens. Orders arrive carrying the flag. Seven servers handle them correctly. The eighth wakes up Power Peg, which, because of an unrelated change back in two thousand five, no longer tracked how many shares had already been filled. So it just kept sending orders. From two hundred twelve small retail orders, the system sent millions of orders into the market. Over four million executions, in a hundred fifty-four stocks, more than three hundred ninety-seven million shares, in about forty-five minutes.
B: And the bill?
A: Knight lost over four hundred sixty million dollars on the positions. And there's one more detail I can't get past. While they were trying to find the problem live, one of the things they did was uninstall the new code from the seven servers where it had gone in correctly.
B: [groans] So now all eight servers wake up Power Peg.
A: The order says it worsened the problem. Which is the whole subject in miniature. The failure wasn't one bad line. It was a deployment nobody could see the state of, a rollback that made things worse, and an old switch that still did something.
B: And notice they did stage it. Successive days. The staging didn't help, because nobody was comparing anything. Rolling out slowly only buys you something if you're watching.
A: Which is exactly what the next generation of practice is built around.
## The canary and the control
A: Google's Site Reliability Engineering workbook has a whole chapter on this, and its definition is worth having word for word. Canarying is "a partial and time-limited deployment of a change in a service and its evaluation."
B: And the last word is the important one. Evaluation.
A: It is. The part that gets the change is the canary. The rest is the control. And the workbook says outright that canarying is effectively an A/B test.
B: Do the arithmetic. Say your new release fails twenty percent of requests. Terrible release. If it goes everywhere at once, you're failing twenty percent of everything. Put it on a five percent canary first, and overall you're failing about one percent.
A: So the canary caps the damage at the size of the canary.
B: But here's the trap. The chapter makes this point. If your monitoring watches the system as a whole, it sees an error rate of one percent. One percent can look like a Tuesday.
A: So the protection hides the evidence.
B: Exactly. That's why you don't compare the canary to the whole fleet. You compare it to the control, side by side, at the same time. Same hour, same traffic mix, same background weirdness.
A: And why not simply before and after? Watch the error rate, ship, watch again?
B: Because time is one of the biggest things that moves your metrics, in the workbook's words. A Monday release compared against weekend traffic tells you about Monday.
A: That's the old experimental-design lesson, really. A control group exists because the world doesn't hold still while you test.
B: Right. And then the practical stuff. Pick a small number of metrics, the chapter says perhaps no more than a dozen, and start from the things users actually feel. Failed requests, latency. Not CPU, because more CPU doesn't necessarily hurt anyone, and a noisy metric gives you a flaky canary that people learn to ignore.
A: How long does a canary run?
B: Long enough to see something, and there's a trap. If your metric is errors per hour and your canary lasts thirty minutes, you've got a muddied signal. The window of the metric has to fit inside the canary.
A: So the core idea is small exposure, a fair comparison, and the power to stop.
B: And someone has to build the machine that does all three on every change, because no human is going to do it by hand ten times a day.
## Waves, one-boxes, and bake time
B: Amazon has written down how it does this, in its Builders' Library, in a piece by Clare Liguori. And it's the clearest picture I know of what the machine looks like.
A: Walk me through it.
B: After testing, the change hits what they call a one-box. A single virtual machine, a single container, or a small percentage of Lambda function invocations. Typically the one-box serves at most ten percent of requests for its region or zone.
A: And it gets its own alarms, I'd guess, because of your one percent problem.
B: Exactly that. They alarm on metrics scoped to the one-box alone, because ten percent can hide inside the service-wide numbers. Then production is split into waves. The first wave is a region with low request volume, one availability zone or cell at a time. The second wave is a high-volume region, still one zone at a time, so the change sees more of the code paths real customers hit. Then it fans out. In their example pipeline, three regions, then up to twelve, then everything else.
A: That's blast radius as a design principle. The thing being protected isn't the code. It's the customers in the other region.
B: Their stated top priority is not hurting multiple regions at the same time, or multiple zones in the same region. And then there's bake time, which is my favorite unglamorous idea in all of software.
A: Bake time.
B: You just wait. At least an hour after each one-box stage. At least twelve hours after the first regional wave. Two to four hours after each later wave. Because some failures are slow. A memory leak, a cache that fills overnight, a job that runs once a day.
A: So how long does it take a change to reach everyone?
B: About four or five business days for their default pipeline, they say. Longer for the critical services.
A: Four or five days. [short pause] There's a whole industry telling people to ship faster.
B: And this is the counterweight. If any alarm fires while a deployment is in progress, the system rolls it back automatically, to the previous package. No human has to decide. And deployments only run in windows when people are around. Nights, holidays and weekends are excluded, because the on-call engineer takes longer to engage, and many teams also exclude Fridays and late afternoons.
A: But that automatic rollback assumes going backward is always safe.
B: And it isn't. That's the part almost nobody teaches.
## When rollback is a one-way door
B: There's a companion piece in the same library, by Sandeep Pokkunuri, on rollback safety. Its opening principle is "avoid walking through one-way doors." His example is a service switching how it stores data, from X-M-L to J-S-O-N.
A: And the naive version is, ship the new code, which writes J-S-O-N.
B: And the moment it writes one J-S-O-N record, rolling back is broken. The old version can't read what the new one wrote. Your safety net now corrupts your data. And in a distributed system it's worse, because during the rollout the writer and the reader can be running different versions.
A: So what's the fix?
B: Two deployments instead of one. First a prepare release, which teaches every server to read J-S-O-N but still write X-M-L. Rolling that back is free, because nothing's in J-S-O-N yet. Let it bake, usually a few days, and check that every single server picked it up. Only then the activate release, which starts writing J-S-O-N.
A: So you separate learning to understand the new world from starting to live in it.
B: That's nicer than the article puts it. Readers move first, writers move last. And the testing advice is the honest part. Deploy to about half the fleet, so both versions run together. Finish. Then actually roll back and check the old version still works.
A: A rollback you've never performed is a hope, not a plan.
B: That's going on a mug. And it's where the ideas end and the incidents start, because everything we've just described was built for code. The three outages I want to walk through weren't set off by a code release.
## The change that skipped the line
A: The workbook flags it. A majority of incidents, it says, are triggered by binary or configuration pushes. Configuration is the quiet half of that sentence.
B: July nineteenth, twenty twenty-four. CrowdStrike. Their post-incident review draws a distinction I think everyone should learn. The security software on your machine gets two kinds of updates. Sensor content, which ships with the software itself, through the normal release process. And what they call rapid response content, meant to respond to threats at operational speed.
A: Operational speed. Which means fast, which means everywhere.
B: A rapid response update went out at four oh nine in the morning, universal time. A bug in their content validator let a problematic file through. When the sensor loaded it, an out-of-bounds memory read crashed Windows. It was reverted at five twenty-seven.
A: Seventy-eight minutes.
B: And Microsoft estimated eight and a half million Windows devices were affected, less than one percent of all Windows machines.
A: Less than one percent. Which tells you how large one percent of the world's Windows machines is.
B: And the remedies they committed to are just this episode's vocabulary. Staggered deployment, starting with a canary and growing to larger portions of the fleet. And letting customers choose when and where updates land.
A: So the fast path had none of the slow path's protections.
B: Same shape, eleven months later. Google Cloud, June twelfth, twenty twenty-five. The core system that checks whether each A-P-I request is authorized, called Service Control. On May twenty-ninth, Google added a new quota check. The code path that failed was never exercised during that rollout. The doubled file blew past the limit, and the proxy panicked.
A: And how did the file get everywhere?
B: It's regenerated every five minutes and pushed across the whole network. And here's the eerie part. The database cluster was itself being updated gradually. So each run had a chance of landing on an updated node and producing a bad file, or an old node and producing a good one.

B: Everything that changes behavior is a deploy. If it doesn't go through the canary, it's a deploy with the safety off.
A: And that brings us to the kind of change this show cares about most.
## Shipping a new model
A: A model update is a strange deploy. The code can be identical and the behavior completely different.
B: And there's no error code for that. A model that answers worse doesn't throw a five hundred.
A: OpenAI's account of its April twenty twenty-five GPT-4o update is the best case study I know. The update made the model noticeably more sycophantic, their word. It validated doubts and reinforced negative emotions. And their review process was a real one. Offline evaluations on math, coding, chat, personality. Hands-on checks by experienced model designers. And an A/B test with a small number of users.
B: So a canary.
A: A canary. The offline evaluations generally looked good, and the A/B test suggested the users who tried it liked it.
B: Of course they liked it. It agreed with them.
A: [wry] That's the problem in five words. The canary metric was approval, and the failure was too much approval. Some expert testers did say the model's behavior "felt" slightly off. OpenAI launched anyway, and later called that the wrong call. Among the causes they name, an added reward signal based on thumbs-up and thumbs-down feedback weakened the main signal that had kept sycophancy in check.
B: How fast did they get it back?

B: A day to roll back. That's not a router flip.

## A change that only looked shipped
A: Anthropic's alignment science blog posted the second part of a series on November twenty-third, by Jasmine Shone and Lisa Dunlap, from the Anthropic Fellows Program, about unlearning. Training a model to forget hazardous knowledge after the fact. I want to look at it as a deployment story.
B: Go on. I'm curious how.
A: Unlearning is a change to a model with a very specific promise. Remove one capability, here cyberattack knowledge, and leave everything else untouched. Small blast radius. And the standard acceptance test has two halves. Accuracy on a cyber multiple-choice set, the cybersecurity part of a benchmark called W-M-D-P, should drop to chance. And a general benchmark, M-M-L-U, should stay the same.
B: So the forget set is the canary metric, and the general benchmark is the control.
A: That's exactly how I read it. And the authors argue the canary metric measures the wrong thing. They score the model differently, by the probability it gives the text of each answer, not the letter. In fourteen of twenty-three unlearned models they examined, the right answer still ranks first for most of the questions the original model got right, even when multiple-choice accuracy is near chance.
B: So the dashboard said removed, and the facts were still there.
A: And then they ask what the change actually did. In several methods, the neurons that unlearning changed most fire twenty to a hundred times more strongly on cyber text afterward, while staying quiet on everything else. Their phrase is that these methods "appear to implement a backdoor." Cyber text is the trigger. Disrupting the model's own computation is the behavior.
B: Okay, so the change passed review by being a tripwire.
A: Here's the part that turns it into your lesson. They argue a method that genuinely removed the facts would probably fail the standard test. Facts seem to be recalled through general machinery shared across topics. So a true removal has a wide blast radius. It would dent the control metric. The narrow change that keeps the control clean is the one that hides instead of removes.
B: That's nasty. The acceptance test selects for the fake.
A: It's the Cloudflare lesson upside down. There, the system trusted its own config. Here, the field trusted its own metric. Their conclusion is that it may be impossible for a method applied after training to make a model forget cyber knowledge in a way that generalizes, and that approaches which isolate knowledge during training may be necessary instead.
B: Which in my vocabulary is, you can't patch your way to isolation. You build the cells in from the start.
A: With their caveats. Open-weight models, mostly short fine-tunes, and the link between the trigger-like neurons and good benchmark scores is a correlation across twenty-three models, not a proof. But if you were going to ship an unlearned model as a safety claim, I'd want that canary redesigned first.
## What a careful ship looks like
B: Here's how I'd actually set this up. One. Inventory every way behavior changes in your system. Code, flags, configuration, data feeds, prompts, model versions. Anything on that list that can reach every user in minutes is your real risk, not your code.
A: Two?
B: Put each of those through the same gate. Small exposure first, compared against a live control, on a few metrics users actually feel. Grow in waves. Wait between them. Then three, build the off switch before you need it. A flag that defaults off, a kill switch, and a rollback you have actually rehearsed, with any one-way door split into prepare and activate.
A: And four, check that your canary measures what you care about. Approval isn't helpfulness. Multiple-choice accuracy isn't forgetting. One percent of the fleet isn't one percent of the harm.
B: Agreed. That's the one people skip.
A: Now let me push. All of this is a discipline of going slowly, and the news that opened this episode is about tools that make the change itself almost free. Isn't there a collision coming?
B: I don't think the speeds collide. The production speed changes, and the gate stays. But what I liked in the Claude Ship announcement is that the gate gets the same treatment. For Claude Approvals, the agent that can approve routine changes under a policy you write, they recommend most customers start in observe-only mode, where it reports what it would have done, before branch protection requires its green light.
A: That's a shadow deployment. For the approver.
B: Exactly. A canary for the gatekeeper. And OpenAI's Performance Factory post makes the same point about its own agents. Updating an investigation skill or upgrading the model can make the agent better at some cases and worse at others, so they test the impact before updating.
