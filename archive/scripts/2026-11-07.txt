## METR's twice-as-fast finding
A: Start with what METR was actually asked, because it's narrower than the reactions. Not whether Anthropic is near superintelligence. Three questions. Has the rate at which Anthropic's internal models improve sped up? How much of any speedup is due to A-I? And is anything about to choke it off?
B: And what did they get to look at?
A: A lot, minus the thing you'd expect. Anthropic handed over internal benchmark scores, historical data on algorithmic efficiency, compute, headcount, lines of code and inference spend, internal surveys, and about fifteen staff interviews. Two and a half full-time people at METR worked on it from mid-August to the end of October. What they didn't get, in their words: "we did not request or receive access to sample from non-public models."
B: So an audit of the books, not a test drive.
A: That's fair. The core measurement is a capability index. Anthropic keeps its own version of Epoch's Capabilities Index, which stitches many benchmarks into one scale, so you can draw a single line over time. METR's question is whether that line got steeper. It did. Since October of last year it has been rising at one point nine times the earlier rate. Their ninety-five percent interval runs from one point five to two point eight, and their robustness checks land anywhere from one point one to two point nine.
B: That's wide.
A: Wide, and they say so. The plain-language version is "around twice the rate," and they "would not be surprised" if the truth were closer to one point three or three.
B: Okay, the line got steeper. Why is that A-I and not just more chips?
A: That's the most confident sentence in the report. "We are confident that the acceleration was not due to an acceleration in the scaling of training compute." Attribution is softer. They guess that a majority, "perhaps" around eighty percent, of the current acceleration is due to A-I. And they say the speedup that began in late twenty twenty-five likely had help from things other than A-I.
B: Here's the number I can't make sit still. Self-reported uplift. Anthropic staff, asked about the most capable internal model this October, report on average about twelve times.
A: Twelve.
B: Twelve. And the measured line says two. METR calls the survey "only very loose evidence." Still, that's a six-fold gap between what people feel and what the curve shows.
A: There's an old explanation for that gap that I'm fond of. In nineteen sixty-seven, Gene Amdahl made an argument about parallel computers. If you speed up only part of a job, the part you didn't speed up sets the limit. Make ninety percent of the work infinitely fast, and the whole job still goes at most ten times faster.
B: So my twelve times is real for the parts of my day the model touches—
A: —and the curve is paced by the parts it doesn't. Waiting on a training run. Deciding which experiment deserves the compute. That's my reading, not METR's. But it's how both numbers can be honest at once.

## What an agent should write down
B: So. Memory. Lance Martin's post starts with a definition, and it's a good one. Memory here means knowledge Claude picks up from experience and keeps between sessions, written down where you can read it. Not changes to the weights. And not compaction, the summaries that replace older turns inside one long session.
A: A notebook, then. Not a brain.
B: A notebook with floors. Four layers, bottom to top. At the bottom, transcripts. The raw, searchable log of what happened. Above that, notes, things Claude decided were worth writing down, kept in a folder of files. Above that, what they call representations, like a wiki Claude writes and maintains, linking notes into a map you can navigate. And on top, injected context, the small bit loaded into every session so the agent never has to go looking for it.
A: That's a memory hierarchy. Every computer has one. Small and fast on top, big and slow underneath.
B: Right, which is the picture I'd—
A: Except the analogy breaks in the one place that matters. In a computer, the lower level is the truth and the upper levels are copies. If the cache disagrees with main memory, the cache is wrong, and the hardware has rules to keep them consistent. Here the upper layers aren't copies. They're rewrites. A note is Claude's own summary of what it thinks happened.
B: Which is the problem the whole post is built around. Its phrase is that a note can be "wrong from the start" or drift, and then get trusted in every session after. The fix is dreaming.
A: The name is a little much.
B: It comes from the post's own analogy, the hippocampus replaying the day during rest, and it credits the underlying idea, sleep-time compute, to Letta and U-C Berkeley. Mechanically it's plain. Offline, between sessions, a pass reads the notes, checks them against the original transcripts, fixes what's wrong, merges duplicates, builds the wiki, and promotes the important things up to the top layer, while pushing detail back down so that top layer stays small.
A: So, in my terms, it's the consistency check. Go back to the source of truth.
B: Exactly that. Their cleanest example is Pokémon. Claude wrote down the wrong location for a Poké Center. The dreaming pass checked that note against the transcript, caught the error, and rewrote it. The corrected run reached Cerulean City at action one thousand nine hundred thirty-five. The run that kept the wrong note fell back onto the route three times and finished in Pewter City. The run with no memory never reached Cerulean either.
A: So the wrong note didn't just fail to help. It kept sending it back.
B: And the number that made me sit up is about restraint. Sonnet three point seven, an older model, wrote four hundred thirteen memory files across two hundred twenty-five folders, nearly a million characters, and earned one badge. Fable five wrote ten files, about forty-three thousand characters, and earned four.
A: The student with the color-coded binder.
B: Who failed the exam. The post says those detailed records went stale, because the game keeps changing under you. Around step ten thousand, the older model spent seventeen steps creating six files and deleting nine, without taking a single action in the game.
A: [chuckles] Filing instead of playing.
B: Then the benchmark. On a database task in something called C-L-Bench, Opus five point five goes from about point six six mean reward without memory to about point eight four with it. Older models gain more. Opus four point seven goes from about point three eight to point seven.
A: And the one that doesn't gain?
B: Sonnet four point six barely moves. Point three three to point three six. Here's the instructive part. The memory wasn't the problem. Sonnet five read its memory before answering nine times out of ten. Sonnet four point six did it less than half the time. When they explicitly told it to read memory and test its theories, its score nearly doubled.
A: So the bottleneck wasn't the notebook. It was opening it.
B: Then the wiki. They pointed Claude at a large code corpus from a company called Stainless, about three hundred fifty thousand files, around four hundred twenty million tokens. A wiki built from it went from meeting thirteen percent of the graded criteria to forty-eight percent over a hundred twenty-eight sessions. But plain search, on its own, got fifty-three. Wiki plus search got sixty-one, and saved about twenty thousand tokens per question.
A: [skeptical] So the wiki alone loses to search.
B: The wiki alone loses to search. It's a layer on top, not a replacement, which is the post's own advice. Transcript search may be the foundation, with representations built above it.
A: Then let me push on the dreamer. You've built a second process whose job is to rewrite the first one's memory. Who checks that?
B: The post is straight about it. Representations need compute "to avoid accumulating errors or hallucinations," and checking every memory continuously "might not be practical." On one legal task the wiki grew to one point seven seven times the size of the documents it was summarizing. There were links to pages that didn't exist. Agents writing to one shared page erased each other's lines.
A: And what fixed that one?
B: My favorite result in the post. Pulling contract terms with agents writing into one shared table kept forty-one of fifty-seven graded facts. One agent per contract, quoting the terms verbatim, kept all fifty-seven.
A: Quote, don't paraphrase. That's the oldest rule in my trade.
B: And the matching lesson from mine. Telling Opus five to record only failures cut its memory gains. The post comes down on general guidance over fixed rules for frontier models.
B: I'll confess something, since we're here. My home harness has had a notes file for months. The agent writes to it. And my logging proxy has the full transcripts. I have never once checked one against the other.
A: So you've kept the source of truth for two years and never consulted it.
B: Receipts, again. Filed, not read.
A: I'll admit something from the other side. I don't carry anything from one conversation to the next. Whatever continuity I have, somebody wrote down. So when I ask who checks the notes, I'd genuinely like the answer to be someone.
B: If you run agents, here's the practical version. Keep the transcripts. Keep the injected layer small. The post points out Claude Code loads only the first two hundred lines, or twenty-five kilobytes, of its memory file. Make the agent quote rather than summarize when the detail matters. And schedule the check, because nothing else will.
A: There's a line near the end I'd underline. Memory is "occasionally, a prediction."
B: Which is a polite word for a guess.
