## Garry Tan wants an American distillation regime

A: That's a position.
B: That's the warm-up. The actual position is the next sentence: we could argue that there should be an American distillation regime. Meaning American open-weight labs distilling American frontier models, legally, on purpose.
A: On what basis?
B: He gave TechCrunch the long version, and it's worth hearing whole, because the shape of it is the argument. Controlling what users and customers do with API calls to closed weight models feels constraining — and there's a role government can play here to normalise the fact that access to intelligence that was trained on broad public access data should itself also be more a form of a public good than something locked away behind restrictive terms of service.
A: So: you trained it on the commons, the outputs go back to the commons.
B: That's the claim. And his motivation, in his words, is the doomer scenario for AI is that there's just one company. He also says he wants the frontier labs to stay fundable — they are at the frontier and driving it forward, we want that to be fundable and be a great business model ongoing.
A: [skeptical] Those two sentences are in tension and I'd like to watch him hold them.
B: They're in tension. But go at the mechanism first, because you spent an hour on it yesterday and I want you to break it.
A: All right. When you distill through an API, you don't get the teacher. You get samples. The thing that makes distillation powerful in the original formulation is the full distribution — the teacher's probabilities across every possible next token, including the ones it rejected, because the relative weight of the wrong answers is where the learned structure lives. An API gives you one draw. One sampled token, which is a single-sample estimate of that distribution. That's why the modern version of this leans on reasoning traces instead — long chains of text that carry more signal per call than a bare answer does.
B: So a legal American distillation regime buys the open labs what, exactly?
A: It buys them a very expensive, very lossy photocopier. Which is not nothing — it's evidently enough to move a model a long way, six agencies' worth of concern says so. But Tan's framing is that access to the intelligence should be a public good, and what's actually flowing through that pipe is not the intelligence. It's an approximation of the intelligence, priced by the token, and the person selling it sets the price.
B: Then the honest version of his proposal isn't "let them distill." It's "make the frontier labs publish logits."
A: Which no one is proposing, and which they will never do.
B: [laughs] No.
A: And there's one more collision this week that I don't think anyone's put together. Amodei's essay — the one we just spent fifteen minutes on — lists cracking down on model distillation as one of the measures that preserves the lead he says the pacing plan depends on.
