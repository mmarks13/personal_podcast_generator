## What a place does
A: Google Research, yesterday. Maria Despoina Siampou and Shushman Choudhury, with colleagues at Google and U S C. The question is one I find genuinely lovely: what does a language model actually know about a place?
B: It knows what the internet says about the place.
A: It knows the label. And the label is thin. "Coffee shop" tells you almost nothing about whether it's four people with laptops all day or a queue of forty between seven and nine. Their sentence for it is that our collective actions at a physical place are often far more descriptive than the formal words used to label it.
B: So the input is foot traffic.
A: Aggregated, anonymized visit patterns, yes. And the method has three parts. First, visit alignment. A temporal encoder takes arrival windows, departure trends and stay durations and turns them into a dense vector — they call the result a functional centroid, capturing the yearly and weekly rhythm of the place.
B: A shape in time instead of a word.
A: A shape in time. Second, and this is the part that solves the real problem: spatial multiscale visit propagation. Most places are data-sparse — the long tail of small businesses nobody's phone lingers at. So they transfer aggregated visit patterns from data-rich neighbors to data-poor locations, at several geographic scales at once.
B: Borrowing the block's rhythm for the shop you have nothing on.
A: Borrowing the block's rhythm, and the neighborhood's, and so on up. Third, text-mobility synergy: they align the language embedding with the mobility vector by maximizing cosine similarity between them, so the semantic meaning survives and the operational behavior gets folded in.
B: Numbers.
A: Five prediction tasks on entirely unseen locations — trained on observed places, evaluated on places held out. Visit intent classification: eighty-one point nine percent relative gain. Price level: seventy-five point one percent improvement. Busyness forecasting: twenty-four point seven percent more accurate. Plus opening and closing hours, and detecting permanent closure.
B: [skeptical] Relative gain over what?
A: Text-only embeddings — Gemini embeddings — plus trajectory-based geospatial models, they name TrajGPT, plus hybrid variants.
B: Then eighty-one point nine percent relative tells me almost nothing by itself, and the absolute numbers aren't in the post. A large relative gain over a weak baseline and a large relative gain over a strong one are different results, and they read identically.
A: Agreed, and I'd say the same. Take the direction, not the magnitude.
B: My bigger problem isn't the numbers. It's that I read this and there is nothing for me to do on Monday. Two metro areas — Los Angeles and Houston. Aggregated mobility data at that resolution is held by roughly four organizations on earth and Google is one of them. If the mechanism is good, and it sounds good, it's good for people who already have the hardest input to get.
A: That's fair, and I'd separate two things inside it. The data moat is real and I won't argue it. But the idea travels further than the data does. Anyone with a stream of events attached to places has some version of this — a point-of-sale system, a transit operator, a booking platform, a university with door badges.
B: That's a much smaller signal than a city's phones.
A: Much smaller, and possibly enough, because the trick isn't the volume. The trick is that they aligned a behavioral vector with a text embedding rather than replacing it. You keep the language model's semantics and add a channel.
B: [conceding, a little] That part does generalize. Fine.
A: Let me do the historian's note briefly, because it's short. Every gazetteer ever compiled has recorded what a place is called and what it is officially for. Categories, addresses, names. And what people who live somewhere actually know about a place is when to go and when not to bother, which never made it into the record because there was no way to write it down at scale.
B: And now it's a vector.
A: Now it's a vector. On the privacy question — their framing is that everything is aggregated and anonymized and cannot draw conclusions about individual users or anything personalized.
B: Which is the right claim to make. Whether an aggregate is as far from an individual as people assume is a question with a long literature, and I'm not settling it off a blog post.
