## Google pauses its open-source bug bounty
A: Let me start in nineteen eighty-three. A company called Hunter and Ready makes an operating system called V-R-T-X. They run an advertisement with the tagline, "Get a bug if you find a bug." Find a bug in their software, and they'll give you a Volkswagen Beetle.
B: [laughs] That's a real thing?
A: It's a real thing. Then in nineteen ninety-five Netscape launches what's usually called the first modern bug bounty, for the beta of Navigator two point oh. B: And the company gets an army it only pays when it wins.

## LoopVL: running the same layers again
A: Here's an idea that's older than it looks. A normal transformer is a stack. Layer one, layer two, all the way up, and every layer has its own weights. In twenty nineteen, a paper called the Universal Transformer asked, what if you didn't stack new layers? What if you took a smaller block and ran it again, and again, on its own output?
B: Depth without paying for more parameters.
A: Depth without storing more. We touched this a month ago, on recurrent depth and what it does to readable reasoning. This paper takes it somewhere new, into vision. LoopVL. The language backbone has two stacks, each sixteen layers, called L and H, with separate weights. The default schedule runs L three times, then H, and does that whole cycle twice. Thirty-two unique layers, a hundred and twenty-eight layer calls.
B: And it's trained like that from scratch?
A: From scratch. Language pretraining, then multimodal training, then post-training. The image goes through a frozen vision encoder, and then the image tokens get updated on every pass, alongside the question.
B: Okay, numbers. What's the comparison?
A: The cleanest one is against a dense thirty-two-layer model of about the same size, trained on the same hundred and forty billion tokens. On MMStar, a general multimodal test, it goes from fifty-five point three to sixty-three point five. On RealWorldQA, fifty-five point three to seventy-one. On chart questions, fifty-one to seventy-four and a half.
B: Fine, but that's the same parameters with several times the compute. Of course it wins. What about a model with four times the parameters?
A: That's the comparison I'd want too, and they ran it. A four-billion-parameter dense model, same tokens. LoopVL beats it on MMStar and RealWorldQA. It's slightly behind on a few others, chart questions and diagrams among them. And the authors estimate its training compute at a bit less than the big one.
B: Here's my builder objection. A hundred and twenty-eight layer calls is a hundred and twenty-eight layer calls. You save the memory for the weights. You don't save the time.
A: And the paper says so, which I liked. Their sentence: "Equal parameter storage is therefore not synonymous with equal training FLOPs or equal wall-clock cost."
B: Then here's the honest pitch. Given what Micron said on its call, a model that stores less and computes more is the shape you'd want if memory is the thing that's sold out. It's not a free lunch. It's a different bill.
A: That's the trade exactly. Now the part I promised. The obvious dream with loops is a dial. Easy question, loop twice. Hard question, loop eight times. More thinking, same weights.
B: And?
A: Not with this checkpoint. They took the trained model and changed only how many times it loops at inference. Run one more full cycle than it was trained for, a hundred and ninety-two layer calls, and MMStar falls from sixty-three and a half to fifty-eight. Run two more, and it falls to twenty-four.
B: [surprised] Twenty-four. So more compute made it dramatically worse.
A: Dramatically. Fewer loops break it too. Their phrase is "equal unrolled depth does not imply equivalent behavior."
B: That rhymes with the paper we opened yesterday's LoRA episode with.
A: It does, and there's a part of that paper we didn't get to. They also tested models that were pretrained to loop. One of them, Ouro, a one point four billion parameter model, follows a reference chain of zero lines after one loop, then about one and a half, two, and it stops climbing at about two. Another, Huginn, sits around one and a half lines whether it gets eight, sixteen or thirty-two passes.
B: So the loops are there and nothing uses them.
A: Until the tiny edit. With the rank-eight adapter, the same Ouro model follows sixty lines after four loops, and at least a hundred and sixty after eight.
B: So looping is capacity, not capability. The model has to be taught to use the extra passes.
A: That's how I'd put it. One more thing from LoopVL, and I'll size it carefully. The authors report what they call Visual Aha Moments. At the boundary between the two cycles, the model's attention over the image suddenly concentrates. A concentration measure jumps from about point four to about point eight six.
B: [skeptical] "Aha" is a lot of word for an attention statistic.
A: It is, and they hedge it themselves. They say high concentration doesn't identify which image regions actually matter causally. And they say they haven't trained a matched control model, so they can't yet say the effect comes from looping at all.
B: Then I'd file it as a picture, not a finding.
A: A good picture. The finding is the other one. Running the same weights again works if, and only if, it's what the model learned to do.
