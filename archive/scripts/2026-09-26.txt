## Guardrails, on trial
A: Let me start with the label, because "supply chain risk" sounds like it's about shipping containers. It's a procurement tool. It lets the government keep a vendor's product out of its systems because of what that product might do once it's inside. The political energy behind these powers came from the era of worrying about Kaspersky antivirus and Huawei telecoms gear. The question was always whether somebody else could reach through the product into ours.
B: And nobody's alleging a foreign hand on Claude.
A: Nobody. The fight is about what Claude won't do. Here's the timeline as CNBC lays it out. Anthropic signed a two-hundred-million-dollar contract with the Pentagon in July of last year. That September, negotiating Claude's deployment on the department's GenAI dot mil platform, the talks collapsed. The department wanted access for all lawful purposes. Anthropic wanted assurance that Claude would not be used for fully autonomous weapons or domestic mass surveillance. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth accused Anthropic of trying to "seize veto power over the operational decisions of the United States military." In March, the department designated Anthropic a supply chain risk.
B: Under two laws, which is why there are two courts.
A: Two designations, two courts, and so far two opposite answers. In San Francisco last month, Judge Rita Lin struck one down. She found the government's actions "were based on a desire to make a public example out of Anthropic," blocked the government-wide ban, and blocked the order barring military contractors from doing any business with the company. On Friday, the D-C Circuit upheld the other one, under what CNBC calls the Supply Chain Security Act. Judge Gregory Katsas wrote the majority, Judge Neomi Rao joined, and Judge Karen LeCraft Henderson dissented.
B: So what's actually still in force?
A: On CNBC's description, the upheld designation keeps the military from using Anthropic's models, and keeps defense contractors from using them in their work with the department. Narrower than a government-wide ban. But for a lab, the defense market is not a rounding error. Anthropic says the blacklisting has cost it billions in lost business, and it's on the way to an I-P-O.
B: So why did the majority side with Hegseth?
A: Here's the sentence. "The Secretary raises the deeply sobering prospect of overly constrained A-I models shutting down unexpectedly and thus causing important military operations to fail." B: [skeptical] Okay. Let me be the engineer about "shutting down unexpectedly," because that's an engineering claim wearing a judge's robe. A refusal isn't a crash.
A: Isn't it, from the operator's chair?
