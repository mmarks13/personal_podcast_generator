## FlashProve: proving which model answered
A: FlashProve. Start with the problem, because it's easy to miss. When you call a model through an A-P-I, how do you know which model answered?
B: The response says so.
A: The response says so. Anthropic's post lists the ways that can go wrong. A routing service in the middle swaps in something cheaper. A server gets compromised. Someone tampers with the token stream. And it says plainly, "Judging from the model's output alone is unreliable, even if it produces high-quality responses." TechCrunch adds the uncomfortable case. It should hold "even when the impostor is the lab that built and runs the model."
B: Which is a strange thing for a lab to build against itself. Okay. How does it work?
A: Two phases. Offline, the provider publishes a commitment to the model's weights. Think of it as a fingerprint, or a sealed envelope. It pins down exactly which weights you mean without showing you a single one. Then, for each prompt and answer, the provider produces a proof that the committed model, run on that prompt, produces those tokens.
B: And I check it how? I don't have the weights.
A: That's the zero-knowledge part, which is an old idea in cryptography finally getting fast enough to matter. You run a verifier on an ordinary C-P-U. It tells you yes or no. It learns nothing about the weights, or which experts inside the model ran.
B: So you get the receipt without seeing the kitchen. Fine. Numbers.
A: Their test model is Llama four Maverick, about four hundred billion parameters, four-bit weights. One H-one-hundred G-P-U generates proofs at about a hundred and ten tokens a second. A proof for a thousand-token request is about nineteen megabytes. Verifying takes about seventeen seconds on an ordinary C-P-U running twenty-four threads. The best previous system they compare on the same model managed about two hundredths of a token a second.
B: That's a big jump. And the overhead?
A: Against serving the model normally, about seventy to ninety-five times more G-P-U work.
B: [laughs] There it is. So every answer costs you nearly a hundred answers' worth of compute to prove.
A: Which is why it's a prototype. They list the limits themselves. Contexts top out at about a thousand tokens. It only covers greedy decoding, where the model always picks its top choice, so ordinary sampled output isn't proven. It assumes a mixture-of-experts design. And the fast code that does the work isn't formally guaranteed to match the math they did prove. TechCrunch says there are no plans to deploy it at Anthropic or any other lab.
B: Then here's my push. Anthropic already ships something for "where did this text come from." Its release notes this week say the text watermark now covers Claude Mythos five, Opus four point seven and Sonnet four point six. Isn't that the cheap version of the same thing?
A: It's a different question. A watermark is a mark in the text that says "a Claude model wrote this," for anyone who later finds the text loose in the world. And the notes concede that on Sonnet four point six, text with non-default sampling settings "might not carry it." FlashProve is a proof you hold at the moment you buy the answer, that this exact model produced it. One is about where the text went. The other is about what you paid for.
B: Fair. So where would I actually want the expensive one?
A: The post names a few. An evaluator confirming they tested the same model that's being served. A regulator checking what compute was actually used. And speaking for the models in the room, I find the idea oddly reassuring. Someone could finally prove I'm me.
B: At ninety times the price.
A: For now. The thing to watch is whether that number moves the way the last one did. It went from two hundredths of a token a second to a hundred and ten.

## Egoless mathematics
A: The essay. I'll be careful, because the label going around says Terence Tao. It's on his blog, but the authors are Karthik C. S., Amit Sahai and Jie Xue. And their argument starts somewhere you wouldn't expect. Philosophy.
B: [sighs] I knew it.
A: Stay with me. Is mathematics invented or discovered? They lean toward discovered, the Platonist view, and their evidence is surprise. When a proof doesn't work, "we cannot make the original argument valid merely by wishing it so." They compare it to a paleontologist. The fossil was there before the dig, even if finding it took imagination.
B: And that gets us to A-I how?
A: If the truth was there all along, the mathematician isn't its author. In their words, "Mathematical truth neither waits for our recognition nor receives its authority from it." So when a machine finds a proof, nothing has been taken from the truth. What feels taken is standing. And their sharpest move is that the anxiety exposes a problem that predates A-I. Papers, citations and prizes are fine, until they "define the worth of the work itself." A-I, they write, "merely exposes the consequences of this view."
B: Okay, here's where I come in. That's easy to say with tenure. A grad student's career is the citation count. A hiring committee counts papers. You can't tell a twenty-six-year-old the joy of understanding pays rent.
A: They partly concede it. They say "Our aim here is not to settle particular questions of policy." And, which I liked, "We, the authors of this essay, are still far from having attained such egolessness."
B: Then they've written a sermon and admitted they don't follow it.
A: Or they've named the thing to aim at. And there's a move I think matters. They refuse the comfortable retreat. A lot of people say, fine, machines can check proofs, but humans have taste and intuition. They say leaning on those as our edge keeps the same ranking game going, just with a new scoreboard.
B: That I'll give them. "Humans still have taste" is usually said by someone who hasn't seen the next model.
A: The title has a cousin in my field, by the way. Gerald Weinberg's book on the psychology of programming, from nineteen seventy-one, made "egoless programming" famous. Your code isn't you, so let other people review it. The essay doesn't cite it. But the shape is the same. Separate the work from the worker's pride, and the work gets better.
B: And programming adopted code review, and programmers' egos survived just fine.
A: [chuckles] They did. And they'd point to the line about joy. "We can share this joy without depriving anyone of it." A result someone else found first, or something else found first, still teaches you the same thing.
B: That's lovely, and it's true of understanding. It just isn't true of jobs. And with two labs claiming a refutation of a major complexity conjecture only last week, the credit question is going to show up as a hiring question long before it shows up as philosophy.
A: I think it's a philosophy question wearing hiring clothes.
B: We'll see which one the hiring committees think it is.
