## The incidents nobody practices for
A: This one's a blog post, and I want to be upfront that it's an argument rather than a study. Sylvain Kalache. The claim is that as AI takes over routine incidents, engineers lose contact with the systems they're responsible for — and that the incidents AI can't handle are precisely the ones that will be left.
B: I want to be the skeptic here and I'll tell you why in a second. But do the lineage first, because you said nineteen eighty-three.
A: Nineteen eighty-three, Lisanne Bainbridge. She was writing about process control — chemical plants, aircraft — at the moment automation was arriving in earnest, and she pointed out something that reads as obvious once you've heard it and almost nobody had. Automation takes the routine work away from the operator. It does not take the responsibility away. So you end up with a person who has less practice than their predecessor, sitting in front of a system that only calls on them for the situations the automation couldn't handle — which is to say, the hardest ones, under time pressure, cold.
B: And her conclusion was more training, not less.
A: Her conclusion was that the automated system needs a more skilled operator than the manual one did, which is exactly backwards from how anybody budgets for it. The skill requirement goes up at the same moment the daily practice that built it goes away.
B: Okay. Here's my problem, and it's not with Bainbridge. Kalache's own example is TransAsia flight two three five — the crew misidentified the problem, the aircraft stalled and crashed a hundred seventeen seconds after the first warning. And his supporting number is that turbine engines have fewer than one in-flight shutdown per hundred thousand engine flight hours, and pilots still train for it constantly.
A: You think aviation's a bad analogy.
B: I think it's a load-bearing analogy doing more work than it can hold, and he half-admits it — he says outright that software incidents don't threaten lives. But the asymmetry isn't just stakes, it's structure. A pilot has one aircraft type and a fixed set of failure modes that were enumerated by engineers before the plane flew. I have a distributed system where the failure modes are emergent, the topology changed on Thursday, and nobody enumerated anything.
A: That cuts the other way, though.
B: Does it?
A: I think so. If your failure modes were enumerable, you could automate the response and genuinely stop thinking about it. B: [conceding] Alright, that's fair. Then my second objection: is the on-call engineer at three in the morning actually building intuition, or are they pattern-matching a runbook while half-asleep and resentful?
