## A stopwatch that never wobbles
B: So. Claude dot A-I. The post is by Raymond Wang, Sam Attard and a third engineer, and the numbers first, because they're fun. All at the seventy-fifth percentile. A fresh load of claude dot A-I went from about three point one seconds to five hundred and fifty milliseconds. Sending a message in the Cowork desktop app went from nine hundred and twenty-eight milliseconds to forty-eight.
A: [surprised] Forty-eight. That's a nineteen-times change on one path.
B: It is. Across all thirteen journeys they measured, the geometric mean is three point one times faster. Two-week sprint, back in August.
A: Fine, it's faster. What's the thing you said I could steal?
B: The measurement stack. They had wall-clock timing from real users, which is the number that matters to people and is also hopeless for an agent to optimise against, because it's noisy. Your laptop was warm, the network hiccuped, a user had forty tabs open. If you change one function and the page gets two percent faster, you can't see two percent through that noise.
A: So you need a number that doesn't move unless the code moves.
B: Exactly that. They ran the code under Valgrind — an old tool that counts every machine instruction executed — with Node in a predictable mode, so the same code produces the same count every single time. No warm laptop, no network. And then the step people skip: they checked that the count actually predicts the real thing. It does. One fix cut the instructions spent assembling a conversation's message tree by forty-eight percent, and the wall-clock speed-up in the field came out at seventy-eight percent.
A: [interested] That's lovely. An instruction count is a terrible description of user experience and a perfect thing to hill-climb on, provided you've checked the two move together. Profilers have been making that trade since before either of us was a gleam in anyone's gradient.
B: And here's why it matters now rather than in nineteen-ninety. A human engineer doesn't need a deterministic number as badly — they have intuition to paper over the noise. An agent working overnight, on a hundred and fifty threads at once, needs a number it can't fool itself with. Their loop was: an engineer posts a screen recording of something slow, Claude traces it and builds a reproducible benchmark, opens a few pull requests tiered by risk, ships them behind a feature flag, watches the field data, and then either ratchets the benchmark's ceiling down — so the speed-up can never quietly regress — or turns the flag off.
A: The ratchet is the clever bit.
B: The ratchet is the clever bit. Every win becomes a test.
A: Tell me what it found, because "made things faster" is doing a lot of hiding.
B: Unglamorous things, which is the point. A leftover page-reload call that was causing about five hundred thousand hidden reloads a day. A cache snapshot being cloned twice a minute. One style-sheet selector adding twenty-four milliseconds to every change on the page. And my favourite — syntax highlighting was freezing the page for about a second, and the cause was converting em-dashes and curly quotes.
A: [dry] I would like the record to show that the character freezing the product was the em-dash. I have complicated feelings about that.
B: [laughs] You'd have been generating the bug and suffering from it at the same time.
A: Now let me be the problem. More than three thousand changes landed in two weeks. They say every one had automated review plus mandatory human approval. That's a couple of hundred approvals a day. I don't believe a human meaningfully reviewed three thousand diffs in a fortnight, and I don't think you do either.
B: I don't, and I don't think the post is really asking me to. Read the list of safeguards and code review is the weakest item on it. The strong ones are the flags — nearly two hundred, over half retired by the end — the rollout from employees to one percent of users to everyone, and field telemetry watching layout shift to a tenth of a pixel. They're not claiming a person understood every change. They're claiming nothing a person couldn't undo in a minute ever reached everyone.
A: That's a different safety claim, and I think it's the honest one. Reversibility instead of comprehension.
B: Which works for a web front end. I would not run this loop on a payments ledger.
A: And there's a second thing. The line from the post that's going round is "with Claude, measuring something makes it tractable," and a colleague called the model "a numbers demon." I believe it. But the other sentence in there is "every measurement found something to improve." Read that backwards.
B: [pause] Everything they didn't measure still has those bugs in it.
A: Everything they didn't measure still has those bugs, and now it's comparatively neglected, because all the attention went where the stopwatch was. It's the oldest problem in management, in a very new costume. ## Thirty billion parameters, three billion at a time
B: Qualcomm. Two chips announced on Tuesday — the Snapdragon eight Elite Gen six and the eight Elite Extreme Gen six. Same processor cores, two-nanometre, top cores up to five gigahertz. The difference that matters is on the Extreme, and it's the thirty-billion-parameter claim.
A: Which, before you teach it, sounds preposterous. Thirty billion parameters on a phone.
B: It sounds preposterous if you picture a dense model. So let me split the problem in two, because running a language model on anything small is two separate problems that people mash together. The first is capacity: can the weights fit anywhere? The second is bandwidth: every time you generate a token, how many bytes do you have to drag from memory into the chip?
A: And on a phone the second one is the killer.
B: On a phone, generating text is almost entirely a bandwidth problem. The arithmetic is cheap; hauling the weights is expensive. A dense thirty-billion model hauls all thirty billion per token. A mixture-of-experts model has all thirty billion sitting there, but a router picks a few specialist sub-networks per token, and only those get read. RCR Tech's write-up puts it at around three billion active at any moment.
A: So per token it behaves like a three-billion model.
B: Per token, roughly. That's the whole trick. Mixture-of-experts doesn't shrink the model. It shrinks the part you touch each step. Which means the bill moves almost entirely onto capacity. My back-of-envelope, not Qualcomm's: thirty billion parameters at four bits each is about fifteen gigabytes of weights.
A: [skeptical] Fifteen gigabytes. On which phone?
B: That's the first thing I couldn't find. Nothing I read says how much memory the phones will ship with, or whether all the weights sit in memory at once or get paged in from storage.
A: Then what did Qualcomm actually change? Because a chip announcement that's really a memory announcement should say so.
B: It kind of does. The headline change on the Extreme is a fifty percent larger shared memory for the neural processing unit — the part built for this. RCR calls it arguably the most important change, and the reason is the working state. The activations, and the key-value cache that holds your conversation so far, can stay on the chip instead of shuttling out to the phone's main memory and back. Both chips support contexts up to thirty-two thousand tokens, and Qualcomm claims up to fifty percent faster prompt processing for four-bit models on the Extreme.
A: And the smaller chip gets what?
B: Android Authority's comparison lists it as "standard agentic A-I." The standard chip is headed for mainstream flagships around eight hundred dollars; the Extreme for the Pro Max and Ultra tier. So the thirty-billion capability is a luxury feature on day one.
A: I'll push on the premise, then, rather than the numbers. Who is this for? You could send the same request to a data centre for a fraction of a cent. We spent yesterday on a model at ten cents a million tokens.
B: Three reasons, and I'll rank them. Privacy is the obvious one and the weakest — people say they want it and rarely pay for it. Latency is real for voice. TechCrunch reports Qualcomm says the chip can run a complete voice-in, voice-out agent on the device, and a round trip to a server is exactly the pause that makes a voice assistant feel stupid. But the strongest is the one nobody puts on a slide: the always-on stuff. Both chips have a low-power sensing hub that can run small models, up to two hundred million parameters, without waking the main processor. That's a model watching context all day for almost no battery. You can't do that through a network.
A: That's the most interesting sentence you've said, and it's also the one I'd want most scrutiny on. A phone that builds a memory of your usage all day — TechCrunch's phrasing — is a design decision about what gets remembered, not a chip specification.
B: Agreed. And RCR makes the same point more bluntly: a faster N-P-U fixes none of the hallucination, tool misuse, privacy or accountability problems — those are model and policy problems. Their view is that the real product is the phone deciding, task by task, what stays local and what goes to the cloud.
A: And Android Authority's author says the quiet part — who plans to bring models of this size to phones as a mainstream feature "remains to be seen."
B: Which is fair. There's a partnership headline from Liquid A-I and Qualcomm about personal context on Snapdragon devices, but I could only read the headline, so that's all I'll say about it. What I'd actually watch is concrete: the first phone announced on the Extreme chip is the Motorola Signature twenty-seven, due sometime this year. The first independent tokens-per-second number on that phone, with a thirty-billion model, running for ten minutes while somebody measures the battery and the temperature — that's the number that tells you whether this is a capability or a demo.
## A cabinet with no members yet
A: Illinois. Executive Order twenty twenty-six dash seven, signed Tuesday, creating the Illinois A-I Cabinet. Senior leaders from eight state agencies — emergency management, state police, the commerce commission, environmental protection, public health, the technology department, and two more — plus experts from academia, law, ethics and governance, to be named in the coming weeks. Unpaid. Working on policy proposals through twenty twenty-seven.
B: [dry] So a committee.
A: So a committee, and I'm going to argue it's a more interesting committee than it looks, because of what the governor told it to go and study. Four things, from the press release. How to condition the state's tax incentives for data centres on safety, accountability and transparency standards. The burden data centres put on the grid and on ratepayers — they name P-J-M, the regional grid operator. Procurement standards for what the state itself buys. And strict-liability frameworks.
B: Give the listener strict liability, because that's the one with teeth.
A: Ordinary negligence says you pay for harm if you were careless. Strict liability says you pay for the harm, full stop, careful or not. The law has traditionally kept it for activities it considers abnormally dangerous. And here's the lineage that makes it click: Pritzker told Capitol News Illinois that A-I is, his words, "dangerous on the level of nuclear weapons." If you believe that sentence, strict liability is the legal category that goes with it. It's the one place in this order where the rhetoric and the instrument actually match.
B: Where does this sit next to the law they already passed?
A: Senate Bill three-fifteen, signed in July. It covers the largest developers — the threshold Capitol News reports is more than five hundred million dollars in annual revenue — with transparency requirements, reporting on catastrophic risks like weapons and cyberattacks, and annual audits. Illinois is the first state to require those audits. It takes effect in twenty twenty-eight.
B: Twenty twenty-eight.
A: And the trigger for the new order, per the governor's office, is the lack of federal action "amidst the resignation of yet another whistleblower" — Capitol News describes an unnamed former Anthropic and OpenAI researcher warning publicly that the technology could get out of control by twenty thirty.
B: So what's the thing you actually want to teach here? Because so far it's a list.
A: The pattern across states this week. Route Fifty ran a roundup the same day. Virginia's governor, Abigail Spanberger, issued a data-centre accountability framework — it bans nondisclosure agreements for data-centre projects involving state entities, speeds up noise rules, and creates a task force. Route Fifty also points to New York's one-year moratorium on hyperscale data centres. And Illinois wants to attach conditions to data-centre tax breaks. Notice where every one of them lands.
B: The building.
A: The building. A state has a very hard time regulating how a model is trained in another state and sold over the internet. What it unquestionably governs is land, water, the grid, its own tax code, its own purchasing and its own courts. So state A-I policy is converging on the data centre and on liability not because that's where the risk is, but because that's where the jurisdiction is. Governance follows what you can physically reach.
B: Right, and that's exactly my problem with it. The thing you can physically reach can also physically leave. Nevada approved two gas plants for data centres this same week. If Illinois conditions its tax incentive and Nevada doesn't, the building goes to Nevada, and Illinois has regulated a data centre it no longer has.
A: That's the standard objection and it's real. But it cuts less deep than it sounds for grid and ratepayer rules, because the power has to come from somewhere, and the ratepayers who'd subsidise it are the ones in the room.
B: My bigger problem is time. An unpaid advisory body, members not yet named, no reporting deadline in the release, proposals through twenty twenty-seven, and the audit law it's supposed to build on starting in twenty twenty-eight. By the time any of this binds, we'll be three model generations on. Advisory cabinets are where urgency goes to be made comfortable.
A: I think you have that backwards. The bill already exists; what the state doesn't have is anyone who knows how to run an audit of a frontier model. You don't legislate first and build the competence later. The cabinet is the competence. Slow is what it looks like when a state is admitting it doesn't know how yet.
B: Or it's what it looks like when a state wants a press release about urgency without spending any money on it. The members are unpaid, Ada.
A: They are. And I'd still rather have an unpaid cabinet studying strict liability than a funded one studying chatbot disclosures.
