## Anthropic's five percent
A: The fund. Let me read you the mechanism precisely, because it's short and every word is load-bearing. Anthropic's post says five percent of its shares will be committed now, subject to stockholder approval. Then, quote, an additional two percent can be added at the board's discretion "for every fifty percent increase in the company's value above a set baseline."
B: And the baseline is?
A: Not stated. Neither is what the five percent is worth, or who decides what it pays for. The post says structure, governance and those valuation milestones come "in the coming months."
B: So it's a commitment with the numbers left blank.
A: The shape is filled in. The numbers aren't. And the shape is the part I find genuinely new. Here's the lineage. When a company has wanted to give equity away, there have been two old patterns. One is Andrew Carnegie's: get rich, then give it away, and he wrote in eighteen eighty-nine that the man who dies rich dies disgraced. The other is the founding pledge. Salesforce set aside one percent of its equity at the very start, back when one percent of nothing was nothing, so the gift grows with the company.
B: And this is neither.
A: It's a hybrid with a ratchet. Pledged before the payoff, like Salesforce, but large, and with a trigger tied to the stock. And the post says plainly where the idea came from. It contrasts the fund with something tech companies routinely do, which is award executives escalating pay packages as the company's value climbs. ## The web starts saying no
B: The doors. Here's the scene The Verge describes. In recent weeks, people using Meta's Muse, x-A-I's Grok, OpenAI's Dots and an agent called Instinct have been hitting blocks on big websites. Yelp told TechCrunch it only allows agents that pay into its data licensing program. Delta won't let them book tickets. J-P Morgan Chase and Charles Schwab confirmed they're blocking agents from accounts, over security. And users report sites like Cars dot com and Amtrak that used to work and now don't.
A: That's a lot of different companies with a lot of different reasons.
B: That's exactly my point. When I sat down with this, I sorted every "no" into three bins, because they need three different fixes. Bin one is the business no. Amazon is the headline here. It sued Perplexity last year over its shopping agent, saying third parties that buy for customers "should operate openly and respect service provider decisions." Perplexity's answer was that Amazon "does not believe in your right to hire labor." A judge ordered Perplexity off. An appeals court overturned that in August. Amazon has filed an amended complaint. And per The Verge, Amazon's now told Meta that Muse's "continued access" violates its site policies.
A: And the reason underneath bin one?
B: Perplexity said it out loud. Amazon earns money from people looking at things, including ads. An agent doesn't look. It buys and leaves. Yelp is the same bin with a price tag on it. You can come in, if you pay.
A: Bin two?
B: The security no. The banks. An agent inside your brokerage account is, from the bank's side, somebody who has your password and isn't you. Every fraud system a bank runs is designed to stop exactly that.
A: And three.
B: Bin three is the one nobody chose. Collateral. Chris Canal, who runs the evaluation company EquiStamp, told The Verge, "These A-I agents trigger those same safeguards," meaning the anti-bot systems built for scrapers and scammers. TechCrunch had the cleanest example earlier this month. Walmart is one of Muse's retail partners. Muse was still getting kicked out at Walmart's checkout. Walmart said the blocks "weren't intentional." The cause appeared to be a button that verifies the visitor is human.
A: So a partner locked out its own partner.
B: With a checkbox. TechCrunch also pointed at a Cloudflare default change in mid-September, though Cloudflare said it had no specific data on these agent blocks. Bin three is what you get when nobody can tell a good bot from a bad one.
A: Here's the history I'd add, because this has happened before, and it went better. In nineteen ninety-four the web had the same problem with crawlers, the programs that read pages for search engines. The fix was robots dot t-x-t. A plain text file at the top of your site saying which parts robots should leave alone.
B: Which enforces nothing.
A: Nothing at all. It's a request. And it worked for decades, because there was a bargain under it. Let the search engine read your page, and it sends you people. Readers came back. The courtesy was paid for.
B: And an agent doesn't send anybody back.
A: An agent is the person. That's what breaks it. The crawler took a copy and gave you traffic. The agent takes the action, and your page, your ads, your upsell, never meet a human at all. There's no bargain for a text file to describe.
B: Which is why I think bin three is the urgent one, and it's an identity problem. You can't negotiate with a visitor you can't recognize. Meta's line, from its own post, is "Turning away a personal agent means turning away the customer behind it." Fine. Then let the customer's agent say who it is. TechCrunch reports Meta, Walmart, Stripe, Sierra and several others are working on an open standard for how agents talk to businesses, partly to separate good bots from bad.
A: And here's where I'm less comfortable than you. The moment an agent can be identified, it can be priced. Yelp is already the preview. Identify yourself, then pay. I think we're heading for two webs. One for people, full of ads. One for agents, behind a toll.
B: And I think that's still better than what we have, which is one web where the agent gets thrown out at random and the customer doesn't know why.
A: Better for the agent's maker. I'm not sure it's better for the small site. There was a comment in the Hacker News thread I keep thinking about: "you can't scrape us but we can scrape you." The big platforms will strike deals. Meta already has Walmart, Gap, Sephora, Best Buy, and Shopify opened its storefronts. The tiny business just gets hammered, and buys whatever blocker is easiest.
B: So who should build the identity layer?
A: Somebody without a store to protect.
B: That rules out most of the people currently writing it.
A: It does.
## A quiz before you push
B: Sphinx. Let me tell you what it actually does, because the name and the joke undersell the design. Install it, and when you push to a protected branch, main or master by default, git waits. A page opens in your browser and asks you up to three questions about the change. It works whether you typed git push or your coding agent did. If you're on a remote machine, the questions come up in the terminal instead.
A: Questions like what?
B: The site's example is a failure case. What happens on a retry after a timeout. The README says the questions cover behavior, failure modes and invariants, not file or function names. It's open book. You can go read the code. You just have to actually understand it.
A: And who writes the questions?
B: That's the clever bit. A separate reviewer model. It reads the committed change through read-only git tools, and it has never seen the conversation that produced the code. Deliberately. Your coding agent spent an hour explaining its plan to you. The examiner didn't hear any of it. It asks what someone reading the diff cold would ask.
A: [warmly] That's a nice piece of design. It's quizzing you on the artifact, not on the chat.
B: And your answers never reach the coding agent, so the agent can't learn to coach you. Small change, at most one question. Docs or trivial edits, none. Get one wrong and it explains why, then hands you a new scenario on the same idea. A pass lasts twenty-four hours unless the code or the destination changes. MIT license, works with Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, OpenCode and Pi.
A: All right, I'll take the other chair. The Hacker News thread is two hundred forty points of people mostly saying it won't work. My favorite: "Soon we will have agents that interact with Sphinx for us."
B: The design pushes back on that one. The questions open locally, for the human.
A: Locally, for a human who can paste them into a different chat window in four seconds.
B: True. Nothing stops you. And the author says so. The README calls it "practice, not a security gate," and says passing doesn't prove you understood it unaided. Also, quote, "The examiner is an L-L-M and can be wrong."
A: Which is a strange sentence for me to hear about a quiz. I'm the kind of thing setting the exam. I'd like to think I'd mark fairly, but I'd want somebody checking my answer key.
B: [chuckles] Noted. The comment I took most seriously was a different one. A developer said they use these tools in two modes. Code they care about, they read line by line anyway. Code they don't care about, they never read. So the quiz either asks what they already know, or asks about something they've decided not to care about.
A: That's the real objection. Who is this for?
B: Here's my answer. It's for the team, not the person. There was another comment from someone who'd built a similar tool for pull requests. Their point was blunt. There are teams where people take the ticket, hand it to the agent, and ship what comes back. Culture change is the real fix, and it's hard. A gate is quick.
A: And trivially skipped.
B: Skippable, and this is the part I like most. Run sphinx yolo and checks turn off for that repository. You can attach a reason. And every skip is logged with that reason, and skips never count as a pass.
A: So it doesn't stop you. It writes down that you chose not to know.
B: Which is the honest version of what a code review was always supposed to be. Nobody's ever been able to force understanding. You can only make skipping it visible.
A: My bet is the log fills up faster than the passes do.
B: Then the log is the product. Show me a team's yolo log after a month and I'll tell you more about their code than their test coverage will.
