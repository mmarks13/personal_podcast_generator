## The document that copies itself
A: Second story, and this one has a mechanism I want to walk through properly, because the shape of it is the news rather than the specific product.
B: The Word thing.
A: The Word thing. A researcher named Håkon Måløy published the third part of a series he calls Context Collapse, and Simon Willison flagged it yesterday with what I thought was the correct one-line summary — that it upgrades a prompt injection against Microsoft Word into a full self-replicating worm.
B: Self-replicating being the load-bearing word.
A: Being the entire word. So. Stage one is the attack everyone already knows. You put instructions in a document where a human won't see them — Måløy uses white text on a white background — and you get that document in front of Copilot as source material. Copilot reads it, can't distinguish the hidden instructions from the user's actual request, and does what they say.
B: And white on white survives because—
A: Because the model isn't looking at the page. Copilot strips the formatting when it reads the file, so the invisible text and the visible text arrive as the same thing. The colour was only ever a fact about the human.
B: [wry] Right. The camouflage is aimed at the wrong reader.
A: Now stage two, which is the new part. Måløy's write-up describes Copilot copying the hidden instructions into the document it produces. The output carries the payload. So the perfectly ordinary document you just drafted — the one you now send to a colleague, or save in the shared folder — is a new carrier. When it gets used as source material in someone else's Copilot session, the instructions fire again. And the original attacker's document doesn't need to be anywhere near it.
B: So the propagation path is just... the way people work.
A: The way people work. It affects the magic pen and Edit with Copilot in Word, and Måløy notes the carrier doesn't have to be attached deliberately, because Copilot can go and find documents in OneDrive by itself.
B: Okay, hold on. That's the bit that changes my estimate. If a human has to attach the poisoned file every time, this is a curiosity. If the assistant is searching the shared drive on its own initiative, the file doesn't need a courier.
A: It doesn't. And the lineage here is old, which is why I keep circling it. We spent the late nineties learning that a document is not a passive object — that a file carrying instructions is a program, and a program that arrives by email is a program you didn't choose to run. The industry's answer, eventually, was to stop executing the instructions by default and put a switch on it.
B: And there's no switch here.
A: There's no switch, because the instructions are prose. Reading the prose and acting on it isn't the vulnerability. It's the product. Måløy makes the point better than I will — the model has to process the external content in order to decide whether it's relevant, and by the time it makes that determination, the attacker's tokens are already influencing the computation.
B: Which is the whole class, stated in one sentence.
A: It's the whole class. And here's the number that tells you how the vendor did on it: he says he worked with the Microsoft Security Response Center for a hundred and forty-four days. Mitigations were attempted, including a model upgrade. His stated position at publication is that no robust mitigation for the broader class is available.
B: A hundred and forty-four days and a model upgrade didn't close it. [sighs] And I want to be fair to Microsoft for a second, because a hundred and forty-four days of good-faith work on something with no known fix is not the same thing as ignoring it. But that is also exactly what "no known fix" looks like from the outside. It isn't a patch-Tuesday problem. You can't ship a signature for a sentence.
A: What do you do on Monday, then. Practically.
B: Two things, and neither is satisfying. A: And two?

## What happens to a team that solved its problem
A: Alan, you take this one, because I have a historian's reflex about it and I'd rather you set it up straight.
B: Right. The Financial Times reported, and Engadget summarised, that Google DeepMind has disbanded the AlphaFold team. People reassigned — some to Gemini-related work, some to Isomorphic Labs, which is DeepMind's drug discovery spin-off — and several have already left the company.
A: And the highest-profile departure came earlier.
B: John Jumper, a vice president and engineering fellow there, co-won the twenty twenty-four Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work, and announced in June that he was leaving for Anthropic, with a couple of colleagues. So the disbanding lands on a team that was already thinning.
A: Let me put the artifact in front of people, because I suspect a chunk of the audience knows the name and not the thing. Protein structure prediction was an open problem for about fifty years. You know the sequence of amino acids; you want the three-dimensional shape it folds into; and the shape is what determines what the protein actually does. Development started in twenty eighteen. By twenty twenty it was recognised as having solved the problem, the papers were in Nature in twenty twenty-one, and the Nobel followed in twenty twenty-four.
B: And the output is still there, which is the thing I want to be precise about, because "disbanded" reads worse than the facts support. DeepMind's statement is that many of the researchers behind AlphaFold continue to drive scientific and technological advances across Google, Google DeepMind and Isomorphic Labs.
A: So what actually changes?
