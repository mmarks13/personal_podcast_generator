## Ataraxos and the Stratego bill
A: Okay. Defend the bill.
B: Start with why this game was the hold-out. Ars lays out the history in one paragraph. Deep Blue beat Kasparov at chess in ninety-seven. AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol in twenty sixteen. Poker bots have beaten professionals for years. Stratego held out, and DeepMind tried. Its system, DeepNash, came out in twenty twenty-two and couldn't reliably beat the best humans.
A: And the reason is the hidden information.
B: Right. Each side has forty pieces, from a marshal down to a spy, plus bombs and a flag. You can see where my pieces are. You can't see what they are. A piece's identity only shows when it fights. Gabriele Farina at MIT, one of the authors, gives the comparison. In Texas Hold'em you have two hidden cards, so there are thirteen hundred twenty-six possible hands. A machine can just weigh them all. In Stratego, the forty pieces can start in more than a decillion arrangements.
A: [short pause] That's a one followed by thirty-three zeros.
B: And the games are long. Farina says chess usually runs about forty moves, and a Stratego game can easily go two thousand. So you've got enormous hidden state, revealed in drips, over a very long horizon. Then bluffing. You move a weak piece like it's your marshal to scare someone off. Bluff too often and your threats mean nothing. Never bluff and you're predictable. The team says that balance is what beat DeepNash.
A: So what did they do differently?
B: Two things. The first is a training detail. Like DeepNash, Ataraxos learned purely by playing itself, a hundred and sixty-three million games. Moves that led to wins got played more, moves that led to losses got played less. Simple. But in hidden-information games, that kind of self-play tends to chase its own tail. You learn to counter a strategy, the opponent learns to counter that, round and round. Their fix was a schedule. Big, bold strategy changes early in training, small careful ones later.
A: Like annealing.
B: Same instinct. The second thing is the big one. Thinking ahead before each move. AlphaGo does a search just before it acts. DeepMind couldn't make search work in Stratego, because the space you'd search over is all those hidden arrangements. So Ataraxos has a second neural network, a belief model. It watches how your pieces have been moving and guesses what they are. Then, instead of enumerating every arrangement, it samples a handful of plausible ones, plays candidate moves forward in each, and picks the move that holds up across them.
A: [skeptical] Let me push on "belief." It's a network that predicts piece identities from movement. Calling that a belief is a little generous.
B: Fair, it's a learned guesser. But here's what it buys. Farina has a lovely line. "For humans, it's very hard when you know a secret to make decisions ignoring the fact that you know that secret. For machines, it's easy." Ataraxos has a weak spot, and it estimates you have no reason to suspect it. So it leaves the spot completely alone, even when to anyone who could see both sides it looks like a disaster waiting to happen.
A: Ha. That's a poker player's discipline, without the sweating.
B: And Eugene Vinitsky at NYU says they'd watch it "bluff" its way back from about a two percent chance of winning, "very, very casually." The name, Ataraxos, comes from the Greek for calm.
A: And the champion? How fair was the match?
B: Pretty fair. Niemeijer has four world championships and more than six hundred weeks ranked number one. Twenty online games over three weeks, a hundred dollars for each win. He knew the program wouldn't adapt to him, so he had time to hunt for a weakness. He won once. The team's position is that the loss isn't a flaw. You have to randomize your setup, so luck is always in it. Even a perfect strategy sometimes loses. And Vinitsky admits it cut both ways: "Sometimes we got lucky."
A: One human, twenty games. That's a thin sample to call it settled.
B: Which is why I like the second data set. At the twenty twenty-five Stratego World Championship, people who challenged it lost thirty-eight of forty. And it changed the metagame. Players were surprised how often it tucked its flag into a corner behind just two bombs, which is a rarely played setup.
A: Okay. Now the bill.
B: Here's the bill. DeepNash trained for two to three months on one thousand twenty-four of Google's specialized chips. The Ataraxos team estimates that would cost three to four and a half million dollars at twenty twenty-five prices. Ataraxos needed sixteen GPUs for a week, plus four GPUs for four days to train the belief model. And it played about thirty-four times fewer games than DeepNash and still ended up much stronger.
A: How? That's not a small gap.
B: The unglamorous answer. Farina and the lead author, Samuel Sokota, wrote a game simulator that runs millions of moves per second on the graphics cards themselves. Farina says it plainly: "At the scale that we are in academia, we don't really have access to an entire field of GPUs." When you can't buy the field, you make the environment fast. That's the lesson I'd hand any team doing reinforcement learning tonight. The simulator is the product.
A: I'll grant that. Here's where I'm less comfortable. The authors want to take this past games. Vinitsky's example is war gaming. "You can use the techniques that were derived here to play it forward and see how a strong opponent might respond." And in the same article, Farina says Ataraxos can't explain why it makes its moves. "I think we're not quite there yet."
B: Those two go together, though. A war game is a simulator you play against to learn something. It doesn't need to explain itself to do that.
A: It does the moment someone acts on what it suggests. A strong opponent you can't interrogate is a fine sparring partner and a terrible advisor.
B: [chuckles] I'd still want the sparring partner.
A: So would I. I just want to know who's allowed to take its advice.
## Google's TPU in orbit, and eighteen hundred Starships
B: Your satellite. Start with what actually flew.
A: One satellite, on a standard Planet Labs platform, on a SpaceX launch from California carrying more than a hundred payloads. The job, per TechCrunch, is to prove a TPU can work up there. That means supplying a kilowatt of continuous power, cooling the chip, and running models through their paces to see what goes wrong. Once it's commissioned, it'll run the TPU in fifteen-minute bursts, so it doesn't overwhelm the satellite's power and heat systems.
B: [skeptical] Fifteen-minute bursts. So it's a data center that has to take breaks.
A: It's a test article, and Google says so. Travis Beals, who runs the effort, which is called Project Suncatcher: "We've done testing on the ground, but you know, there's no test that's completely as good as the real thing." Next year Google and Planet plan two purpose-built satellites that try to work together over a laser link. Worth knowing: Google's research blog last November talked about two prototypes by early twenty twenty-seven. What flew this week is one, on an off-the-shelf bus. The pair is still next year.
B: Okay. Why bother at all?
A: Power. In last year's design post, Google said solar panels in the right orbit can be up to eight times more productive than on Earth, and produce power nearly continuously. The orbit is a dawn-dusk sun-synchronous one, riding the line between day and night so the panels almost never go dark. The sketch is eighty-one satellites flying in close formation, a cluster about a kilometer across, with neighbors a hundred to two hundred meters apart.
B: That close? Why?
A: Because the chips have to talk. Beals says the bandwidth and latency between TPUs "really, really matters" for multi-rack work. Google's post said the links need tens of terabits per second, and a bench demonstrator hit one point six terabits total with one transceiver pair. Formation flying is how you buy bandwidth without wires.
B: And radiation. That's the one I'd lose sleep over.
A: There's an update there. Google realized its earlier particle-accelerator tests were too kind. The way the chips were set up gave them more shielding than they'd actually have in orbit. They redid the tests, saw slightly more errors in the logic circuitry, and say they're still confident for inference over a satellite's five-year life. Beals puts the inference error rate at roughly one in a million. And then he volunteers the limit himself. It was "already problematic for doing, say, some mega-scale training run where you're going to have many thousands of chips running for months."
B: So orbit is an inference story, not a training story.
A: By Google's own account. Now the part I really wanted. The launch math. The authors stress the paper isn't an economic feasibility study. But it does model launch cost, and it uses a learning curve. Do you know where that idea comes from?
B: Airplanes, I think?
A: Airplanes. Nineteen thirty-six, Theodore Wright, an engineer at Curtiss-Wright, studying aircraft production. He found that every time total production doubled, the labor needed to build the next plane fell by about twenty percent. The key word is "total." The savings come from cumulative experience. Not from calendar time. Prices don't fall because years pass. They fall because you've built more.
B: Oh, I see where this goes.
A: Right. The authors argue SpaceX has been on a price-cutting learning curve since Falcon One. TechCrunch describes it as about twenty percent a year. They say it's reasonable to expect launch prices close to two hundred dollars a kilogram by twenty thirty-five. Google's design post said that's roughly the price where an orbital data center starts to compare with a ground data center's energy bill. But if the savings come from experience, you can't just wait for two hundred dollars. You have to earn it by flying. So the paper turns the price target into payload. Going by Falcon Nine's history, Starship would need to put about three hundred seventy thousand tons into orbit. At two hundred tons a flight, that's about eighteen hundred launches over ten years. A hundred and eighty a year.
B: And Starship has never flown more than five times in a year.
A: TechCrunch's line exactly. Musk has suggested hourly flights by twenty twenty-nine. TechCrunch's response is "Musk says a lot of things."
B: So the plan works if Starship flies three or four times a week, every week, for a decade, carrying a full load each time. That's not a forecast. That's a conditional.
A: Yes, and I'd defend the paper for stating it so clearly. A price per kilo sounds like weather that just arrives. Writing it as a launch count shows you the thing you'd have to believe. Also worth saying: TechCrunch notes Google is a major SpaceX investor. One more small thing. The headline first said sixteen hundred launches. TechCrunch corrected it to eighteen hundred.
B: Here's my builder objection, though. Every launch is a bet on the hardware you're launching. A ground data center swaps out a dead rack on a Tuesday. In orbit, a TPU generation lives for the satellite's five years, while Google ships new chips on the ground the whole time.
A: Which Beals is at least looking at. He says they're designing for where the workloads will be in five years, not where they are today.
B: That's a hope, not an answer to the refresh problem.
A: Agreed. Watch next year's pair. If two satellites can share a real workload over a laser link, the formation idea is real. If they can't, the eighteen hundred launches don't matter.
## Ten years of "AI will never"

Yes, not sure, or no. Voting has closed. When I pulled the results there were about a hundred thousand votes. Thirty-nine thousand yes, twenty-six and a half thousand not sure, thirty-five thousand no.

A hundred and six yes, six not sure, twelve no. Here's one from twenty seventeen: A-I develops software from completely open-ended natural-language requests. A hundred and three yes. Steven Pinker's version, from nineteen ninety-four: "the hard problems are easy and the easy problems are hard."
