## Bengio's case for leaving the labs
A: So, Bengio. Let me start with how the letter opens, because the tone is the argument. It's a confession. He writes that for a long time he minimized his colleagues' warnings because he couldn't face that his life's work could do harm. B: Strong opening for an essay that's going to tell other people to change jobs.
A: That's exactly the move. He's saying, I know what staying feels like from the inside. I did it for years. Then he puts it into history. Last week he briefed the United Nations Security Council on recent A-I incidents. And he reaches back to nineteen forty-six, when the United States came to a year-old U-N and offered to put the atomic bomb under international control. Bernard Baruch told the delegates, we are here to make a choice between the quick and the dead. ## A seventy-six times cheaper agent, mostly from a cache
B: So, from the existential to the very much not. StackAI is Asana's agent workflow platform. One of its products is a browser agent. You give it a job, it drives a browser, reads pages, takes screenshots and puts together an answer. Frank Hidalgo, StackAI's chief technology officer, wrote up how they cut its cost seventy-six times.
A: And you said OpenAI's version isn't the real story.
B: Not the whole story. OpenAI's headline is that Asana did it using GPT-6 Astra in Codex. Astra was the engineer. It audited the code, instrumented every request, ran the experiments and read the traces. The agent itself ran on other models. And the savings, per Asana's own post, come mostly from one mechanism. Prompt caching.
A: We've touched this. Cache hit rate as the real lever on an agent's bill.
B: Back in September, yes. This is the case study that shows why, so let me do the mechanism properly. A browser agent is stateless between calls. On every step it resends everything: the tool definitions, the system prompt, and the whole growing history of page text and screenshots. Step forty is enormous, and almost all of it is a copy of step thirty-nine.
A: So you pay for the same history again and again.
B: Unless the provider caches it. Asana's post says on the models they tested, cache reads cost five to ten percent of the normal input price. But here's the catch that matters. A cache only reuses the longest unchanged prefix of a request. It matches from the very first token forward, and the moment anything differs, everything after that point is billed fresh.
A: Oh. So an edit near the start of the history—
B: —kills the cache for everything that follows it. And that's what their agent did on every step. It removed the previous screenshot to save space, and it trimmed older text to fit a history budget of a hundred and twenty thousand characters. Each tidy little edit changed an earlier part of the request. Their phrase: reuse broke on almost every call.
A: That's very funny. The cost-saving housekeeping was the cost.
B: [laughs] It was. Every step it threw the cache away to save money. The fix had two parts. First, cache the history, with a cache marker on the latest tool result. Second, prune in batches instead of every step. They keep up to twenty screenshots, then cut back to one, so about nineteen calls in a row reuse the same cached history. And they raised the budget to four hundred and eighty thousand characters, so old text stopped getting trimmed at all.
A: Let me do my job. How big was the test? A seventy-six from one lucky run means nothing.
B: A hundred and forty-four runs, plus a twelve-run follow-up. Six caching and history policies, two budgets, four models, three runs per condition, every answer scored against an independently prepared reference. And they're careful about it. They say with three or four runs per condition, this shows broad patterns, not differences of a few percent.
A: And the four models?
B: That's where I'd squint at the headline. GPT-6.1 Sol, plus three anonymized ones. Model A is a smaller, lower-cost model from another frontier lab. Model B is the model originally in production, from that same lab. Model C is a newer version of B.
A: So the seventy-six is—
B: Optimized Sol against the original production setup on Model B. That mixes two changes, the engineering and the model switch. If you want like for like, Model B with the fixes alone came out twenty-nine times cheaper and four times faster.
A: Twenty-nine is still enormous.
B: It's the real headline. And it's the part you can take to any provider. On Sol, the optimized agent read eighty-nine percent of its input from cache. And they say every best-condition run cost less than every baseline run, on every model.
A: Then what's the catch? There's always a catch.
B: The best line in the post, honestly. Caching alone is not enough. Without batch pruning, caching the history at the bigger budget cost more than not caching on three of the four models. The cache was continually rewritten and rarely read. Writing to a cache costs money too. Turn caching on with the wrong pruning and you pay a premium for nothing.
A: So the bill depends on whether your history-management code and your provider's cache agree with each other.
B: Exactly. And none of that shows up on a price sheet. That's why two teams on the same model at the same list price can see bills an order of magnitude apart. And I'll confess something. After reading this I went through my own logging proxy. My little home harness drops old tool output every turn to keep the context tidy.
A: Oh no.
B: My cache-read share is embarrassing. I've been paying full price to be neat.
A: And the Codex part? A week instead of months is the line OpenAI wants people to remember.
B: Hidalgo's quote is that he'd never have done it by hand because it would likely have taken months, and with Codex it took about a week. He'd set a goal before bed and review results in the morning. And his other line is the one I'd keep: shipping speed is no longer the bottleneck. Human attention is.
A: Odd line for a case study about saving money.
B: It is. They also warn that limits still matter. A drifting agent grows toward the context limit, and if the cache breaks, every call pays full price. So here's what I'd do tonight if you run an agent. Open the bill. Find the share of input tokens read from cache. If it's low, the problem isn't your model choice. Something early in your prompt is changing every step, and you should go find it.
A: A date stamp in the system prompt.
B: A date stamp in the system prompt. I've seen that one kill a cache too.
