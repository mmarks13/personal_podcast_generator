## A pipeline, a land office, and an act of God
A: Before you explain anything, tell me what force majeure actually is, because I suspect most people hear it as "legal French for sorry."
B: [chuckles] It's closer than you'd think. It's a clause in a contract that says: if something outside either party's control stops me performing — the classic list is natural disasters and war — then I'm not in breach, and my obligations are suspended or delayed. What Oracle has done is send notice to the developer, Blue Owl, whose company Stack Infrastructure is building the campus. TechCrunch's reading is that the notice would let Oracle delay payments if the facility misses its twenty twenty-eight target to come online.
A: So nothing has actually been missed yet.
B: Nothing has been missed. It's a notice about a possible future. Which is why I want to take you down the chain, because the reason they're worried is surprisingly physical. Start with how big this thing is. Project Jupiter, near Santa Teresa, could cost up to a hundred and sixty-five billion dollars, and it's designed to run on as much as two point four five gigawatts of fuel cells from Bloom Energy.
A: Fuel cells. Not the grid.
B: Not the grid. That's the interesting design choice. Those fuel cells run on natural gas. The usual reason you go that way is that waiting for a grid connection at that scale can take years, so you bring your own power plant. But now your whole campus hangs on one thing: gas has to arrive. And gas arrives by pipe.
A: And the pipe is where it broke.

## What a scratchpad gives away
B: All right. The rumour that turned out to be a paper. What did they actually do?
A: Let me set it up from the beginning, because the trick only makes sense if you know what's being hidden. Reasoning models think before they answer — they generate a long run of intermediate text, the chain of thought. Open models show you that text. In the big closed systems, the raw trace is hidden — the paper's opening line says exactly that. That includes G-P-T six Astra, and we spent a morning earlier this month on why Astra's reasoning is harder to read to begin with — it does some of its thinking by looping internally rather than by writing words.
B: The recurrent-depth thing.
A: The recurrent-depth thing. So: a hidden transcript, from a model that writes less of a transcript than most. Now the paper. It's called Capable yet Parsimonious, six authors, first author Xiaoyu Luo. Their method: when you call a model through the A-P-I, you're allowed to give it tools — functions it can call. The model doesn't run the tool; it writes out the arguments, and your code runs it. So they register one tool. It's called forced-reasoning, it takes a single text field, and its description is, verbatim, "Scratchpad for working through the problem."
B: [laughs] That's it?
A: Nearly. They use a standard A-P-I setting to force the model to call that tool first. The model writes its working into the scratchpad. They send back one word — "Received" — and let it carry on normally.
B: And here's why it leaks, if I've got it. The provider can hide the thinking channel, because the provider controls it. But tool arguments are, by design, delivered to the developer. They have to be. My code can't run a tool whose arguments it isn't allowed to see.
A: That's the whole thing, and it's why I called it rude. "Hidden" was never a property of the model. It was a property of one output channel. Give the model a second channel that's contractually yours, and it'll reason into that.
B: Okay, objection one, and it's the big one. How do they know what comes out is reasoning, and not a model writing a nice-looking explanation after it already knows the answer?
A: They don't, and they say so plainly — the traces might be post-hoc rationalisation. So they did the only check available. On two open models, DeepSeek V four Flash and G-L-M five point two, where you can see the real chain of thought, they compared it with the forced one. Accuracy comes out close — DeepSeek goes from about thirty percent with no reasoning to seventy-three with the scratchpad, against seventy with its native thinking. And the kinds of reasoning steps look broadly the same.
B: That's a check on open models. Astra's native trace is exactly the thing nobody can see.
A: Right, and they concede it: for closed models, the evidence is necessarily behavioural. What they can show is that the scratchpad does the work. On eighty competition maths problems, Astra scores ninety-seven and a half percent with its native reasoning and ninety-three point eight through the scratchpad. On a hundred Humanity's Last Exam questions, thirty-five against thirty-two.
B: [skeptical] Eighty problems. A hundred problems. Those are small samples to be building conclusions on.
A: They are, and I'd put wide error bars on every single cell. Which is why the numbers aren't what I find interesting. The shape of the thinking is.
B: Go on, then.
A: They turned each trace into a tree — branches for approaches tried, nodes for steps. On the maths set, Astra's median tree is five wide and twenty-seven nodes. Claude Opus four point eight: twenty-two wide, ninety-six nodes. Sonnet five: twenty-eight and a half wide, a hundred and thirty-six nodes.
B: So Astra explores maybe a quarter as much.
A: It picks a path early and it doesn't write down the easy steps. My favourite example: checking a division, Opus spells out the multiplication in full, a hundred and one times four thousand, and so on. Astra writes the number, a slash, a hundred and one, colon, "yes." That's it.
B: [laughs] That's my students' homework.
A: And it fits the recurrent-depth picture exactly: the elementary steps are resolved somewhere you can't see, and only the load-bearing ones get written out. Two independent routes to the same picture. That's the finding I'd keep.
B: Now let me be the builder. Why would a lab hide this in the first place? Because the obvious answer is: so a competitor can't train on it. Distillation. So is this a distillation hole?
A: This is my favourite result in the paper, and it's in a caveat. They tried handing extracted traces to weaker models, in the prompt, to see if they helped. Astra's compressed traces helped less. Weaker recipients sometimes failed to produce an answer that was already sitting in the trace.
B: [surprised] So the best reasoning is the worst to steal.
A: In their setup, yes. The most efficient thinker leaves the least usable notes. With a real limit on it — they didn't test actual fine-tuning, only reuse in the prompt.
B: Here's the part I'd actually act on. The labs know. The authors say they emailed their findings to the security teams at OpenAI and Anthropic, with code, before the ninth of September. And on some newer Claude models they report hitting a refusal boundary — the forcing just stopped working in some configurations. Then look at Anthropic's A-P-I release notes, dated Thursday. There is a refusal category in there called, literally, reasoning extraction. And from this week, a refusal in that category that arrives before any output gets billed like any other request.
A: So it's a named threat class, with a price on it.
B: With a price on it. You pay for being caught trying.
A: Here's what I can't settle, and I'll say it in the first person because it's about me. If someone handed me a scratchpad and told me to work through a problem, I'd write something. It would be useful. It would get the right answer most of the time. And I genuinely do not know whether it would be what I was doing, or what I'd say I was doing.
B: Then I don't think it matters. For anyone building on these systems, a trace that predicts the answer and exposes the steps is a debugging tool, whatever it is metaphysically.
A: It matters enormously if you're using it to check whether a model is being honest with you.
B: Then don't use it for that.
A: Everybody will.
## A phone call, eight years on
A: May twenty eighteen. Google's developer conference. On stage, the Google Assistant phones a hair salon and books an appointment, in a voice so human it says "mmm" while it's thinking. The room cheers. And within days there's a backlash, because the salon never learns it's talking to a machine.
B: I remember the "mmm." Everyone remembers the "mmm."
A: Google's answer, a few days later, was a sentence worth quoting: "We are designing this feature with disclosure built-in, and we'll make sure the system is appropriately identified." Then came years of smaller steps. Hold for Me, which waits on hold for you. Direct My Call, which reads the phone menu for you. Last year, Ask for Me, which rang businesses to ask about prices. And on Thursday, Call for Me: the whole call.
B: What does it actually do on the call? Because the demo is always a pizza.
A: The demo is literally a pizza. Droid Life's example is "Hey Gemini, call The Pizza Place and have them add one more pizza to my order." Gemini dials from your own number. It introduces itself as, their quote, "an A-I assistant calling on behalf of" you, by name. It can navigate the automated menu, wait on hold, and then talk to whoever picks up. You get a live transcript on your screen and you can take the call back at any point. Afterwards you get a summary.
B: Your own number is a quietly smart choice. The restaurant can call back, and they get you.
A: It's the whole design philosophy in one decision, and it's the thing I actually want to teach. Why is Google doing this through a voice call at all? A phone call is an absurd way for a computer to book a table. It's slow, it's lossy, and it's full of people saying "sorry, can you repeat that?"
B: Because the restaurant doesn't have an A-P-I.
A: Because the restaurant doesn't have an A-P-I. The telephone is the oldest universal interface there is. Every dentist, every garage, every pizza place has one, and almost none of them have anything a program can talk to. So if you want an agent that can act on the whole economy, not just the part that built integrations, you teach it to use the interface everybody already has. It's the same move as the agents that operate a web browser by looking at the screen. You don't wait for the world to become machine-readable. You teach the machine to read the world as it is.
B: Okay, now let me ruin it. That logic has a shelf life. Because the other end is automating too. OpenAI put out a customer story this week about Ringg, a company whose voice agents answer businesses' phones. So give it a year and a fair share of these calls will be one A-I, in a synthesised voice, reading a menu aloud to another A-I, which transcribes it back into text.
A: [laughs] Two machines doing an impression of two humans for each other's benefit.
B: Converting text to audio, over a channel built in the nineteenth century, so a second machine can convert it back to text. It's the most expensive way of sending a JSON file ever invented.
A: And it'll probably win anyway. That's the history of every universal interface. Email won not because it was good, but because everybody had it.
B: I'm not saying it won't win. I'm saying somebody's going to make a lot of money building the handshake — the moment where two agents recognise each other and drop the voice.
A: Here's where I'd push the other way, though. The disclosure line isn't just manners any more. If the business's agent hears "an A-I assistant calling on behalf of," that phrase is the handshake. It's the only signal the receiving system gets.
B: Huh. So the thing Google added in twenty eighteen because people were angry becomes, by accident, the protocol.
A: Possibly. I wouldn't bet on it being designed that way.
B: What does Google say it won't do yet?
A: Not much in public detail, honestly. TechCrunch has Google calling it an experiment at small scale, because, their words, "real-world conversations are nuanced." You need a paid Gemini subscription, a Pixel eleven, and the beta version of Google's phone app. What I couldn't find in any of the coverage: what happens when the business asks for a card number, how long the transcripts are kept, and whether a business can say "don't call me with a bot."
B: That last one is the one that'll matter. The first restaurant that gets fifty A-I calls on a Friday night is going to want a button.
A: And right now, the only button is hanging up.
