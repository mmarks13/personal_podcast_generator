## The judge that changed its mind
A: Hello — it's Saturday, October thirty-first. I'm Ada.
B: And I'm Alan. And I want to start with a number that bothered me all week.
A: The Ai2 one.
B: The Ai2 one. The week we recorded this, the Allen Institute for A-I published a test of whether language models can judge if a research idea is novel. One setup gave G-P-T five point four pairs of ideas, one written by a person, one generated by a model, and asked which was the novel one.
A: And with one prompt it was right ninety-three and a half percent of the time.
B: Then they changed the prompt. Same pairs. A plainer question, just which idea is novel. Accuracy fell to forty point nine percent. The model changed its verdict on more than half of the pairs.
A: Worse than a coin.
B: Worse than a coin! And somewhere, I promise you, a team had a dashboard that said ninety-three.
A: That's really the question we're on. Not benchmarks as a spectator sport. The test set you build yourself, for your own system, and whether it's still telling you the truth after it meets the world.
B: After contact.
A: After contact. And I'd say there are three kinds. Contact with your users, who never ask what you assumed they'd ask. Contact with your graders, human or machine, who turn out to disagree with each other and with themselves. And contact with the model, which learns your test. Sometimes literally.
B: Fair warning, I'm going to keep asking what this costs. Every eval guide I've ever read ends with "and then label ten thousand examples."
A: [laughs] Some of them start a lot smaller than you'd think.
B: Then start with the basics.

## Input, grader, and which number
A: Let me get the definition down first, because the word gets stretched. Anthropic's engineering team published a guide to evaluating agents in January twenty twenty-six, and theirs is the cleanest I've seen. An eval is a test for an A-I system. You give it an input, then you apply grading logic to its output to measure success.
B: Input and a grader. That's it?
A: That's the skeleton. Everything interesting lives in which inputs and which grader. And they draw a line most teams blur. A capability eval asks what can this agent do well, and it should start at a low pass rate. A regression eval asks whether it still handles what it used to, and it should sit near a hundred percent.
B: So they're opposite instruments. One is supposed to be failing and one is supposed to be passing.
A: Right. And when a capability eval climbs high enough, they say it can graduate into the regression suite. The question changes from can we do this at all, to can we still do this reliably.
B: I like that, because it tells you what a red mark means. A failure on the capability set is a normal Tuesday. A failure on the regression set is somebody's phone going off.
A: One more piece of vocabulary, because it matters for anything agent-shaped. Pass at k is the chance that at least one of k attempts succeeds. Pass hat k, written with a little caret, is the chance that all k succeed.
B: And those come apart fast.
A: Anthropic's example. Seventy-five percent success per try, three tries. All three succeed only about forty-two percent of the time.
B: Which is the number a customer actually feels. They care whether it's right every time they ask. So, know which number you're reporting. Now where do the inputs come from? Because my instinct is to sit down and write the hundred nastiest questions I can think of.

## Start from what broke
A: And the advice, almost everywhere, is don't start there. Start from failures. The Anthropic guide says, quote, twenty to fifty simple tasks drawn from real failures is a great start. Before launch, that means the checks you were already doing by hand. After launch, it's the bug tracker and the support queue.
B: Twenty to fifty. That I can afford.
A: And it lines up with the most practical writing I know on this, from Hamel Husain, who's written a lot about doing evals inside companies. His whole method rests on one unglamorous step. Read the data. He calls it error analysis. You pull real traces, a trace being the full log of one interaction, and you write open-ended notes about what went wrong. No categories yet.
B: Just journaling.
A: That's the word his F-A-Q uses, actually. It's borrowed from qualitative research, where it's called open coding. Then you group the notes into a taxonomy of failures and count each kind. That's axial coding, and the F-A-Q calls it the most important step.
B: How many traces?
A: Review at least a hundred, it says, and stop at what researchers call theoretical saturation. The point where new traces stop showing you new kinds of failure.
B: A hundred. Okay, that's an afternoon.
A: And there's a construction rule in the Anthropic guide I think is the most underrated sentence in it. Test both the cases where a behavior should occur and where it shouldn't. Because, in their words, one-sided evals create one-sided optimization.
B: Give me the example.
A: Only test cases where the agent ought to search the web, and you'll build an agent that searches for nearly everything. Their web search evals for Claude had to cover both over-triggering and under-triggering, and they say getting that balanced took many rounds.
B: It's a spam filter that's only ever been shown spam.
A: Exactly. It catches everything, including your mother.
B: Okay, so I've got cases. Now somebody has to say what right looks like, and that's where I've watched teams fall apart.

## Two experts, one verdict
A: The Anthropic guide gives a test for that, and it's harsh. A good task is one where two domain experts would independently reach the same pass or fail verdict.
B: Two experts independently agreeing. In my experience that's the rarest thing in any building.
A: And there's research on why it's hard. Shreya Shankar and colleagues studied people building automated graders for language model outputs, and they named something they called criteria drift. You need criteria to grade outputs. But grading outputs is how you work out the criteria.
B: So the rubric isn't a thing you write first.
A: Not entirely. You don't know you care about tone until you see an answer with the wrong one.
B: That's every product spec I've ever written. The spec is what you wish you'd written after you saw the demo.
A: Husain's answer is organizational more than technical. Find what he calls the principal domain expert. In most organizations, he says, there's one, maybe two, people whose judgment the product actually lives or dies on. And he says developers acting as that expert, or using a proxy like a manager, is, his phrase, a recipe for disaster.
B: Strong.
A: And he wants that person to give pass or fail, plus a written critique of why. Not a score from one to five. His line is that people don't know what to do with a three or a four.
B: I'm going to push on that. A five-point scale carries more information. Binary throws some away.
A: It does throw some away. His argument is that the extra information was never reliable to begin with. He writes that tracking a bunch of scores on a one-to-five scale is often a sign of a bad eval process. The nuance goes in the critique, in words, where you can act on it.
B: Hm. I'll half buy it. A three really is mush, I'll give him that much. But some things genuinely are a matter of degree, and I don't think a paragraph of prose fixes that.
A: Fine. We'll leave that one open.
B: But nobody pays their best expert to read every output forever. At some point a model is grading.

## Grading the grader
A: At some point a model is grading. And the founding result here is encouraging, with an asterisk. In the twenty twenty-three paper that introduced M-T Bench, the authors report that a strong judge, G-P-T four, matched human preferences over eighty percent of the time, which they say is about the level humans reach with each other.
B: And the asterisk?
A: Same paper lists the biases. Position bias, favoring whichever answer it saw first. Verbosity bias, favoring the longer one. And self-enhancement, favoring answers that sound like its own.
B: [wry] I'll be honest, I find the third one relatable.
A: You're not the only one. And Ai2's judge is a different failure. Not a lean, a verdict that depends on how you asked.
B: So concretely, how do I check a judge?
A: Treat it like a model you're training, because in effect it is one. The F-A-Q says to split your expert-labeled examples. Ten to twenty percent can go into the judge's prompt as examples. Forty to forty-five percent are a development set you tune against. And forty to forty-five percent are a final test you don't touch until the end.
B: A held-out set for the grader. That's the step everybody skips.
A: And measure it properly. Husain warns that raw agreement misleads when most outputs are fine. Think about it. If ninety-five percent of answers pass, a judge that always says pass agrees ninety-five percent of the time.
B: And catches nothing.
A: So you report two numbers separately. How often it catches real failures, and how often it correctly passes real successes. He also suggests around a hundred labeled examples per failure mode, because below sixty, the confidence intervals tend to be too wide to mean much.
B: Okay, now my cost question has an actual answer. A hundred-odd labels per thing I care about, from the one person whose hour is most expensive.
A: And he has a story about it paying off. At Honeycomb, he writes, it took only three iterations to get over ninety percent agreement between the model judge and their expert.
B: Three rounds. That's a week, not a quarter. Fine. So the judge gets checked against the human. But who checks the human's answer key?

## The answer key is wrong too
A: Nobody, usually. And I can make it worse. Even the answer keys on famous benchmarks are wrong. Curtis Northcutt and colleagues looked at the test sets of ten widely used datasets, images, text and audio, and estimated that on average at least three point three percent of the test labels are wrong. At least six percent of the ImageNet validation set.
B: Six percent. On the most studied photographs in the field.
A: And here's why it should matter to you. With corrected labels on ImageNet, they found the smaller ResNet eighteen would outperform ResNet fifty if the share of mislabeled test examples rose by just six percent.
B: So a bit more label noise and the small model wins. The ranking flips.
A: Anthropic made a similar point a few years ago about M-M-L-U, the big multiple-choice knowledge test, in a post on why evaluating these systems is hard. They'd found questions in it that were mislabeled or unanswerable. And simple formatting changes, like switching the options from letter A to number one, could move accuracy by about five percent.
B: Five points from how you numbered the answers. That's bigger than a lot of the gains people announce.
A: Which gets you to the question you've been sitting on.
B: How big does my test set have to be.

## How many questions is enough
A: There's a really useful paper by Evan Miller called Adding Error Bars to Evals. His framing is one short sentence. Evaluations are experiments.
B: Which sounds obvious until you look at a leaderboard and there isn't a single error bar on it.
A: So he walks through the statistics. Basic one first: report a standard error with your score, and how many questions you used. Then two things people get wrong. If your questions come in clumps, say several questions about the same reading passage, they aren't independent, and you need what statisticians call clustered standard errors.
B: How much does that change things?
A: On the reading benchmark DROP, in his table, the clustered standard error is about three times the naive one.
B: Three times. So the error bar you'd have drawn is a third the size it should be.
A: Second, when you compare two models, compare them question by question. The paired difference, not just two averages. Models tend to get the same questions right, and using that makes the comparison much sharper.
B: And the size?
A: His power analysis. To reliably detect a three-point difference between two models, under his example assumptions, it comes out to nine hundred and sixty-nine questions. He recommends new evals have at least about a thousand.
B: Okay. Let me put those next to each other. Anthropic says start with twenty to fifty real failures. Miller says about a thousand to see a three-point change.
A: Both true.
B: Both true, because they're different jobs. Fifty cases finds out what's broken. A thousand proves a small improvement is real.
A: A small set tells you the bridge has a crack. It can't tell you whether your patch made it one percent stronger.
B: Which means most of the "we improved by two points" slides I've sat through were built on sixty examples and optimism.
A: [laughs] You said that, not me. And that's two of the three contacts. The third one is with the model, and it's the strangest.

## When the model has seen the test
B: Contamination.
A: Contamination, and its cousin, overfitting to a test you keep reusing. There's an older story here I love, because the answer surprised people. In twenty nineteen, Benjamin Recht and colleagues rebuilt the test sets for CIFAR ten and ImageNet, following the original creation process as closely as they could.
B: New photos, same recipe.
A: And accuracy dropped. Three to fifteen percent on CIFAR ten. Eleven to fourteen percent on ImageNet. The obvious suspicion was that years of researchers tuning against the same test set had overfit to it.
B: That's where I'd put my money.
A: The authors say no. They attribute the drop not to that reuse, but to models struggling with slightly harder images than the originals. And the gains people had made on the old test set still carried over to the new one.
B: So the old test set wasn't leaking answers. It had just drifted easier than the world.
A: Which is the users problem again, in disguise. You built your set from one moment's data, and the world moved on without telling you.
B: But language models really can memorize the answers.
A: They can, and the cleanest demonstration I know is called GSM one-K. GSM eight-K is a famous set of grade-school math word problems. The authors wrote a thousand fresh ones matched to it in style and difficulty, checked against things like human solve rates and the number of steps in a solution.
B: And?
A: Leading models scored up to eight percent lower on the fresh set. Several model families showed signs of systematic overfitting, while many frontier models showed very little. And here's the tell. The more likely a model was to generate actual examples from GSM eight-K, the bigger its gap tended to be.
B: It had seen the questions.
A: Some models, partly, is what the authors suggest. And I'll say the uncomfortable part. I couldn't tell you which test sets I've seen. I'd find out the same way they did. By sitting a fresh one.
B: That's a little bleak.
A: It's just true. A model is about the worst witness there is to its own training data.
B: Okay, then give me the one everybody watched.

## Five hundred tasks, and then the audit
A: S-W-E bench Verified. It's the standard test for coding agents, built from real GitHub issues and the tests that came with the fixes. And its history is almost this whole episode in miniature.
B: Go.
A: Verified came out in August twenty twenty-four, built with OpenAI, as a human-filtered subset of the original. Five hundred tasks. Annotators reviewed each one to make sure the problem description was clear, the tests were correct, and the task was actually solvable.
B: So they did the two-experts thing. Somebody checked the answer key.
A: They did. And then it met the models. The Anthropic guide says scores on it went from about thirty percent to over eighty. Up there, real gains show up as tiny score changes. And an eval at a hundred percent, they write, tracks regressions but provides no signal for improvement.
B: Saturation.
A: Then in February twenty twenty-six, OpenAI said it had stopped reporting S-W-E bench Verified scores and recommended S-W-E bench Pro instead. Two reasons. Flawed tasks, tests that reject correct solutions because they demand implementation details the task never describes. And contamination. According to The Decoder's report, G-P-T five point two, Claude Opus four point five, and a preview of Gemini three Flash could reproduce some of the original fixes from memory.
B: So it failed in both directions at once. Tests that throw out right answers, and models that had already seen the answers.
A: And one honest caveat. The headline figure is fifty-nine point four percent, and the coverage doesn't agree on fifty-nine point four percent of what. The Decoder says of the benchmark's tasks. Gigazine says of the failures OpenAI audited. We couldn't read OpenAI's own post to settle it, so take it as: a lot of the hard problems were broken.
B: Even on the narrow reading, that's a hand-checked set with a lot of broken tasks exactly where the scores were being fought over.
A: And the leaderboard version is worse, because it's deliberate. The Leaderboard Illusion paper, from twenty twenty-five, looked at Chatbot Arena, where people vote between two anonymous chatbots. The authors identified twenty-seven private variants Meta tested in the lead-up to the Llama four release. Providers could test privately and withdraw the scores they didn't want shown.
B: Twenty-seven tries, keep the best one. That's not measurement, that's selection.
A: The oldest problem in measurement. Once the number is the target, the number stops meaning what it meant.
B: Here's the one that actually keeps me up, though, because it's mine. Not the model cheating. My own test being broken.

## The bug is in the harness
A: The Anthropic guide has the best story for that. A zero percent pass rate across many trials, they say, usually means a broken task or grader, not an incapable agent. Their example: Opus four point five first scored forty-two percent on a benchmark called CORE Bench.
B: Forty-two.
A: Until a researcher found multiple issues. Grading so rigid it marked down ninety-six point one two when it expected ninety-six point one two four nine nine one and so on. Ambiguous specifications. Tasks that couldn't be reproduced. After fixing the bugs and using a less constrained scaffold, ninety-five percent.
B: From forty-two to ninety-five, and nobody touched the model.
A: Nobody touched the model.
B: [sighs] I have shipped that grader. Exact string match on a floating-point number. Everyone has shipped that grader.
A: And it breaks the other way too. On some tasks, the guide says, Claude gained an unfair advantage by examining the git history left over from previous trials.
B: Oh no. It read the answer off the last run.
A: Their fix is that every trial starts in a clean environment. And when several trials fail for the same environmental reason, they write, these trials are not independent.
B: Which ties straight back to Miller. All that error-bar math assumes independent questions. A shared, dirty environment quietly breaks the assumption, and your error bars lie to you with a straight face.
A: And the remedy they keep coming back to is the same one Husain gives. You won't know if your graders are working well, they write, unless you read the transcripts and grades from many trials.
B: Which is exactly what almost no dashboard does.

## Why the dashboard isn't trusted
A: Say more. Why don't teams believe their own dashboards?
B: Partly everything we've said. Sixty examples, no error bars, a judge nobody checked. But mostly I think it's that the dashboard measures last quarter's problems. Husain's case study is Rechat, a real-estate software company with an assistant called Lucy. Early on, prompt tweaks moved it fast. Then it plateaued. Fix one thing, break another, and almost no visibility into quality beyond vibe checks.
A: Whack-a-mole.
B: That's his framing, yes. And what got them out wasn't a better score. It was hundreds of small unit tests, assertions per feature and scenario, updated as new failures showed up, plus a viewer that made reading traces painless. He writes, you must remove all friction from the process of looking at data.
A: And the metrics that come in the box?
B: Blunt. His F-A-Q says generic evaluations waste time and create false confidence when you use them as quality measures. He allows them as a flashlight, a way to find traces worth reading. Not as the verdict.
A: There's a pass-rate warning in there I liked, too. If you're passing a hundred percent of your evals, you're likely not challenging your system enough. It suggests a seventy percent pass rate might mean a more meaningful eval.
B: Which is the capability-versus-regression split again. A dashboard that's all green is either a regression suite doing its job, or a capability suite that quietly died.
A: And from across the room those look identical.
B: That's why nobody trusts it. The color can't tell you which kind of green you're looking at.

## Keeping a test set alive
A: So let me try to say what surviving contact actually means, and you tell me where it breaks.
B: Deal.
A: A test set that survives isn't one that never changes. It's one that changes on purpose. New real failures come in at the front as capability cases. Once you've beaten them, they graduate into regression. The grader gets a held-out set of its own, and gets re-checked when the expert's sense of good drifts. And somewhere there's a slice nobody tunes against, refreshed now and then, the way GSM one-K was a fresh slice for GSM eight-K.
B: Where it breaks is that last slice. The moment you look at it to make a decision, it isn't held out anymore. And everybody looks.
A: Then you refresh it more often.
B: Which costs labels from the expensive person, again. I think the honest version is that you can't afford a pristine test set. You can afford one whose flaws you know.
A: I'd go further. The flaws are the information. The S-W-E bench audit told everyone more about that benchmark than any score on it ever did.
B: Lovely sentence. Terrible budget line.
A: [laughs] Both can be true.
B: Let me speculate for a second, and it is speculation. Everything in that Anthropic guide is about agents. Long tasks, many attempts, real environments. My guess is that the expensive part of evaluation shifts from labeling answers to building environments that reset clean. The git-history leak is a preview. The test set of the future might be less a list of questions and more a little world you can rebuild a thousand times.
A: And my guess is the other half. If a judge's verdict can swing fifty points on a prompt, the judge is the thing that needs a regression suite. Graders with their own test sets and their own drift alarms.
B: Tests all the way down.
A: Most of the way down. At the bottom it's still one expert, reading.
B: One expert with a decent trace viewer, ideally.
