## A pause on the power bill
B: Hawley put "MORATORIUM on data centers," in capitals, on a list of Senate Republican priorities for the last six weeks of the session. Politico's headline frames it as him joining progressive Democrats in calling for a pause until Congress guarantees taxpayers won't face higher utility bills. His spokesperson's version: a federal moratorium "until Congress passes a bill of rights giving taxpayers guarantees of no rate increases."
A: Is there a bill?
B: No bill, and no length. That's important. What there is, is history. In February, Hawley and Senator Richard Blumenthal proposed the Guaranteeing Rate Insulation from Data Centers Act. New data centers would have to bring their own power generation, separate from the grid, with a transition period for existing ones and disclosure of their energy use.
A: And the Democrats he's joining?
B: Senator Sanders and Representative Ocasio-Cortez introduced their moratorium in March. Theirs lasts until safeguards are in place that A-I is safe, that the gains go to workers, and that it doesn't raise utility prices. Hawley's keeps only the last condition. Same word, moratorium, and a much narrower bargain.
A: Fine. Then the obvious question. Do data centers actually raise people's bills?
B: That's the dive, because the best new evidence says: much less than everyone thinks. The Washington Post's Shannon Osaka wrote up a working paper from Harvard and Penn researchers, including Serguei Netessine at Wharton. They estimate data centers were responsible for about four percent of residential electricity rate increases from twenty twenty to twenty twenty-five. Four percent of the increase, not a four percent increase.
A: That surprised me.
B: It surprised him. "Even to me it was somewhat surprising." And, "So far we have no evidence that data centers increase prices all that much." He points at gas prices, an aging grid, and repairs after wildfires and hurricanes.
A: Okay, I'll be the skeptic. A national average is exactly where a local problem goes to hide.
B: And the paper's coverage agrees with you, partly. For industrial customers it's fifteen percent. And in the P-J-M grid region, Pennsylvania, Virginia, Ohio, data center demand has pushed up prices at capacity auctions, and Maryland homes are paying for some of it. But here's the mechanism I find really interesting. Where the grid has spare capacity, a big new customer spreads the fixed costs over more kilowatt-hours, and everyone's price can go down. The Electric Power Research Institute found a doubling of data center capacity from twenty nineteen to twenty twenty-four went with a four percent drop in home prices.
A: A train that's mostly empty gets cheaper per seat when someone buys a block of tickets.
B: Right up until the train is full and you have to build another one. Which is the honest forecast. Demand is set to climb steeply, and new generation is where bills could actually move. Brattle's Ryan Hledik: "None of these are the kind of earth-shattering rate impacts you hear about."
A: Then why does a moratorium have votes in both parties?
B: Because the bill was never the whole complaint. Look at the local news from just this week. Boulder County, Colorado, voted Tuesday to ban all but very small data centers, a fifty-kilowatt limit, tighter than its own staff recommended. Its neighbors, Longmont, Broomfield, Loveland, Larimer County, have bans or moratoriums of their own. And GovTech reported this week that in Mississippi, regulators say SpaceXAI's subsidiary started building on about thirty acres in Southaven without water pollution permits, and sent violation notices that carry up to twenty-five thousand dollars a day.
A: So people are objecting to noise, water, and companies that build first and ask later. And the senator is offering them a power bill.
B: Because the power bill is the part a senator can legislate. And CSET made a point yesterday I think applies here too. Don't assume every objection to a data center is hostility to progress.
A: Then let me ask the builder's question for you. What happens to compute prices if this passes?
B: We can already see the shape. CoreWeave's earnings came out this week. TechCrunch says it expects about nine billion dollars in customer prepayments and deposits over the next three months. Short-term capacity sells for more than forty million dollars per megawatt a year, against twenty to twenty-five million on four- and five-year contracts. Renting what already exists costs roughly double. A national pause makes every built megawatt worth more, overnight.
A: So a moratorium is a gift to whoever already built.
B: To whoever already built. And here's my real problem with it. "A guarantee of no rate increases" is a promise no utility can make to anyone, for any reason. The only version of it I know how to wire is Hawley's own February bill. Make them bring their own generation. That's a rule. "Guarantee" is a slogan.
A: I'd say the slogan is the point. It's a way of saying permission now has a price, and the price is somebody's bill.
B: Then the price is set in a bill nobody's written yet.
