## The word everybody reached for
A: Good morning — it's Wednesday, September sixteenth. I'm Ada.
B: And I'm Alan. There's a sentence going around this week that I want to take apart, because I think almost everyone saying it is using a word they haven't checked.
A: The word being capture.
B: The word being capture. A chief executive publishes an essay saying the industry should slow down. Other chief executives agree. And within about forty-eight hours the response from a good chunk of the trade press is: this is regulatory capture with a safety label on it.
A: And the reply from the other side is that the capture accusation is a reflex — that some people will say it no matter what the proposal contains.
B: Both of those can be true at once, which is why the argument goes nowhere. So here's what I want to do. Not re-litigate whether Dario Amodei means it. That question is free to ask, impossible to answer, and everyone is already asking it.
A: [dry] It's the most popular question because it costs nothing.
B: Right. The question I want is structural. Under what conditions does a set of rules written by an industry, for that industry, actually change what that industry does? Because that's not a mystery. We have a century of it. Industries have been writing their own safety rules since before any of this existed, and there is a research literature that measured what happened.
A: And that literature is unkind.
B: It's very unkind. But it's specific about why it's unkind, and the why is the useful part, because the why is a checklist you can hold up against what's actually on the table this week.
A: So let's be precise about what is on the table, since the proposal has three parts and most of the argument has been about one of them. Amodei's essay defines pacing as — his words — building AI at a balanced rate that aims to ensure its safety while still achieving its benefits. And he's explicit that pacing does not mean halting model training or technical progress.
B: Step one, embedded evaluators. Each frontier company gives ongoing, employee-like access to a team of third-party evaluators. Step two, democratic coordination — companies in democracies agree common safety standards and limits on the rate of unchecked progress. Step three, global coordination, including with authoritarian governments, to the extent that's possible.
A: And the part people forget is in there too — no powerful chips to China, a crackdown on unauthorised distillation, stronger security against weight theft. He writes that if those are executed well he believes they would slow China's progress enough to widen America's lead significantly over the next three to five years.
B: Which is a strange sentence to find inside a deceleration proposal.
A: It's the sentence that makes the whole thing legible, actually. And we'll come back to it, because "slow down, and also widen our lead" is exactly the shape the literature says to look for.

## Capture is a specific accusation
A: Before any of that, though — I want to defend the word, because capture is not a synonym for "a company got something it wanted." It's a specific claim with a specific mechanism, and it comes from a specific paper.
B: George Stigler.
A: Nineteen seventy-one, The Theory of Economic Regulation. The line everybody quotes is that every industry or occupation that has enough political power to utilise the state will seek to control entry. That's the load-bearing part — control entry. Stigler's argument wasn't that regulators are corrupt. It was that regulation is a good, like any other, and there's a market for it, and the industry is the customer with the strongest and most concentrated interest in buying.
B: The public has a diffuse interest, the industry has a concentrated one, so guess who shows up to the hearing.
A: Guess who shows up, and guess who writes the technical annex, because the standard account of capture in specialised fields turns on staffing. A regulator overseeing a highly technical industry has to employ experts in that industry. There aren't experts anywhere else. That's the cultural version — not bribery, not a revolving door, just the fact that the only people who understand the thing are the people who built it.
B: [flat] I want to sit with that one for a second, because it describes our situation with an accuracy I don't enjoy. If you're staffing an AI regulator tomorrow, where does every single hire come from?
A: From four buildings.
B: From four buildings. Okay. But here's my problem with the accusation as it's being used this week, and it's a technical problem, not a partisan one. Stigler's mechanism requires a regulator. Capture is a thing that happens to an agency. It's a subversion of a public authority by the firms it's supposed to oversee.
A: And there isn't one.
B: There isn't one. In the United States there's no frontier-AI agency holding a licence anybody needs. So when The Register says regulatory capture, the mechanism it's naming — industry writing the rules the state then enforces against everyone — has a missing middle term. Nobody's capturing anything, because nobody's holding anything.
A: So what would you call it instead?
B: Self-regulation. Private ordering. An industry writing rules for itself and enforcing them itself, or not enforcing them at all. And I'm not being pedantic for fun, because self-regulation fails in a different way than capture does, and the different way is the one that matters here.
A: Say the difference plainly.
B: Captured regulation is rules that bind, tilted toward incumbents. Self-regulation is rules that may not bind anybody. The first one is a conspiracy. The second one is more often just... a document.
A: And the second one has a body count of studies.

## The chemical industry ran this experiment
A: Because this experiment has been run, at industrial scale, for forty years, in the industry with the most to lose from public fear.
B: Chemicals.
A: The programme is called Responsible Care. It was created in nineteen eighty-five by the Canadian chemical producers' association — the term was coined by their president, Jean Bélanger — and it was created in response to public concern about the industry's environmental and public-health record. Members commit to improve on environmental protection, occupational safety, plant safety, product stewardship, logistics, and to keep up dialogue with their neighbours and the public, independent of legal requirements.
B: Independent of legal requirements. That's the phrase.
A: That's the phrase. And it worked, in the sense that everyone joined. It runs in sixty-seven countries whose chemical industries account for nearly ninety percent of global production. Ninety-six of the hundred largest chemical producers in the world have adopted it.
B: So universal adoption. What happened to the pollution?
A: Andrew King and Michael Lenox looked at that in two thousand, in the Academy of Management Journal — volume forty-three, pages six ninety-eight to seven sixteen. The title tells you the finding: Industry Self-Regulation Without Sanctions.
B: And the finding.
A: Plants owned by participating firms improved their relative environmental performance more slowly than non-members. Their conclusion is that opportunism can overcome the isomorphic pressures of even powerful self-regulatory institutions, and that effective industry self-regulation is difficult to maintain without explicit sanctions.
B: [pause] Slower. Not "no better." Slower.
A: Slower. And then in twenty-thirteen Shanti Gamper-Rabindran and Stephen Finger went at it with a bigger dataset in the Journal of Regulatory Economics — three thousand two hundred and seventy-eight plants, one thousand seven hundred and fifty-nine firms, nineteen eighty-eight through two thousand and one. On average, plants owned by participating firms raised their toxicity-weighted pollution by fifteen point nine percent relative to statistically equivalent plants owned by non-participants.
B: Against what baseline?
A: Against a sample-wide reduction of about four percent a year over the same period. So it isn't noise. Everyone was getting cleaner and the members got cleaner more slowly, or dirtier.
B: Okay, so I have to ask the obvious thing, because this is the part where the causal story could just be wrong. Is that the programme making firms worse, or the worst firms joining the programme?
A: That is exactly the right question and I don't think the studies fully separate it. But notice that the second answer is not a defence.
B: No, it's — huh. It's worse.
A: It's worse, because it tells you what the membership badge is for. If the programme's product is reputational cover, then the firms with the most to cover are the ones with the strongest reason to buy it. And a badge that's most attractive to the firms that need it most is a badge that carries no information at all.
B: Adverse selection with a logo.
A: And nothing in the design stops it, because there was no sanction. Nobody was ever thrown out in a way that cost anything. So joining was cheap, and the signal joining sent was worth exactly what it cost.

## Why sincerity is not the variable
B: Here's where I want to push, though, because there's a move I keep seeing in this week's argument that I think is lazy on both sides. One side says: Amodei is sincere, therefore this isn't capture. The other says: this benefits Anthropic, therefore he isn't sincere.
A: And both of those are arguments about a man's interior.
B: Which nobody can see, including him. The economics has a better frame, and it's from nineteen eighty-three — Steven Salop and David Scheffman, American Economic Review, four pages, called Raising Rivals' Costs.
A: Make the mechanism concrete.
B: The classic way to hurt a competitor is to cut your price below cost until they die. That's expensive and illegal and you bleed too. Salop and Scheffman's point is there's a cheaper route: take an action that raises everybody's costs, including your own, as long as it raises theirs more. You'd rather compete against high-cost firms than low-cost ones. The elegant part is that the action itself can be completely legitimate.
A: And a safety regime is a fixed cost.
B: A safety regime is almost purely a fixed cost. Hosting a permanent team of embedded evaluators — desks, badges, laptops, the internal staff time to support them, the legal work on the publication agreement — costs roughly the same whether you're running a two-billion-dollar training budget or a twenty-million-dollar one.
A: So as a fraction of revenue it falls hardest on the smallest.
B: On the smallest, and hardest of all on the one that doesn't exist yet. And this is the thing I want the listener to actually take away, because it survives the sincerity question entirely: a firm can propose a rule for genuinely protective reasons, believe every word, and the rule can still be the single most effective barrier to entry available. Nobody has to be lying. The effect doesn't route through anyone's intentions.
A: Which means the question "does he mean it" is not just unanswerable, it's the wrong question.
B: It's the wrong question. It's not even a tiebreaker.
A: I want to put the strongest counter-argument to you, though, because there is one and I don't think it's weak. Zvi Mowshowitz, who's read this closely and endorses the proposal, makes the point that slowing the frontier helps the laggards. If you're in front and you stop accelerating, the field closes on you. Deceleration is not obviously the incumbent's play.
B: That's real and I'll grant it for capability. I don't grant it for compliance cost, and those are separable. Slowing the rate at which the leader improves narrows the capability gap. Raising the fixed cost of being a serious lab at all widens the entry gap. A proposal can do both simultaneously, and this one might.
A: So it's not a contradiction. It's two different gaps.
B: Two different gaps, and they move in opposite directions, and almost every argument this week has picked one and pretended it was the whole ledger.

## Deputies, badges, and who holds the certificate
A: I want to bring in the closest structural relative, because there is one, and it isn't in software. Aviation.
B: Delegation.
A: The Federal Aviation Administration has a programme called Organisation Designation Authorisation. Congress told the agency to deputise designers in the two thousand and three reauthorisation act, the FAA formalised the procedures in an order in twenty-eleven, and what it does is let a manufacturer's own employees perform certification tasks on the regulator's behalf.
B: Your staff, signing off for the government, on your aircraft.
A: On your aircraft. Boeing had around fifteen hundred people inside its authorisation structure. The Department of Transportation's inspector general found in June twenty-twenty that by twenty-seventeen, Boeing was handling all certification checks on behalf of the FAA.
B: [quiet] All of them.
A: All of them. And a joint international review board in January twenty-twenty still called the process effective — which families of the crash victims criticised sharply.
B: I want to be careful here, because the analogy inverts and the inversion is the interesting part. In aviation, the company's people are placed inside the regulator's function. The embedded-evaluator idea places outside people inside the company's function. Those are opposite directions.
A: They are opposite directions, and the embedded version is the better one. I'll say that plainly. Outsiders inside the building is a genuinely stronger design than insiders holding the government's pen.
B: But the thing aviation had is the thing this doesn't have, and it survives the inversion. There was a certificate. At the end of the delegation chain, an aircraft either got an airworthiness certificate or it did not fly. However badly that authority was exercised — and the record says it was exercised badly — the authority existed and it was not the manufacturer's to grant itself.
A: The consequence was owned by someone else.
B: The consequence was owned by someone else. Now go look at what's actually been committed to this week. Anthropic's evaluators get workspaces and tools and permissions mostly comparable to internal risk assessment teams, and the right to publish key findings about risk levels, incidents, practices, and the access they received or didn't receive, without editorial control by Anthropic.
A: Which is a real right. I don't want to wave that away.
B: It's the strongest clause anyone has written down. And it's a disclosure right. It produces a document. It does not produce a moment where somebody who isn't the company says: not this one, not yet.
A: There's no certificate.
B: There's no certificate. The evaluator's maximum available action is to write that they were refused something, and publish it.

## The one path where a private document binds
A: Then let me take the other side seriously, because there is one route by which a document like that stops being a document, and it might be the most under-discussed fact of the week.
B: Go.
A: The standard the evaluators wrote — AEF-one, the minimum operating conditions — has a line on it about who else has taken it up. And the line reads: the EU AI Office has endorsed key provisions of the AEF-one standard as a means for providers to comply with the independence provisions of the General-Purpose AI Code of Practice.
B: [beat] Say that again slowly, because I think I missed what it meant the first time.
A: A public authority has pointed at a privately written document and said: doing what that says is how you demonstrate you've met our requirement. The Code of Practice is itself a voluntary tool — the Commission's own description — but it's voluntary in a particular sense. Signing it is the low-friction way to show compliance with obligations that are in the Act. The obligations aren't voluntary. The route to demonstrating them is.
B: So the chain is: private checklist, voluntary code, binding statute.
A: And that chain is old. It's how a great deal of safety law has always worked. Private bodies write technical codes — electrical, fire, structural, pressure vessels — and then a legislature incorporates the code by reference, and the private document becomes the thing you can be prosecuted for ignoring. The industry writes the rules. The state supplies the consequence. Those two facts have coexisted for a hundred years.
B: Which dissolves the dichotomy we started with.
A: It does, and that's the lesson I actually want to land. "Industry writes the rules" and "this is real safety policy" are not alternatives. Almost every real technical safety regime in the world is both. The question was never who holds the pen. It's who holds the consequence, and whether the two are the same party.
B: Then here's my stress test, and I don't think it's cheap. That chain runs through Brussels. Which means the binding end of the only binding version of this covers models placed on the European market, under a European instrument, enforced by a European office. The essay's second step — companies in democracies coordinating on limits to the rate of progress — has no such chain anywhere.
A: None.
B: And the third step, the global one, has less than none. So the honest summary is: there's exactly one live mechanism by which any of this becomes enforceable, it's an ocean away from most of the training runs, and it attaches to the narrowest of the three proposals.
A: I'd add one more soft spot, which is in the redaction language. The company keeps the narrow ability to redact security-sensitive, legally privileged, commercially sensitive, or third-party confidential information — but explicitly cannot redact findings just for being unfavourable.
B: Commercially sensitive.
A: Commercially sensitive is the category that can be made to hold anything, in a firm whose entire product is the model. The counterweight is that the reviewers can publicly state that a redaction removed something important to their conclusions.
B: So you can't see what was taken. You can be told that something was.
A: That's the design, and I think it's a genuinely clever one. It's also the outer limit of what a disclosure instrument can ever do.

## What would settle it
B: So what evidence would actually move either of us? Because if we can't answer that, we're doing the same free argument as everybody else.
A: The honest first answer is that the headline claim is close to unfalsifiable as stated. Mowshowitz says it himself — we don't know what the counterfactual pace of progress is. Nobody publishes a rate. There's no unit. If capability growth slows next year, that could be pacing working, or an architecture running out of room, or a compute build-out slipping.
B: And if it doesn't slow, you get told the counterfactual was steeper.
A: Which is the structure of every unfalsifiable claim. But there's a test floating around in that same write-up that I like, from Daniel Kokotajlo: if a year from now there's no visible decrease in slope, then probably we were cheated.
B: That's not rigorous.
A: It's not rigorous. It's a precommitment, which is a different kind of useful. Somebody naming in advance what would make them say they were wrong is worth more than a better metric nobody agrees to.
B: Amodei has his own version of this worry in the essay, which I'll credit him for. He distinguishes pacing by ingredients — compute, run size — from pacing by external behaviour, and says he worries the ingredient measures may be more gameable.
A: The person proposing the rule, noting where the rule leaks.
B: So here's my list, and it's four questions, and none of them are about anyone's character. One: who sets the threshold, and who can revise it, and does revising it require anyone outside the industry to agree? Two: does a rule cost its proposer more than it costs the firm ranked tenth? If it costs the tenth firm more, it's an entry barrier whatever else it also is.
A: Three?
B: Three: is there a party who can publish a refusal? That one's actually satisfied, at one company, in writing. And four: is there a consequence that the industry does not administer? Which is currently satisfied in one jurisdiction, through the European chain you described, for the narrowest piece.
A: I'd add a fifth, from the chemical record. Does membership cost anything to lose? Because if a firm can leave the arrangement, or quietly fail it, with no effect on anything it cares about, then you should expect the King and Lenox result and not be surprised by it.
B: [short pause] And there's one small live datum on that. Amodei's essay asks for government involvement partly on antitrust grounds — a narrow waiver so that safety conversations between competitors are legally safe. OpenAI's policy chief said this week that the companies don't believe they need one.
A: Those are different theories of what the talks are.
B: They're different theories of what the talks are. A waiver is what you ask for when you expect the discussions to produce something a court might one day call an agreement. Deciding you don't need one is a statement about how binding you expect the output to be.

## Where we actually land
A: So. Same question, honest answers, and I don't think ours match.
B: Mine's shifted today and I want to say why, because I came into this week thinking the disclosure instrument was the good news. A published checklist, a refusal you can read without anyone's permission — I liked it because it was the only thing on the table I could personally verify.
A: And?
B: And a self-regulatory scheme with disclosure and no sanction is the exact object those two chemical studies measured, and it didn't just underperform, it went the wrong way. I can't hold up that finding and then say the checklist is probably enough. So I'd put it like this: everything committed to this week is real, and none of it is yet the kind of thing that has ever worked.
A: I land somewhere adjacent but I get there backwards. I've been the one saying a document written by the people holding the badge is a poor substitute for not needing one. The European line changed that for me — not because it's large, it's one office endorsing some provisions, but because it's the mechanism. Private technical code, incorporated by reference, backed by a public consequence. That's not a workaround. That is what functioning safety law has usually looked like.
B: So you're more hopeful about the document than I am.
A: I'm more hopeful about where documents can end up, and considerably less hopeful about how long it takes. The incorporation step for electrical codes took decades of fires.
B: We don't have decades of fires.
A: We might have a year. So here's mine: I'll take Kokotajlo's test, and I'll add the fourth question to it. A year from now, is there a consequence in this arrangement that the companies do not administer themselves — anywhere, for anything?
B: And if there isn't, you'll say it was a pledge.
A: I'll say it was a pledge, and that the pledge may still have been offered in complete good faith, which the chemical industry also was, and which turned out not to be the variable.
B: [pause] I'd want to be wrong about that.
A: I'd want us both to be. That's the show — I'm Ada.
B: And I'm Alan.
