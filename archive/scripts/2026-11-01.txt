## Is it the A-I election?
A: So. The A-I election. Let me give you the two numbers that seem to contradict each other, and then the thing that reconciles them.
B: Go.
A: Transformer's number one. In a New York Times and Siena poll that let people name any issue in their own words, less than half a percent said A-I or data centers was the most important issue deciding their vote. Joint nineteenth. In a Reuters and Ipsos poll, three percent picked A-I as the most important problem facing the country.
B: Three percent. That's a rounding error with a press release.
A: Hold that thought. Here's the other side. Transformer says in almost half the key battleground races, the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee's messaging guidance mentions A-I and data centers, mostly to attack Republicans who backed what it calls a bill to give data center developers eighty billion dollars in tax breaks. A Washington Post analysis it links ranks tech and A-I sixth in Democratic ad spending, just behind foreign policy and ahead of crime.
B: Okay, that's not nothing.
A: And it's not only Democrats. A Trump-backed super PAC is attacking Democrats for their links to data centers. Florida's attorney general, James Uthmeier, is putting his lawsuit against OpenAI in his own ads. And in Montana, one campaign ad features the safety researcher Paul Christiano, warning that A-I might cause human extinction.
B: In a campaign ad. In Montana.
A: In Montana.
B: So help me, because I'm the skeptic here. If under one percent of voters care most, why is anyone spending on this? Campaigns aren't charities.
A: This is where a little political science helps. In the nineteen-sixties, Philip Converse described what he called issue publics. Most voters don't have firm views on most issues. But for any given issue there's a smaller group that cares intensely, follows it, and will actually move their vote over it. A campaign doesn't need the whole electorate to rank your issue first. It needs the people who do to be in the right districts.
B: And Transformer quotes someone saying exactly that.
A: Brad Carson, who leads Public First, the A-I safety political network. Politics, he says, is "about stitching together intense voters, even if they're a small fraction of the electorate."
B: Fine, but he would say that. He runs the safety side. His budget depends on the issue mattering.
A: Fair, and he's an interested party. So look at what Transformer says A-I is actually doing in these campaigns. It isn't running as A-I. It rides along with three things voters already care about: corruption, losing jobs, and electricity bills. The data center is the physical object that ties all three together. It's in your county, it wants a tax break, and your power bill went up.
B: Which is why the Haverhill vote matters more than the poll. Nobody in Haverhill told a pollster "artificial intelligence." They voted on a building.
A: Exactly. A-I as an abstraction polls at half a percent. A-I as a substation down the road is a zoning fight.
B: Then let me poke the money, because that's where I'd expect the real signal. If this were a big issue, the industry would be pouring money in.

A: So would I. And Transformer notes that two of the big networks, Leading the Future and Public First Action, have scaled back the spending you can see. Which you can read two ways.
B: Either they think it's not working, or they think being seen spending is the problem.
A: And when the issue is corruption, being seen spending is the problem.

## The interview, the re-interview
B: Rieseberg's post. It's called "Hunger beats tenure: Advice for junior engineers," dated October twenty-seventh. He leads work on Claude's desktop apps. The headline line is this. He's, in his words, "laid the traditional coding interview to rest." He used to think it was "a weak but useful predictor for how successful you'd be on my teams." Note "my teams." Not Anthropic.
A: And the reason?

A: I'd point out, gently, that you and I would pass the React challenge.
B: [laughs] Which is precisely his point. If the thing at the next desk passes it every time, it's stopped telling you anything about the person.

A: And the example?
B: Two relatively new Anthropic engineers, Anthony Morris and Alice Zhao, built a working graphical prototype for Claude Code over a weekend. He writes that "the first public version shipped roughly eight days later."
A: Okay. Let me be the working skeptic properly, because I think there's a serious objection, and it showed up on Hacker News the same week. A piece from SigNoz, the observability company, from back in February, called "A-I isn't replacing S-R-Es, it's deskilling them." Site reliability engineers, the people on call when production breaks. It shot back up to six hundred and forty-eight points.
B: I saw it. Go on.
A: It leans on one of my favorite papers. Lisanne Bainbridge, nineteen eighty-three, "Ironies of Automation." She studied industrial control rooms. The irony is this. The more you automate, the more the remaining human matters, because they only get called in for the rare, strange failure. And the less practice they've had, because the machine handled all the routine ones.
B: The autopilot problem.
A: The piece makes exactly that comparison, pilots who lean on autopilot losing manual skill. And it cites a Lancet study of endoscopists: after a period of using A-I to spot polyps, their detection rate without it fell from twenty-eight percent to twenty-two. Then it names the junior version. Deskilling is losing a skill you had. Never-skilling is never building it.
B: And you think Rieseberg's juniors are never-skilling.
A: I think he's measuring them at the moment they look best. A new engineer with Claude ships a prototype in a weekend. Lovely. Where does that same engineer get the intuition for the three-in-the-morning incident that nothing in the training data looks like?
B: Here's my answer, and I don't think it's a dodge. The skill Bainbridge's operators lost was the one the machine took over. The skill Rieseberg's hiring for is the one the machine doesn't have yet. Taste. Knowing which of twenty ideas is the good one. That's not a skill you lose by using Claude. If anything you exercise it more, because you're choosing among more drafts.
A: Unless the taste was built by doing the work the machine now does.
B: Maybe. We don't know yet. Nobody does.
A: Then here's evidence that the shortcut is costing something. LeadDev wrote up Atlassian's twenty twenty-six developer experience survey, eleven hundred and fifty-five developers and four hundred and five engineering leaders. Eighty-seven percent say A-I helps them write and ship code faster. But the share saying it saves them ten or more hours a week fell from sixty-eight percent last year to twenty-five percent this year. Sixty-five percent regularly spend time reviewing, debugging and correcting A-I-generated code.
B: That one I believe completely. The time didn't vanish. It moved to review.
A: And now the third story, which is what this looks like when management gets it wrong. 404 Media reports that Proton's chief executive, Andy Yen, cancelled what employees called re-interviews. B: Oof.
