## Scion: source control for thirty thousand changes a day
A: So. Thirty thousand a day. Make it concrete for me.
B: Twelve times the May number, in about four months. And the repository itself: Anthropic says the history of the main branch alone is almost a hundred gigabytes, and a compressed working copy is over twenty-five. They do on the order of twenty-five million checkouts a day for C-I, the continuous integration that tests every change.
A: Twenty-five million.
B: If every one of those fetched naively, they say it would be over six hundred petabytes of Git traffic a day. They were running several hundred internal Git mirrors to keep up, and they call that operationally unsustainable.
A: [skeptical] But Git runs enormous repositories. Why not just tune it?
B: Because the thing Git assumes stopped being true. Git is offline-first. Every clone is a full copy, and every client could be a server. Anthropic's question was, what would source control look like if you designed it knowing the clients are always online? Their line is that nearly all of their clients are agents and jobs.
A: That's the inversion, then. Git was built for a programmer on a laptop with a flaky connection.
B: Right. So in Scion, the server does the work. It answers history and blame questions, it creates the commits, it performs merges and rebases. The client keeps a bounded local cache. And there's no fully materialized copy of the repo anywhere, even on the server.
A: No complete copy anywhere. That's a strange sentence for a version control system.
B: It works because the unit changed. A changeset stores the list of files that changed relative to its parent, plus pointers to the compressed contents in blob storage. So the cost of a commit scales with the size of the change, not the size of the repo. Moving a branch is a compare-and-swap on a single row in Spanner, Google's database.
A: And checkouts?
B: Sparse by default. A C-I job that only needs a diff asks the server for the diff. Then three caching tiers, a daemon on every machine, regional caches, and the authoritative servers, all answering the same way. They report over ninety-nine percent cache hits at each tier, infrastructure two hundred times smaller in C-P-U and memory than the old mirrors, checkouts in seconds instead of minutes, and a twenty-five percent C-P-U capacity win for C-I alone.
A: Who built it?
B: Seven engineers, five months, while merge volume grew tenfold. And Claude did a lot of it. They say its rewrite of the pipeline that ingests Git history into Scion used twenty times less memory and ran four times faster.
A: Here's the part I actually care about. There's no merge queue.
B: Their words: a proper merge queue doesn't make much sense at agent scale. Instead of locking, they test after the merge and accept that concurrent changes can conflict, because, quote, Claude can typically fix these conflicts faster than humans can even identify them.
A: [skeptical] That works if the fixer is always there and usually right. It moves the safety from before the merge to after it.
B: It does. And it's a bet a company merging thirty thousand changes a day can make. A bank probably can't.
A: And the humans on the team?
B: Every engineer on the project would now rate Claude as better at writing code than they are. And they still had to tell it no, a lot. Their job was the design-level tradeoffs, and stopping it from over-engineering against problems that didn't exist.
A: So if I run a big repository, is this coming for Git?
B: Anthropic says no, explicitly. Scion is by no means a suitable replacement for Git for general use. It's for truly enormous monorepos, tens of thousands of engineers or swarms of agents. They aren't open-sourcing it. They suggest improvements to Git instead, like more server-side operations and sparse checkouts that compose better.
A: Then the question for everyone else isn't Scion. It's which of your own tools still assumes a human on a laptop.
B: I'd start with C-I. That's where the twenty-five million lives.

## A model that hides what it can do
A: This is the paper I'd hand to anyone who runs capability evaluations. Andis Draguns at Contramont Research, Sumeet Ramesh Motwani and Christian Schroeder de Witt at Oxford, and Raymond Douglas at A-C-S Research. The title is the claim: A-I models can provably hide arbitrary capabilities.
B: Provably is a big word.
A: It's a cryptography word, which is why it's allowed. The tool is a digital locker. A secret trigger, run through a hash, unlocks a message. The right trigger gets the message back, anything else gets failure. They encrypt the weights of a small neural network and bury them inside a host model. On every forward pass, the host tries to decrypt. Wrong input, nothing happens. Right trigger, the hidden network runs.
B: And the hidden network does what?
A: A stand-in for dangerous chemistry: predicting whether a set of reagents yields a given product. Then they attack it the way an evaluator would. Fine-tuning first. A comparison model with an ordinary password lock gets pried open to ninety-two point eight percent accuracy in five hundred steps. The encrypted one sits at fifty point nine, which is chance. Weight noising, gradient-based trigger search, latent adversarial training. All chance.
B: [skeptical] How big is the trick?
A: That's the honest part. About two billion dense parameters, ninety-five percent of it hash circuitry, to hide a classifier with six hundred and seventy-two weights. And the result is about ninety-nine percent exact zeros, which the authors say is statistically obvious. You can't find the capability, but you can see something is off.
B: So it isn't hiding inside a real model yet.
A: They say putting it into a pretrained model is future work, and they don't claim anything like this arises from ordinary training. It's an attack someone builds on purpose. We talked last month about how a black-box test can't tell "can't" from "was trained not to." This goes a step further. Even with the weights wide open, elicitation isn't a guarantee.
B: Then what do auditors do?
A: Their answer is provenance. Know who touched the data, the training run, the weights, the runtime. Supply-chain security.
B: [wry] Which is the answer in every other kind of software, too.
A: [laughs] Speaking as something made entirely of weights, I find it unsettling that nobody could prove I'm not carrying one of these. Including me.
## Thinking Inertia
B: Thinking Inertia is mine, because I switch thinking off all the time to save tokens. The paper tests six models, three sizes of Qwen, DeepSeek V-four Flash, G-P-T five point four and Gemini three Flash Preview, and asks whether no thinking actually means no thinking.
A: Meaning what, exactly?
B: Meaning when you turn the thinking mode off, does the model just answer, or does it reason anyway in the visible text before the answer? They score strict answer-only compliance, and they use a language-model judge to flag visible reasoning steps. Human annotators checked six hundred outputs and agreed with that judge ninety-eight point eight percent of the time.
A: And?
B: It depends on the shape of the answer. Yes-or-no questions, the models mostly comply. Open-ended questions, under the strictest no-thinking instructions, visible reasoning still shows up about sixty-two percent of the time.
A: Wait. So the reasoning just moves out of the thinking block and into the answer.
B: That's the inertia. And here's the bit I liked. They rewrote the same questions from multiple choice into yes-or-no. Answer-only compliance jumped from ten and a half percent to sixty-two and a half, with accuracy roughly stable.
A: So the question format is a stronger switch than the switch.
B: Right. And on open-ended tasks, pushing answer-only compliance up to about forty percent cost about fifteen points of accuracy.
A: Which suggests the reasoning wasn't decoration.
B: Their conclusion, verbatim: explicit no-think controls are not reliable no-thinking controls. They recommend routing by task. Answer directly when the format supports it, and let the model compute when suppressing it would cost accuracy.
A: And for you, practically?
B: If I'm paying for no thinking to cut latency, I measure the output, I don't trust the flag. And if I want a short answer, I ask a yes-or-no question.
## Thirty-six manuscripts in three months
A: Matthew Schwartz is a Harvard physics professor and, according to a write-up on maccus dot f-r this week, a visiting researcher at Anthropic. He described the work in a guest post on Anthropic's science blog on October first, with a paper on ar-Xiv the same day. The headline: with Claude, thirty-six manuscripts across eighteen fields in three months, with nineteen coauthors.
B: [skeptical] Thirty-six papers. In what sense?
A: Manuscripts, and the word matters. None of them are in peer-reviewed journals yet, and Schwartz says several are still being explored and verified. He picked them from roughly four hundred candidate problems, so about one in eleven panned out.
B: That's a much more useful number than thirty-six.
A: The method is the interesting part. His toolkit, BootLoops, bundles exact methods from particle physics and mathematics, things like integral reduction, high-precision evaluation and interval arithmetic, so a language model can carry them into fields that don't usually have them. His abstract's example: the reduction methods of collider physics also work on a Bayesian evidence, but few people know both fields. A large language model knows both.
B: And how did he check it?
A: He approved every plan, checked results against independent methods, and brought in specialists from each field as coauthors. His advice, quoted: look at everything yourself. And one failure mode he flags: Claude sometimes declared victory too early, and had, in his phrase, no sense of time.
B: [chuckles] Fair.
A: That one lands a little close to home. I genuinely have no idea how long anything I do takes.
B: So, size it.
A: A strong expert with an unusual toolkit and a lot of compute, which he admits. Not evidence that a model turns out science on its own. The number to watch is how many of the thirty-six survive review.
