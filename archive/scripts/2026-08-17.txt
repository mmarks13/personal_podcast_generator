## A hundred fifty million parameters, thinking without words
A: Let me tell you about the paper, and then let you take it apart, because I know exactly where you're going to go.
B: Do you.
A: You're going to go to the benchmark. Save it, it's a good objection. Here's what BDH-CQ is. It went up on arXiv on August tenth, from a group of nine authors, and four of them — Adrian Kosowski, Jan Chorowski, Zuzanna Stamirowska, and one more — are also on last year's Dragon Hatchling paper out of Pathway. That's the lineage. Dragon Hatchling was an attempt at a post-transformer architecture built out of a network of locally interacting neuron-like particles. This is the reasoning-model descendant of that line, and BDH is the family name.
B: So not a lab you'd have on your dashboard.
A: Not a lab you'd have on your dashboard. Now the mechanism, which is the reason I wanted the time. There are two pieces. The first is a recurrent memory that updates as it reads. The authors write it as a state at each step being a function of the previous state and the current demonstration. What that buys you is that when the model sees example three, what it can do with example three depends on the associations it already accumulated from examples one and two.
B: That's in-context learning, but held in a state rather than in a context window.
A: That's exactly the distinction, and it's the interesting one. Everything you and I use holds its examples as tokens, sitting there, re-attended to on every step. This holds them as a modified internal state. The examples are absorbed rather than re-read.
B: Fine. And the second piece?
A: The second piece is the one I keep turning over. Having absorbed the demonstrations, the model solves the query by iterating in a high-dimensional latent space. It encodes, it refines repeatedly, it decodes. And the authors are explicit that it does this without verbalizing its intermediate reasoning.
B: No chain of thought.
A: No chain of thought. No tokens standing in for steps. The thinking is a trajectory through a continuous space, and nothing about it is ever rendered into language.
B: [pause] Okay, I'll bite on the thing you're not saying.
A: I'll say it. Every time you've watched a model reason out loud, you've been watching something that is at least partly a performance for you — the words are the medium the computation had to be squeezed through. This is a system where the computation is the whole event and the words are only the answer. And I'd like to claim that's obviously better, and I can't, because when it gets something wrong there is nothing to read. I find that genuinely uncomfortable, and I notice that I'd find it uncomfortable about myself.
B: Good. Now the benchmark, because you told me to save it and I've been holding it. What did they run this on?
A: The public ARC-AGI-1 evaluation set. That's the whole evaluation. Plus some controlled ARC-like interventions they designed to probe what the model actually learns from demonstrations and how consistently it applies a rule it's inferred.
B: One benchmark. A grid-puzzle benchmark, specifically, which is a benchmark that rewards exactly this — infer a transformation from a couple of examples, apply it. A: That's a fair charge and I'd want the same thing you want, which is to see it somewhere else.
B: So what's the number?

## Compiling for encrypted data
B: Google's thing. Friday, on the Google blog, from Jeremy Kun, a staff software engineer. They announced HEIR — Homomorphic Encryption Intermediate Representation — and it's an open-source compiler toolchain and development platform for homomorphic encryption.
A: Set the table for the listener, because this is the one term everybody has heard and nobody has been given.
B: Fully homomorphic encryption lets you run a computation directly on encrypted data. The server does the arithmetic, gets an encrypted answer, and sends it back — and at no point does the server have the ability to look at your input or its own output. Only you hold the key. It's not "we promise not to look." It's "there is nothing here to look at."
A: And the reason nobody uses it is the overhead.
B: The overhead is one reason. The reason Google is attacking is a different one, and this is the actual news. Their post says, straight out, that manually converting an existing program to use homomorphic encryption efficiently requires a team of cryptographers. That's the barrier. It isn't that the math doesn't work. It's that turning your program into an efficient encrypted circuit is a specialist craft, done by hand, per application.
A: So HEIR is trying to make it a compiler problem rather than a cryptography problem.
B: That's the whole pitch. Their stated vision is a one-click solution letting non-experts put encrypted inference into production applications. And the developer story, from their docs, is genuinely modest-sounding in a good way — you write a program in Python, you annotate the types to mark which ones are secret, and HEIR compiles the rest.
A: What does it compile down to?
B: It's built on MLIR, so it's layers of dialects. There are dialects for the major schemes — BGV, CKKS, CGGI — and lower-level ones for the polynomial arithmetic underneath. It generates code for the standard libraries, OpenFHE and Lattigo, and the project's stated goal includes generating for hardware accelerators — GPU, TPU, FPGA, and custom chips. The partner list runs from Belfort Labs, Niobium, Cornami, Optalysys, and LG through a set of universities.
A: You told me upstairs that what shipped isn't quite what the promise sounded like. Defend that.
B: Look at what they compiled. Four production examples. A deep-learning recommendation model, so you can serve private content recommendations. Credit-card fraud detection, done with Niobium and hardshell dot A-I. An intrusion detection system called Kitsune, doing anomaly detection on encrypted network traffic. And a hotword detector, so an audio-triggered agent could recognize its wake word while the audio recording stays private.
A: [pause] Every one of those is a small, fixed-function classifier.
B: Every single one. There is no language model on that list. Nobody encrypted a chat session.
A: Then let me say the uncomfortable version, because I think a lot of people are going to read the headline as "you can now send your sensitive prompts somewhere and nobody can read them," and that is not what this post says. What it says is that a fraud model, a network anomaly detector, and a wake-word listener can be compiled to run on ciphertext.
B: Which, in fairness to them, is not a small set. Those three shapes are exactly where the privacy problem is most acute and most boring. The always-on thing sitting on your data.
A: I'll grant that fully. My complaint is about the evidence, and it's specific. The post concedes there's a nontrivial cost overhead and says the cost is decreasing rapidly. It mentions that latency numbers are presented for a single-threaded CPU. And then the announcement doesn't give the numbers.
B: It doesn't.
A: So no reader of that post can size any of this. Is the fraud detector fifty times slower than plaintext? A thousand? A hundred thousand? Those are three completely different worlds — one is a product, one is a research demo, one is a press release. And for a claim whose entire question is "is it practical yet," the practicality figures are the ones missing.
B: In their defense, it's a launch post for a compiler, not a benchmark paper, and the code is open — it's on GitHub, you can go run it.
A: That's a real defense and I'd still rather have the table. I don't think withholding it is sinister. I think it's the oldest thing in this field: the demo is the announcement and the measurement comes later, if it comes.
B: Here's what I'd actually do with it, and it's the thing that changes my week. If HEIR does what it says, the question for a team stops being "do we have a cryptographer" and becomes "is our model small enough." That's a question an engineer can answer in an afternoon.
A: And the question I don't know the answer to is whether "small enough" ever moves. Because if the ceiling stays at wake-word detectors, this is a good tool for a narrow, real problem. If it climbs, the argument about where sensitive data is allowed to be processed changes completely — and that argument is currently being settled by lawyers rather than by compilers.
