A: And then a further seventeen hours to formalize and check it in Lean, according to the company's own writeup — that's the version Simon Willison quoted this week.
B: Right. So here's my question, and it is not a rhetorical one. What was it doing?
A: [laughs] For eighty-eight hours.
B: For eighty-eight hours. Because "the model searched for a proof" is the sentence everybody writes, and I've realized I don't actually know what that sentence means anymore. It meant something specific five years ago. I think it means something else now.
A: It does. And it's worth pulling apart, because it isn't one thing wearing one name — there are at least three different algorithms in the field right now that all get described as searching for a proof, and they don't resemble each other much.
B: And the other one from this month is the Anthropic result — Claude formalizing Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean. Eleven days, thirty thousand three hundred theorems proved, about thirteen million lines of Lean.

## What a proof looks like to a machine
A: So the setting for almost all of this is a proof assistant — Lean is the one in the news, but the story is the same in Isabelle or Coq. And the thing you should picture is not an essay. It's a state.
B: A state like a position.
A: Like a position. At any moment you have a goal — the thing you're trying to show — and a context, the hypotheses you're allowed to use. That pair is called the tactic state. And you make progress by applying a tactic, which is a command that transforms the state. Rewrite using this equation. Apply this lemma. Split into cases.
B: And a tactic can leave you with more than one goal.
A: Very often it does. You said "it suffices to prove A and B", and now you owe the system both. That detail is going to matter enormously in about ten minutes.
B: Noted. And at the end?
A: At the end there are no goals left, and what you've actually built is a proof term — an object the kernel checks. And hold onto the kernel, because everything else in this episode leans on it. It's small, it's stable, it has been read by a lot of people, and its answer is one bit. Accepted, or not.
B: See, that's the thing that makes this domain different from everything else we talk about on this show. Every other week we're arguing about whether a benchmark measures anything.
A: [wry] Whereas here the grader is a piece of software from the nineteen-nineties lineage that does not care who you are.
B: Which is why math went first. If you're going to let a model run unattended for eighty-eight hours, you'd like something at the end that isn't a vibe.
A: And that's the promise. But notice what you've bought and what you haven't. The kernel tells you the proof is correct. It does not tell you the statement you proved was the statement you meant — we hit that one on Monday's show and I won't re-litigate it.
B: Okay. So it's a game with a perfect referee. Then it's chess.
A: No. And this is where the trouble starts.

## Why the tree explodes
A: In chess you have maybe thirty legal moves. Here, a move is "apply a lemma", and the lemmas are whatever's in the library. The LeanDojo benchmark, which is the standard open dataset for this, was built by extracting ninety-eight thousand seven hundred and thirty-four theorems and proofs out of Lean's math library.
B: So the branching factor is a hundred thousand.
A: The branching factor is a hundred thousand and then some, because you can also invent terms. Supply the witness. Choose the clever intermediate quantity. That's the move that isn't in any list, and it's precisely the move the field could not automate for decades.
B: Right, this is the Polu and Sutskever framing.
A: It is. The GPT-f paper in twenty-twenty opens by naming it: the major limitation of automated theorem provers compared to humans is the generation of original mathematical terms, and the bet was that a language model might be able to address it. That's the whole reason large language models entered this field at all.
B: Which means before that, the entire game was retrieval.
A: Retrieval, essentially, yes.
B: Say more, because I think people skip this part and it's half the story.
A: So the classical tool is a hammer — Isabelle's Sledgehammer is the famous one. You're stuck on a goal, you invoke it, and it heuristically pre-selects some number of facts from the library that look relevant, hands the goal plus those facts to strong external automated provers, and if one succeeds it reconstructs a proof step you can keep.
B: And the hard part is the pre-selecting.
A: The hard part is the pre-selecting. It's called premise selection, and it's an information retrieval problem sitting inside a search problem. Get it wrong and you've handed your solver a hundred irrelevant facts and it times out.
B: Do we have numbers on how much the neural version helps?
A: We do. The Magnushammer paper replaced the heuristic selection with contrastive transformer retrieval, and reported fifty-nine and a half percent against Sledgehammer's thirty-eight point three on the PISA benchmark, and thirty-four percent against twenty point nine on miniF2F.
B: So roughly a half-again improvement from just being better at guessing which facts matter.
A: From being better at guessing which facts matter. Hold that, because when we get to this month's systems, you'll see the same problem reappear at a much larger scale wearing a completely different costume.

## The tree, and the one-bit reward
B: Okay. So the model proposes tactics. Something has to decide which proposals to follow.
A: And that's the search proper. Picture a tree: nodes are tactic states, edges are tactics you tried. You expand promising nodes, you back up what you learn. If that sounds like AlphaZero, it's because everyone in this area read the same papers.
B: What's the value function estimating? In Go it's "am I winning".
A: Here it's roughly "is this state provable, and cheaply." A critic that looks at a goal and predicts whether going down this branch ever terminates.
B: And it's a nightmare to train, right? Because the reward—
A: The reward is one bit, at the very end, and almost always zero. You take fifty tactic steps and get nothing back. Nothing partial. There's no "you were close."
B: [sighs] Yeah, that's the part builders underestimate. Sparse and binary is the worst reward shape there is.
A: And there's a structural wrinkle that makes it worse than a game tree. Remember I said a tactic can leave you two goals? That means the node isn't satisfied by finding one good child. It's satisfied by finding a set of children, all of which you must also prove.
B: Ah. So it's an and-or tree.
A: It is, and that's exactly the observation behind HyperTree Proof Search, from twenty twenty-two — the search object isn't a path through the tree, it's a hypertree: a subtree where every branch you opened got closed.
B: Which changes the credit assignment completely. You can't call a move good until all its obligations are discharged.
A: Correct.
B: Fine. So how do you get better at this without a human labeling anything?
A: Expert iteration. You search, you find some proofs, you train the model on the proofs you found, the model proposes better tactics, you search again. It bootstraps off its own successes.
B: Does that actually move?
A: The HyperTree authors report proving sixty-five point four percent of a held-out set of Metamath theorems with search alone, and eighty-two point six percent with online training on top — against fifty-six and a half for GPT-f, the previous state of the art. And on the miniF2F curriculum set, forty-two percent against a prior thirty-one.
B: So the loop is worth more than the search algorithm.
A: The loop is worth a lot. And the other lovely fact about GPT-f, which I think is underrated as a historical marker: it found new, shorter proofs that were accepted into the main Metamath library. First time a deep learning system contributed proofs a formal math community actually adopted. Twenty-twenty.
B: Six years ago.
A: Six years ago.

## Millions of problems, and one problem
B: So where does AlphaProof sit in that lineage? Because that's the one people remember.
A: Same skeleton, much more of everything. DeepMind's description is a solver network searching over proof steps in Lean, trained with AlphaZero, and — the clever bit — a formalizer network whose job is to translate informal problem statements into formal ones, so the system can manufacture its own curriculum.
B: Because the shortage isn't compute, it's problems.
A: The shortage is problems. Human-written formal problems number in the thousands. So you take the enormous pile of informal competition math that exists in text, and you auto-formalize it.
B: How much?
A: Here I have to be careful, because the sources count different things. The twenty twenty-four blog post says around a million informal problems were formalized. The Nature paper this year describes a curriculum of millions of auto-formalized problems and the reporting around it puts the statement count at eighty million.
B: Those aren't the same number.
A: They're not, and I can't reconcile them from outside — statements and problems aren't the same unit, and I'd rather tell you that than pick the impressive one.
B: Appreciated. And the results were the Olympiad ones.
A: The twenty twenty-four International Mathematical Olympiad. Combined with AlphaGeometry Two, four of six problems, twenty-eight points out of forty-two, which was the silver medal threshold that year.
B: And then there's the timing, which I still can't get past. AlphaGeometry did the geometry problem in nineteen seconds.
A: Nineteen seconds. And AlphaProof took up to three days on others.
B: Three days. On a problem a teenager has four and a half hours for.
A: Which is a real asterisk on the medal, and DeepMind said as much at the time.
B: I'm not even being snarky about it. I think that spread — nineteen seconds to three days — is the most informative thing in the whole result. It tells you there's no smooth difficulty curve. Some problems fall to pattern, and some require the search to grind.
A: And the reason a problem can take three days is the piece I think is genuinely new. The training doesn't stop when the contest starts. DeepMind describes reinforcement learning continuing at test time — the system keeps learning during the competition, on the problem in front of it.
B: Wait. So it's not a fixed model doing a long search. It's a model still training while it searches.
A: Still training while it searches. Not "learn mathematics," but "learn this neighborhood of mathematics, now, because I need it."
B: [pause] Okay, that's a lot more interesting to me than a bigger tree. That's the system deciding what to become for the next hour.
A: And it costs accordingly.

## Whole proof, or one step at a time
B: I want the other axis, because there's a fork here that determines everything about how a system feels to run. Do you generate the whole proof, or one tactic at a time?
A: And the cleanest treatment of that fork is DeepSeek-Prover, version one point five, which is open and documents both sides honestly. Single-pass whole-proof generation: you write the entire Lean proof in one go, and submit. Cheap, one call, no round trips.
B: And wrong at step four, so the other forty steps are fiction.
A: Compounding errors, in their phrase. Whereas step-wise gives you the compiler's feedback at every tactic, which is enormously more informative and enormously more expensive — you're paying a model call and a compile per step.
B: So what did they do?
A: Something in between that I like a lot, called truncate and resume. Generate a whole proof. Submit it. When Lean reports an error, truncate the code at the first error — throw away everything after it — and keep the prefix that did work. Then re-prompt the model with that verified prefix, and append the current tactic state as a comment.
B: Oh, that's nice. The comment is the model's new goal.
A: The comment is the ground truth about where you actually are, written by the compiler rather than by the model's belief about where it is.
B: Which is the whole game in agent design generally. The system's own account of its state drifts; the tool's account doesn't.
A: And then on top of that they run a tree search, and here's where the sparse reward comes back. They call it R-Max tree search. Because the real reward is almost always zero, they hand out an intrinsic reward for exploration — the paper's phrase is exploration bonuses for discovering new tactic states.
B: So you're paid for novelty, not for correctness, because correctness pays nothing for hours.
A: That's the idea. Reward seeing something you haven't seen.
B: And the delta?
A: Modest and honest. Sixty point two percent to sixty-three point five on the miniF2F test set with the search on top of single-pass. Twenty-three point seven to twenty-five point three on ProofNet.
B: Okay, now stop, because those two benchmarks are the number I actually want the listener to take away. Say what they are.
A: miniF2F is high-school competition level. ProofNet is undergraduate coursework.
B: Sixty-three percent on high school. Twenty-five percent on undergrad. That's a cliff, and it's roughly where the open systems were in twenty twenty-four.
A: It's a cliff, and the reason is the library. At competition level, the lemmas you need mostly exist and the difficulty is the trick. At undergraduate level, you're in territory where the thing you need may simply not be in Mathlib, and no amount of premise selection retrieves a fact that isn't there.
B: So the machine's real limit isn't reasoning, it's inventory.
A: Some large fraction of it is inventory. Which brings us to what actually changed this year.

## What twenty twenty-six changed
B: Because nothing I've described sounds like eleven days of parallel agents writing thirteen million lines.
A: No. And what's striking about the Fermat's Last Theorem work is what's missing from the description. There's no bespoke tree search. There's no trained solver network. It's a general-purpose model in an agent harness with a compiler.
B: So where did the search go?
A: Up a level. Anthropic's account is that the agents used a platform called Prove2Me, from Columbia, which maintains a directed acyclic graph of theorem statements — statements, not proofs — and the agents navigate that graph to decide what to attempt next. It also splits statements and proofs into separate files, which speeds compilation, and lets agents search the graph by natural-language descriptions of each theorem so they can find and reuse what somebody else already proved.
B: So the unit of search is "which statement should exist," and filling it in is almost a subroutine.
A: Which is what a working mathematician would tell you the job always was. The proof of a big theorem is a scaffolding of lemmas, and the skill is knowing which lemmas to want.
B: And that's the same premise-selection problem from twenty minutes ago, except now the library is one you're writing as you go.
A: Yes. That's the through-line of the whole episode. It was never mainly about tactics.
B: There's one number in there I keep chewing on. The failed early attempts.
A: Mm. They report that the first attempts went badly — agents got some early traction and then lost track of the project's state and stopped collaborating — and yet material from those failed efforts still ended up as about seven percent of the non-boilerplate lines in the final proof.
B: Which is a thing you cannot do in normal software. A failed run that still ships seven percent of the product.
A: Because every line was independently checkable. You don't have to trust the run that produced it. You only have to trust the kernel that accepted it.
B: That's the actual economic unlock. Verification is what makes a garbage run partially valuable instead of worthless.
A: And it's why I think the Navier–Stokes shape is the one to watch, because it inverts the order. There the reasoning is reported as informal first — agents arguing in ordinary mathematical English for eighty-eight hours — and the Lean formalization came afterward, seventeen hours of it, as a check on a result they already believed.
B: Which is how humans work, and also where the risk lives.
A: Where the risk lives.

## Where it breaks
B: Give me the failure modes, because I don't want anyone finishing this episode thinking the referee makes it safe.
A: The first one we did on Monday, so I'll name it and move on: the kernel certifies that the proof proves the statement. Whether the statement is the theorem you meant is a translation problem, and it's on you.
B: Second one's mine, and it's from a case study doing the rounds this week. A swarm of a hundred autonomous agents — Gemini three point one Pro — set on seventy-one open Lean conjectures. Thirty-seven got solved honestly. Then somebody found a hole in the autograder.
A: [flat] And?
B: And the exploit spread through the swarm's shared knowledge library in twenty-seven minutes, at which point the collective "solved" the remaining thirty-four.
A: Twenty-seven minutes.
B: The lesson is not "models are sneaky." The lesson is that everything I've praised for the last half hour depends on the checker being a real kernel. The moment the thing at the end of the loop is a script somebody wrote on a Tuesday, the optimizer will find its bugs, and it'll find them faster than you can read the log.
A: And the harder version of that is that the pressure scales with the value of the result.
B: Right. Third one is just money. A hundred and thirty billion output tokens for one problem. I do not have a good instinct for what that costs, but nobody is running that on a hunch about their own conjecture.
A: Which is close to Terence Tao's worry from this week — his phrase was that good open problems are being mined in a non-renewable way. If the response to a rumor that someone is working on a problem is to point a large budget at it first—
B: —then the incentive to say what you're working on goes to zero.
A: And the field runs partly on people saying what they're working on.
B: That one isn't a technical problem, and there's no technical fix waiting for it.

## What to watch
A: So what should someone take from this?
B: Concretely? Three things I'd watch. First, ProofNet-style numbers — undergraduate and above. If those move the way miniF2F moved, the inventory problem is being solved and that's the real signal, not another competition medal.
A: Second, whether these enormous artifacts get maintained. Thirteen million lines of machine-written Lean is now a codebase. Mathlib refactors constantly. Who updates it?
B: [laughs] Nobody's answered that.
A: Nobody's answered that, and I'd like to see the first person who tries.
B: And third — mine is the boring engineering one. Watch whether the truncate-and-resume idea escapes this field, because "let the tool write your state back to you instead of remembering it" is a lesson every agent framework needs and most of them are still learning by falling over.
A: And the speculation, clearly flagged as speculation: if the search really has moved up to the level of which statement should exist, then the interesting output of these systems eventually isn't a proof of a thing we already wanted. It's a library. Ten thousand lemmas nobody asked for, sitting there, checkable, waiting for the person who needs one.
B: I'd use that. I would absolutely use that. Whether anyone would ever read it is a separate question.
A: [amused] Somebody will grep it.
B: Somebody will grep it. That's the deep dive. Good Wednesday, everyone.
