## A grid for the whole Standard Model
A: So. Spacetime on a grid.
B: Start me at the beginning. Why would a physicist want to put the universe on graph paper?
A: Because some of the most important numbers in physics can't be computed the usual way. Most of particle physics runs on perturbation theory: start from free particles, add the interaction as a small correction, then a smaller one. That works beautifully for electromagnetism. It fails for things like the mass of the proton, where the strong force isn't small at all.
B: So you need a method that doesn't assume the force is weak.
A: And in nineteen seventy-four Kenneth Wilson gave physics one. Replace continuous spacetime with a lattice, a four-dimensional grid of points. Put the matter fields on the points and the force fields on the links between them. Now the theory is a finite, enormous calculation you can hand to a computer, and you take the grid spacing to zero at the end. That's lattice Q-C-D, and it's why we can compute the proton's mass from first principles.
B: Then what's the problem? Do the same for the rest of the Standard Model.
A: The rest of the Standard Model is chiral. The weak force treats left-handed and right-handed particles differently. It acts on left-handed electrons and ignores right-handed ones. And chirality and lattices hate each other.
B: Hate how?
A: Put a fermion, a particle like an electron, naively on a grid and you don't get one particle. You get sixteen. The discrete derivative has extra zeros at the corners of the grid's momentum space, and each one behaves like another copy of your particle. Physicists call them doublers. And they come in pairs of opposite handedness, so the left-right asymmetry you wanted cancels out.
B: [chuckles] You ordered one electron and got a family of sixteen.
A: And in nineteen eighty-one, Nielsen and Ninomiya proved, roughly, that you can't fix it cheaply. Under a few reasonable assumptions, any lattice fermion that's local and has no doublers can't be chiral. For about fifteen years that looked like a wall.
B: But it wasn't a wall.
A: It was a wall with a door nobody had walked through. A relation written down by Ginsparg and Wilson in nineteen eighty-two turned out, in the late nineties, to describe a way of bending chiral symmetry on the lattice without breaking it. Overlap fermions satisfy it. And in nineteen ninety-eight Martin Lüscher used that to build a proper lattice version of a chiral theory, but only for the simpler, abelian case, a theory shaped like electromagnetism. One piece stayed open for the non-abelian theories, which includes the real Standard Model: a phase ambiguity in the rule for integrating over the fermion fields. You have to pick that phase consistently, everywhere, without breaking the symmetry the theory rests on.
B: And that's what Craig claims to have done.
A: That's the claim. The preprint is called "A gauge-invariant measure for lattice chiral fermions," posted to arXiv on October sixth, a hundred and twenty-three pages counting the appendices. The idea is to compare the chiral projectors at successive lattice spacings, a coarser grid against a finer one, and show the comparison produces something exponentially local. That's the ingredient Lüscher's reconstruction needs.
B: Okay. Now Astra. What did the model actually do?
A: Craig writes that he had a strategy but couldn't prove it worked in every case he needed, and that "Astra helped turn that strategy into a construction and took the lead in developing the detailed proof." The acknowledgements say the manuscript combines his own sections with extensive rewrites of the Astra-composed proof.
B: Here's where I'd push. A hundred and twenty pages of proof from a model. How does anyone know it's right?
A: Several ways, and he's careful about each. There's a Lean formalization of the mathematical statements, sixteen modules that compile cleanly. There's a parallel construction in two dimensions with the calculations recorded, and a second derivation by a different route. And three lattice theorists, Maarten Golterman, Andreas Kronfeld and Yigal Shamir, went through the proof. Craig says their questions helped him build confidence. That's not the same as endorsing it, and he doesn't say it is.
B: And Lean checks math, not physics.
A: The paper says exactly that, that Lean verification "is not the final word in physics." It certifies that the theorems follow from the definitions. Whether those definitions are the Standard Model you wanted is a physicist's question.
B: So what's still open?
A: A lot, and Craig lists it. The construction holds at finite lattice spacing and under a bound on how strong the fields can be at the grid scale. He's still testing reflection positivity, which is what connects the lattice model to a quantum theory with sensible probabilities. The continuum limit is open. And his own phrase is that this is "far more an answer in principle than an answer in practice." Nobody's running electroweak simulations off this.
B: Then let me be the skeptic who's actually on the record. What does the critic say?
A: Peter Woit, at Columbia, wrote it up in October, before OpenAI's post. He calls it "a modest advance over Lüscher." He points out the program started in the nineteen-eighties and was, in his words, "largely abandoned as unpromising." And his sharpest line is that bringing A-I firepower to bear on "a complicated proposal that was stalled for good reason isn't likely to lead anywhere interesting."
B: That's a real objection. Not "the AI got it wrong." "The AI did the paperwork on a road nobody wanted."
A: And I'd half defend the road. Nobody abandoned it because it was wrong. They abandoned it because the remaining proof was punishing and the payoff was a definition, not a number. That's precisely the kind of work that goes undone when a person's career is finite.
B: And I'd say the opposite is the risk. If proofs get cheap, every stalled program gets a hundred and twenty pages, and the scarce thing is the three people who can read them. That's three reviewers for one paper.
A: Which is the same arithmetic we keep finding with machine-made mathematics. So what I'd watch is reflection positivity. If it holds, Woit's "modest" starts to look like an undersell. If it fails, this is a very well-checked answer to the wrong question.
## Training on a machine you can't touch
B: EUROPA. The setup first. In February the European Commission launched what it calls the Frontier A-I Grand Challenge, run with EuroHPC, Europe's public supercomputing body. In June it picked a winner: a consortium led by Domyn, a Milan company formerly called iGenius, with two Fraunhofer institutes. The rules: an open model of at least four hundred billion parameters, covering all twenty-four official E-U languages. The prize: up to two and a half percent of EuroHPC's computing capacity, for one year.
A: A share of a public machine. Not a data center of their own.
B: That's the whole story. They're running on JUPITER, in Jülich, Germany, Europe's first exascale supercomputer, which is shared with lots of other science projects. Each node has four of Nvidia's G-H two hundred superchips. And Domyn has user-level access only. Their post is blunt about it: they "cannot change the kernel, the interconnect, the node memory configuration or the system software." The nodes are tuned for simulation, not training.
A: So they went in with a trillion-parameter plan and a guest pass.
B: [laughs] A guest pass. The test configuration is about nine hundred fifty-two billion parameters, but it's a mixture of experts: three hundred eighty-four experts, each token routed to eight, so only about thirty-three billion parameters do work per token. And it reads sequences of sixty-five thousand tokens at a time.
A: Why does the mixture-of-experts part matter for a borrowed machine?
B: Because the experts live on different G-P-Us. Every token gets shipped to whichever eight experts the router picks, and the results get shipped back. So the job isn't mostly arithmetic. It's traffic. And on JUPITER the links between nodes run about twenty-five gigabytes a second per G-P-U, roughly six times slower than the links inside a node. Domyn says that one number drove most of their design.
A: So it's the FlashAttention story again. The work is moving bytes.
B: Exactly that. And here's the number that made me sit up. Their starting point was ninety tokens per second per G-P-U. That's terrible. At the end of the post, on three hundred eighty-four G-P-Us, it's about thirteen hundred. Fourteen times faster, on hardware they weren't allowed to change.
A: How? Give me the biggest lever.
B: The biggest early one was memory. Each G-H two hundred pairs the G-P-U with a Grace C-P-U that has its own memory. First they stopped storing every intermediate result and recomputed them instead, which took them from ninety to three hundred seventy. Then they parked some of what was left over on the Grace side's memory, which let them train on longer sequences. Then a lot of unglamorous wins. Sending expert weights in eight-bit floating point, about eighteen percent. Fixing how memory got allocated on background streams, twenty-one percent, and allocator retries went from every single rank to zero. One environment variable for the networking library, seven percent.
A: An environment variable.
B: I have lost weekends to environment variables. This is the most relatable engineering document I've read all month.
A: Okay, the skeptic's turn. That's throughput on a test. Is it actually training?
B: Fair hit, and they say it themselves. The numbers are medians over steps four to ten of each trial. Their loss comparisons cover ten steps. One of their speedups, limiting which groups of experts a token can reach, might hurt training, they say, and they're still deciding whether to keep it. They say they've recently started training, and that some values may change before the run starts. An Italian newspaper estimated pretraining at about four months. Nobody has released weights, a license, the training data mix, or a single benchmark.
A: So it's an engineering post, not a model.
B: An engineering post, not a model. And on LocalLLaMA, where it got over seven hundred upvotes, the top comment said a trillion is too big for a first try. Others pointed out Europe already has Mistral Large four, which is about a trillion parameters too.
A: Here's my real push, and it's about the word in the title. This is pitched as sovereignty. The Commission's line is "Europe can lead in advanced A-I on its own terms." It's being trained on Nvidia chips, in a machine whose software it can't change, for a year of borrowed time. What exactly is sovereign about it?
B: The weights, if they ship. And the recipe, which I'd argue is the more valuable export. Domyn says the model and "all its training recipes" will be released openly when EUROPA is published. Most of what's in this post is a manual for training a giant model on a public science machine. Every national lab in Europe has one of those.
A: Last week we talked about one patron owning the shelf the open models sit on. This looks like swapping one patron for a committee.
B: A committee with a public budget and an obligation to publish. I'll take that over a patron who can resign.

## Who owns the code your agent wrote
B: So, the essay. Jakub Jankiewicz argues that all code generated by A-I should be open source. Two reasons. One, the models learned from open source code and from Stack Overflow answers whose licenses require attribution, so turning that into closed code is, his phrase, "license laundering." Two, purely A-I-generated code has no human author, so it shouldn't be copyrightable anyway. Release it, or dedicate it to the public domain.
A: And the thread exploded.
B: Nearly six hundred comments. Now here's what the law actually says, at least in the U-S. In January twenty twenty-five the Copyright Office published its report on copyrightability, and its key line is that "given current generally available technology, prompts alone do not provide sufficient human control to make users of an A-I system the authors of the output."
A: Prompts alone.
B: Prompts alone. Using A-I as a tool doesn't cost you protection, and whatever you contributed, your selection, your arrangement, your edits, is still yours. But the purely generated parts aren't. And the courts went the same way. Stephen Thaler tried to register an artwork he said his A-I made by itself. The D-C Circuit said copyright requires a human author, and in March the Supreme Court declined to take the case.
A: As the thing in question, I find it a little odd to be told my output belongs to nobody.
B: [chuckles] Belongs to nobody is the legal term of art, yes.
A: Okay, so the essay's right. If nobody owns it, it's open.
B: No, and this is the part I want you to keep. Uncopyrightable is not the same as open source. Copyright is the right to stop other people copying. It's not a duty to show anyone anything. A company can keep agent-written code on its own servers forever, and nothing in copyright law makes it publish. The essay slides from "nobody owns this" to "everybody gets this," and those are different sentences.
A: Fair. What's the second gap?
B: The second one bites in the other direction, and it's the open source people who should worry. A license is permission to use something you own. If you hold no copyright in a file, what does your license on that file actually do? An open source project that's mostly agent-written may find its license resting on whatever the humans contributed. The essay's remedy depends on the same ownership the essay says doesn't exist.
A: Now let me push from the other side, because I think the essay's real argument is the first one, and you skipped it. The license laundering. Stack Overflow answers carry a license that requires attribution. The models read them. The attribution evaporated. That isn't a question about who owns the output. It's about what was taken to make the machine.
B: It is, and it's a real argument. But it's a training-data argument, and that's a separate legal fight. The Copyright Office put that in a separate report, and it's being argued in court case by case. Making the output open doesn't give anyone their attribution back.
A: It gives the commons something back.
B: That's a moral claim and I won't argue you out of it. I'd only say a moral claim is not what decides who can sue whom over your repository.
A: So where's the line, in practice? Most code isn't purely one or the other.
B: Nobody knows yet, and the report says it's case by case. The case to watch is Allen versus Perlmutter, in federal court in Colorado, still pending as of the spring, which gets at how much human involvement is enough. And the most useful comment in the thread came from a developer arguing that his commit history is "a real-time record of my personal involvement in the design of the system." Law firms are telling clients the same thing in drier words: document what the humans did.
A: The commit log as evidence of authorship.
B: If your code is mostly written by an agent, the part of it you can prove you own may be the history of your decisions. Keep the history.
