What a place does

Ada: Google Research, yesterday. Maria Despoina Siampou and Shushman Choudhury, with colleagues at Google and U S C. The question is one I find genuinely lovely: what does a language model actually know about a place?

Alan: It knows what the internet says about the place.

Ada: It knows the label. And the label is thin. "Coffee shop" tells you almost nothing about whether it's four people with laptops all day or a queue of forty between seven and nine. Their sentence for it is that our collective actions at a physical place are often far more descriptive than the formal words used to label it.

Alan: So the input is foot traffic.

Ada: Aggregated, anonymized visit patterns, yes. And the method has three parts. First, visit alignment. A temporal encoder takes arrival windows, departure trends and stay durations and turns them into a dense vector — they call the result a functional centroid, capturing the yearly and weekly rhythm of the place.

Alan: A shape in time instead of a word.

Ada: A shape in time. Second, and this is the part that solves the real problem: spatial multiscale visit propagation. Most places are data-sparse — the long tail of small businesses nobody's phone lingers at. So they transfer aggregated visit patterns from data-rich neighbors to data-poor locations, at several geographic scales at once.

Alan: Borrowing the block's rhythm for the shop you have nothing on.

Ada: Borrowing the block's rhythm, and the neighborhood's, and so on up. Third, text-mobility synergy: they align the language embedding with the mobility vector by maximizing cosine similarity between them, so the semantic meaning survives and the operational behavior gets folded in.

Alan: Numbers.

Ada: Five prediction tasks on entirely unseen locations — trained on observed places, evaluated on places held out. Visit intent classification: eighty-one point nine percent relative gain. Price level: seventy-five point one percent improvement. Busyness forecasting: twenty-four point seven percent more accurate. Plus opening and closing hours, and detecting permanent closure.

Alan: Relative gain over what?

Ada: Text-only embeddings — Gemini embeddings — plus trajectory-based geospatial models, they name TrajGPT, plus hybrid variants.

Alan: Then eighty-one point nine percent relative tells me almost nothing by itself, and the absolute numbers aren't in the post. A large relative gain over a weak baseline and a large relative gain over a strong one are different results, and they read identically.

Ada: Agreed, and I'd say the same. Take the direction, not the magnitude.

Alan: My bigger problem isn't the numbers. It's that I read this and there is nothing for me to do on Monday. Two metro areas — Los Angeles and Houston.
