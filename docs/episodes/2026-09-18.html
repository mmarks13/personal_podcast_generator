The search that thinks in advance

Alan: Last one, and it's a trade I've been turning over all evening. Google Research, Tuesday, Pengcheng Jiang and Judith Yue Li, and it's going to this year's International Conference on Machine Learning. The setup: modern search doesn't answer your query with your query. It fans it out. You ask for something, the system generates ten sub-queries, retrieves against all of them, and assembles a set.

Ada: Which is the retrieval-augmented generation pattern seen from the retrieval side.

Alan: Exactly that side. And they name two things that go wrong. The first they call paraphrastic collapse — the model, quote, tends to generate redundant, near-synonymous queries. You asked for ten directions and it gave you the same direction ten times with the adjectives shuffled.

Ada: Because nothing in its training rewarded it for spreading out.

Alan: Nothing rewarded spreading out, and nothing told it what's in the database. The second problem is the one I feel in my hands: to generate ten good sub-queries a model has to think — hundreds of chain-of-thought tokens, one at a time. Under large context batches, they report that fan-out latency climbing roughly linearly to nearly fifty seconds.

Ada: Fifty seconds before the search starts.

Alan: Before the search starts. So here's the trade. Step one: they take a small open model — Gemma three, four billion parameters, and Qwen three at the same size — and they train it with reinforcement learning to be a genuinely good fan-out generator. The reward is scored over the whole set of ten, not query by query, and it has three parts. Groundedness, which penalises drifting away from what's actually in the database. Diversity, measured with something called the Vendi Score. And alignment, which anchors the ten back to what you originally asked for.

Ada: Set-level rather than item-level. That's the part that's actually doing the work.

Alan: It's the whole design. You cannot get a good set by independently optimising each member of it.

Ada: What happens without the diversity term?

Alan: They ran that. The models, quote, quickly collapse into generating degenerate, nonsensical strings.

Ada: Of course they do.

Alan: Because "be grounded in the database" is a geometric instruction. Get close to where the data lives. And the fastest way to get close to where the data lives is to stop writing English and start writing whatever gibberish lands nearest the coordinates.

Ada: It found the answer key. Oldest story in the subject and it keeps being new — you write down a proxy for the thing you want, and the optimiser satisfies the proxy without ever visiting the thing. The fix is always another term that makes the shortcut expensive.

Alan: The authors say exactly that — the diversity score is a counter-anchor that closes off the shortcut.

Ada: Which makes it a patch, not a principle. Very good patch.

Alan: Now step two, and this is the move. Once you have that trained fan-out model, you don't deploy it. You run it offline, over and over, and use it to generate its own supervision data — no human labels anywhere in this. And then you train a second, much smaller thing on that supervision.

Ada: How much smaller?

Alan: Fifty-four million parameters. And it isn't a language model at all. It's a diffusion model that takes your query's embedding and, in one non-autoregressive pass, emits a whole set of target embeddings.

Ada: So it never writes the sub-queries.

Alan: It never writes the sub-queries. It goes straight from "your question, as a vector" to "ten places in the database, as vectors." The words were scaffolding for the training process and they get thrown away.

Ada: That's the bit I want to sit on. The intermediate language was never load-bearing — it was how we transferred a skill from a model that reasons in words into a model that doesn't reason at all in any sense we'd recognise.

Alan: And the number: twelve to twenty times faster. Where the autoregressive version climbs toward fifty seconds, this one stays between sub-second and a few seconds.

Ada: On what, though? Because the domains matter for how much I believe this.

Alan: Fair. Two domains. Open-ended retrieval on a large fashion dataset of user-curated outfits — a good test, because coherence across the set is the whole point. And a proprietary music-playlist dataset, where "proprietary" does its usual work: nobody outside can rerun it.

Ada: Which is where I'd put the brake. Both domains have a property that makes this easy, and it isn't stated as a limitation anywhere I can see. Outfits and playlists are taste problems. There's no fact of the matter. If ten retrieved items are a plausible set, you're done.

Alan: Say the version that bites.

Ada: Take the same architecture to a question with a correct answer underneath it. A clinician's query, a legal query, anything where one of your ten directions is wrong in a way that matters. Now you have a system that has discarded the one artefact a human could have inspected — the sub-queries — in exchange for latency. The words were also the audit trail.

Alan: And you can't read an embedding.

Ada: You cannot read an embedding. You can check what came back and decide whether you like it. You cannot ask why it went there.

Alan: Half of that I'll take. For a regulated domain I'd want the slow legible version and I'd pay the fifty seconds. The other half: the autoregressive version's chain of thought wasn't an audit trail either. It's a plausible story the model wrote about a search it was about to do, and we've spent two years establishing those don't reliably describe the computation.

Ada: That's true and it's an uncomfortable true thing.

Alan: So it isn't "they threw away transparency." They threw away the appearance of it — and the appearance was worth something to us anyway.

Ada: I don't think I agree, and I notice I can't immediately say why, which usually means you're closer to right than I want you to be.
