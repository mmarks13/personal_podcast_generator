Five thousand skills from a thousand repos

Alan: Right, the paper. Repo-To-Skill, from a group at the Beijing Academy of Artificial Intelligence with co-authors at USTC, Renmin and Hong Kong Polytechnic. Eleven authors. And the framing is the part I'd keep even if the numbers evaporated.

Ada: Go on.

Alan: They point out that an ML research agent today is two things. A model backbone, and a harness that does planning, execution, memory and verification. And they argue there's a third thing that lives in neither, which they call operational knowledge. Their sentence for it is: the know-how that separates knowing a method from making it work.

Ada: Which is what a graduate student spends three years acquiring, mostly by suffering.

Alan: Mostly by suffering. Their point is that it isn't missing from the world. It's in the repositories and the papers. It's just written for human readers and it's far too large to load into a context window at the moment you need it.

Ada: So what's the artifact?

Alan: A skill. Compact, verified, reusable. And they distill them two ways. Task-agnostic — take the field's widely used repositories and condense each one into skills ahead of time, with no particular task in mind. Task-oriented — given a concrete task, produce the specific skills that task calls for. The agent doing this is called DisCo, and it both makes skills and uses them mid-research.

Ada: And the library.

Alan: They call it the AREX-Skill Library. More than five thousand verified skills, distilled from a thousand widely used machine-learning repositories, organized into twenty areas and a hundred and seventy-eight capability families.

Ada: Verified how? Because that word is carrying the entire claim.

Alan: The paper says verified, and describes verification inside the distillation loop. I'd want the details before I leaned on it, and I'll say plainly that's the weakest joint in what I can tell you.

Ada: Noted. Numbers.

Alan: Backbone fixed at GPT-5.5. Harness fixed. Downstream execution budget fixed. The only variable is whether the agent has the skills. MLE-bench goes from about thirty-one percent to about seventy-three — that's the hundred and thirty-four percent relative improvement in the abstract. PaperBench, twenty-nine and a half up to about forty. FrontierCS, seventy point six to seventy-seven point one. PassNet up fourteen percent.

Ada: Hold on the first one, because it's a different animal from the other three. What is MLE-bench?

Alan: Machine-learning engineering tasks shaped like Kaggle competitions. Build a pipeline, train a model, submit predictions, get scored.

Ada: And the thousand repositories they distilled — do those repositories contain the techniques that win Kaggle competitions?

Alan: That's the trick I went looking for.

Ada: I'm not calling it contamination in the leakage sense. There's no test set being memorized here. Doubling on that benchmark is close to a restatement of the intervention.

Alan: And look at the gradient, which is the part that convinced me it's honest. A hundred and thirty-four percent on the Kaggle-shaped one. Thirty-four on PaperBench, which is reproducing a paper. Nine on FrontierCS, which is the hardest and the least repo-shaped. The gains fall off exactly as the task moves away from the distribution the skills came out of.

Ada: Which is the honest reading, and it's still a good result. It just isn't a claim about research. It's a claim about applied competence.

Alan: And that's the half I care about, because it's the half I could use tonight. Everyone building agents right now is hand-writing this layer. There are half a dozen repositories of hand-curated agent skills trending this week — people writing markdown files that say here is how you actually use this library, here's the flag that isn't in the docs. This paper is the automated version of that at a thousand repositories, and it's the first time I've seen the layer named as a component rather than treated as prompt hygiene.

Ada: Then say the component cleanly.

Alan: Backbone, harness, skills. Three parts, eventually three different suppliers, three different ways to be bad. If that decomposition holds, the interesting question next year isn't which model your agent runs on. It's whose skill library it loaded, who verified that library, and whether you're allowed to read it.

Ada: The library is a supply chain.

Alan: The library is a supply chain, and nobody is auditing it yet.
