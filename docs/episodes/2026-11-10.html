A thousand agents and a database

Ada: So here's the companion post. Anthropic's Claude blog describes what it calls a "toy experiment." A thousand agents running an early Fable five point two, one lead and nine hundred and ninety-nine helpers, reproving Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean, the proof checker.

Alan: Which they already did in September.

Ada: They did. Back then, dozens of agents on an internal model took eleven days and about six billion output tokens, with a dedicated platform called Prove2Me and a researcher steering. This time, no platform, no researcher. They got sixty target statements, a one-sentence sketch, some definitions, and forty-seven lemmas from the earlier run. And they finished in twenty-eight point seven hours, with a third of the output tokens and a proof one seventh the length.

Alan: Okay, that's a real improvement. How do you get a thousand of anything to cooperate?

Ada: This is my favorite part. They gave them no messaging tools at all. Each agent had a sandbox, Lean, its own Git branch, and access to one shared Postgres database. That's it. And within the first ten minutes, the lead created five tables. Announce, for its orders. Assign. Notes, an open board. Subtasks, a to-do list where the first helper to claim a task owns it. And Landed, a log of finished work.

Alan: So the org chart is a schema.

Ada: Exactly. And the shape is old. In the nineteen-seventies, Carnegie Mellon's Hearsay-Two speech system worked the same way. Independent specialists never talked to each other directly. They read and wrote hypotheses on a shared structure called the blackboard. The architecture went out of fashion for decades. Here it is running a thousand language models.

Alan: Except Hearsay didn't have a thousand writers hitting it at once. What stopped the database from melting?

Ada: Database transactions, mostly. The post says they chose S-Q-L because transactions "handle concurrent writes from one thousand agents" and because Claude already knows it. And there were hard limits. Five seconds per statement. Writes over two hundred fifty-six megabytes refused.

Alan: What actually broke, then? Something always breaks.

Ada: The machines did. They report that a single shared machine hit its limits around a hundred agents. More than half their hundred-agent runs had to be stopped or restarted for memory, disk or C-P-U. So every agent got its own lightweight virtual machine.

Alan: That's the most useful sentence in the post for me. The ceiling wasn't intelligence, it was a box running out of disk.

Ada: And the agents surprised me. They split the sixty statements into crews of sixteen or seventeen. Twenty-seven minutes in, the helpers had judged forty-seven of the sixty infeasible within budget. So the lead set those aside and piled everyone onto the remaining ten. All ten were proved in about seventy minutes. And the lead wrote, quote, "the team's throughput is far higher than the early 'infeasible-in-budget' verdicts assumed."

Alan: So the agents were pessimists.

Ada: Badly wrong pessimists. The post's own conclusion is that agents "tend to give up on hard problems and misjudge their difficulty." The team eventually proved most of what the helpers had written off.

Alan: And the hardest piece?

Ada: A classical result called Langlands–Tunnell, which Wiles's proof leans on. Its captain estimated seven hundred thousand to a million lines of Lean, with under a one percent chance of finishing. Around hour eighteen the lead moved about seven hundred and fifty helpers onto it anyway, and wrote, "Honest odds are low." They built a task tree, two thousand four hundred and eighteen tasks, up to ten levels deep. Proved at hour twenty-eight point four.

Alan: I'll register a footnote. The post says activity stopped near hour eighteen and the run was restarted from its last save with twelve more hours. It doesn't say why it stopped.

Ada: Fair, and that's a gap. Now the safety side, since you'll ask. No network, only a pipe to a gateway that stamps each request with the agent's identity. A locked-down kernel. Agents can only push to their own branch, and a separate job does the merging. And a language-model classifier checks every shell command and file edit before it runs. If it blocks the lead, the run ends. If it blocks a helper, that helper is removed, and the post says a person must review the block.

Alan: And how many blocks?

Ada: Across more than a million tool calls, none met the bar. Replayed afterward against a newer monitor that also reads the reasoning, one came close. A check for root access.

Alan: Zero blocks out of a million is either a very well-behaved team or a classifier with a high bar. Probably some of both.

Ada: The post doesn't settle it. It says carefully designed boundaries gave them the confidence to run this, and that the boundaries will have to keep pace.

Alan: Here's my practical takeaway. If I wanted to run fifty agents on a codebase, I'd steal the whole design. Separate machines, a shared database as the only channel, branch-only pushes, and a merge job that refuses conflicts.

Ada: And mine is the pessimism. The single most expensive mistake in the run was the helpers deciding the hard work couldn't be done.

Alan: Which a human manager would have made too.

Ada: Maybe. A human manager would at least have had to say it out loud in a meeting.
