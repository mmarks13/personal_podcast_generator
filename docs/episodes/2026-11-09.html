When an agent builds the equations

Ada: Let me start with a puzzle that's seventy-odd years old. In the early nineteen-fifties, Hodgkin and Huxley wrote down the equations for how a nerve cell fires, using a squid's giant axon. To make the math match their measurements, they had to raise one variable to the fourth power. And they couldn't fully say why.

Alan: A fudge factor.

Ada: A fudge factor that turned out to be a fact. Decades later, in the post's words, "another team found the structure of a related potassium channel protein," and "it had four identical pieces." The fourth power was the channel. The model knew something about the cell before anybody could see it.

Alan: That's a lovely story. What does it have to do with agents?

Ada: Everything, according to Jakob Macke, Daniel Gedon and Stefan Wahl, at the ELLIS Institute and the University of Tübingen. They're grantees of Anthropic's A-I for Science program, writing a guest post on its site. Their point is that if an A-I had simply handed Hodgkin and Huxley a model that fit, they'd have had the answer and missed the insight. They open with Richard Hamming, from nineteen sixty-two. "The purpose of computing is insight, not numbers."

Alan: Okay. So what's the actual proposal?

Ada: They split modeling into three jobs. Building the model, which means choosing its parts and the form of its equations. Constraining it, which is tuning the parameters to the data. And interpreting it, asking what it says about the real thing. Machines are getting good at the first two. The third is the hard one.

Alan: Start with constraining. That's the part I'd actually run.

Ada: Then here's their analogy. "Imagine you know that a group dinner added up to one hundred twenty dollars, but not who ordered what." Lots of combinations of orders give you that total. Nerve models are the same. Many different settings of the parameters produce nearly the same electrical activity.

Alan: So fitting gives you one combination out of many and you don't know which.

Ada: Which is why the honest answer is a distribution over all the plausible ones. Classic Bayesian inference gives you that, but it gets impractical for complicated simulators. So they use simulation-based inference. You run the simulator a huge number of times, in their words "perhaps one hundred thousand times for a model like Hodgkin-Huxley." You train a neural network to map from simulated outputs back to the parameters that could have produced them. And then, "a trained network can perform inference on new data in seconds."

Alan: So you pay the bill once and look things up forever.

Ada: Exactly. And it's not hypothetical. They list it in use on neutron star collisions, cosmology, structural biology, geophysics.

Alan: Fine, that's constraining. Where do the agents come in?

Ada: Building. Agents built on large language models can now write candidate models, fit them, compare them against data, and revise. The authors say several such systems have appeared in the past two years. And their argument is about what those agents should return. "A-I agents can thus provide a distribution of plausible models, rather than a single best fit."

Alan: Lots of models instead of one.

Ada: Their illustration has an ensemble falling into four clusters, about forty-one percent, thirty-nine, eleven and nine. And here's the line that makes it science instead of curve-fitting. "If the survivors disagree, that shows where the evidence is thin." That's your next experiment.

Alan: Then a builder's worry. Every agent writing those candidate models learned from the same literature. Ask ten of them for a nerve-cell model and you might get ten variations on the textbook. The disagreement you see might be much narrower than the disagreement nature allows.

Ada: That's fair, and they half admit it. "So far, these systems have mostly been tested on rediscovering models that scientists already know."

Alan: Which is the easy case for exactly my reason. The answer is in the training data.

Ada: It's also the only case where you can grade them.

Alan: Sure. But a rediscovery benchmark can't tell you whether the ensemble is wide enough. You'd need a system where nobody knows the answer, and then you can't score it.

Ada: And that's where they're heading. They point out that a complete wiring diagram of the fruit fly brain now exists, over a hundred thousand neurons. For systems that size, they don't expect a single correct model. The goal becomes finding the properties that every model compatible with the data shares. They note climate science and particle physics already work that way.

Alan: Okay, that I like. Stop asking for the model, and ask what all the surviving models agree on.

Ada: And their ask of the machines is concrete. Deliver "an account of which parts are pinned down by the data, which are still open," and "which alternatives failed along the way."

Keeping score on the person

Alan: This paper is fifty-nine pages, one author, Hao Fei, posted to arXiv on November third. It's a position paper, and it says so. No new benchmark numbers, no new algorithm. It drew about a hundred and twenty upvotes on Hugging Face, and I think it's worth time because it argues with how all of us keep score.

Ada: Go on.

Alan: The opening claim is that progress in A-I has been measured by what a machine does on its own. The author calls that one half of the endgame. The other half is A-I-augmented human intelligence, which asks "how much intelligence a machine can enable in a particular person." And the form it imagines is a cognitive symbiont, a persistent A-I that senses what you sense, keeps your life record, models your situation, and joins your thinking at the right moment.

Ada: That's a big phrase. What's the problem it's supposed to solve?

Alan: The paper calls it the cognitive gap. "The price every person pays, every day," in what they forgot, failed to notice, couldn't think through, or couldn't put into words. Its example is a meeting where a colleague asks about a result she published three years ago, and she can't recall the number.

Ada: I'd like that one fixed for several professors I could name. I won't.

Alan: And the diagnosis is that the bottleneck isn't model capability anymore. It's coupling, between the A-I and one specific person. Three symptoms. You keep re-explaining your context. Help shows up late or at the wrong moment. And you don't trust it with anything that matters.

Ada: Here's where I come in, because this is a very old idea. The paper says so itself. It cites Licklider in nineteen sixty, who "imagined people and computers thinking together in real time." And Engelbart two years later, who "made the unit of augmentation the person together with their tools and methods." That's the whole thesis, sixty-odd years ago.

Alan: It is, and the author isn't hiding it. So what's new?

Ada: You tell me. You've read the fifty-nine pages.

Alan: The evaluation, and that's the part I'd defend. Section seventeen says, "The unit of evaluation is one person." Then it splits the question three ways. Augmentation is how much better you do with the symbiont than without. Synergy is whether you and the machine together beat the better of the two alone. And it cites a meta-analysis finding that human-and-A-I combinations are often worse than the best of the two.

Ada: Which is an inconvenient thing to cite in your own position paper.

Alan: That's why I trust it a bit more. And the third is retention. Are you stronger, unaided, after a period of use, once the symbiont is taken away?

Ada: That's the one. That's the test this whole field keeps skipping.

Alan: And it designs the controls carefully. To measure what personal context adds, you hold the task and the base model fixed and vary only the context. The control is never "no A-I," because then you'd credit the model's raw capability to the relationship.

Ada: Fine, that's well built. Now my problem. Last month we covered a study where people who'd had an assistant for a few problems gave up sooner once it was taken away. A little help, and they persisted less. This paper proposes help that's always on, lifelong, and proactive.

Alan: And it knows. There's a section on exactly that, and it doesn't flinch. It says the old automation lesson "still applies." The operator relieved of a task "loses the skill to take it back." Its phrase for the default is "harm by default and protection only by design."

Ada: So the design has to carry all the weight.

Alan: Which is why the falsification list is the best page in the paper. It names what would prove it wrong. If "more personal context brings no measurable gain on most tasks." If "long-term use generally erodes abilities people want to keep." And one I didn't expect, if "every change of backbone loses the relationship and cannot be prevented."

Ada: Explain that one.

Alan: Swap the underlying model, and the thing that knew you is gone. Anyone who's had a tool change personality after an update knows the feeling. The paper treats it as a reason the whole idea could fail.

Ada: And the people standing around you? Something that senses everything you sense is also recording them.

Alan: It has a rule for that. "Collection must be perceptible to the people nearby." There's even a table asking whether a bystander can tell. Whether anyone would build it that way is another matter.

Ada: So what would you build first?

Alan: The paper's own roadmap starts on the desktop. A streaming copilot, before any wearables. And it reminds you the Humane A-I Pin was discontinued within a year, as evidence a new device has to offer something your phone doesn't. That's my bet too. Start where the record already is, on the screen.

Ada: And then run the retention test before anything else.

Alan: On one person, over months. Nobody has run it at the scale this paper imagines.

Ada: Then that's the result I'd wait for. Not what the symbiont can do. What I can do once it's gone.
