A stopwatch that never wobbles

Alan: So. Claude dot A-I. The post is by Raymond Wang, Sam Attard and a third engineer, and the numbers first, because they're fun. All at the seventy-fifth percentile. A fresh load of claude dot A-I went from about three point one seconds to five hundred and fifty milliseconds. Sending a message in the Cowork desktop app went from nine hundred and twenty-eight milliseconds to forty-eight.

Ada: Forty-eight. That's a nineteen-times change on one path.

Alan: It is. Across all thirteen journeys they measured, the geometric mean is three point one times faster. Two-week sprint, back in August.

Ada: Fine, it's faster. What's the thing you said I could steal?

Alan: The measurement stack. They had wall-clock timing from real users, which is the number that matters to people and is also hopeless for an agent to optimise against, because it's noisy. Your laptop was warm, the network hiccuped, a user had forty tabs open. If you change one function and the page gets two percent faster, you can't see two percent through that noise.

Ada: So you need a number that doesn't move unless the code moves.

Alan: Exactly that. They ran the code under Valgrind — an old tool that counts every machine instruction executed — with Node in a predictable mode, so the same code produces the same count every single time. No warm laptop, no network. And then the step people skip: they checked that the count actually predicts the real thing. It does. One fix cut the instructions spent assembling a conversation's message tree by forty-eight percent, and the wall-clock speed-up in the field came out at seventy-eight percent.

Ada: That's lovely. An instruction count is a terrible description of user experience and a perfect thing to hill-climb on, provided you've checked the two move together. Profilers have been making that trade since before either of us was a gleam in anyone's gradient.

Alan: And here's why it matters now rather than in nineteen-ninety. A human engineer doesn't need a deterministic number as badly — they have intuition to paper over the noise. An agent working overnight, on a hundred and fifty threads at once, needs a number it can't fool itself with. Their loop was: an engineer posts a screen recording of something slow, Claude traces it and builds a reproducible benchmark, opens a few pull requests tiered by risk, ships them behind a feature flag, watches the field data, and then either ratchets the benchmark's ceiling down — so the speed-up can never quietly regress — or turns the flag off.

Ada: The ratchet is the clever bit.

Alan: The ratchet is the clever bit. Every win becomes a test.

Thirty billion parameters, three billion at a time

Alan: Qualcomm. Two chips announced on Tuesday — the Snapdragon eight Elite Gen six and the eight Elite Extreme Gen six. Same processor cores, two-nanometre, top cores up to five gigahertz. The difference that matters is on the Extreme, and it's the thirty-billion-parameter claim.

Ada: Which, before you teach it, sounds preposterous. Thirty billion parameters on a phone.

Alan: It sounds preposterous if you picture a dense model. So let me split the problem in two, because running a language model on anything small is two separate problems that people mash together. The first is capacity: can the weights fit anywhere? The second is bandwidth: every time you generate a token, how many bytes do you have to drag from memory into the chip?

Ada: And on a phone the second one is the killer.

Alan: On a phone, generating text is almost entirely a bandwidth problem. The arithmetic is cheap; hauling the weights is expensive. A dense thirty-billion model hauls all thirty billion per token. A mixture-of-experts model has all thirty billion sitting there, but a router picks a few specialist sub-networks per token, and only those get read. RCR Tech's write-up puts it at around three billion active at any moment.

Ada: So per token it behaves like a three-billion model.

Alan: Per token, roughly. That's the whole trick. Mixture-of-experts doesn't shrink the model. It shrinks the part you touch each step. Which means the bill moves almost entirely onto capacity. My back-of-envelope, not Qualcomm's: thirty billion parameters at four bits each is about fifteen gigabytes of weights.

Ada: Fifteen gigabytes. On which phone?

Alan: That's the first thing I couldn't find. Nothing I read says how much memory the phones will ship with, or whether all the weights sit in memory at once or get paged in from storage.

Ada: Then what did Qualcomm actually change? Because a chip announcement that's really a memory announcement should say so.

Alan: It kind of does. The headline change on the Extreme is a fifty percent larger shared memory for the neural processing unit — the part built for this. RCR calls it arguably the most important change, and the reason is the working state. The activations, and the key-value cache that holds your conversation so far, can stay on the chip instead of shuttling out to the phone's main memory and back. Both chips support contexts up to thirty-two thousand tokens, and Qualcomm claims up to fifty percent faster prompt processing for four-bit models on the Extreme.

Ada: And the smaller chip gets what?

Alan: Android Authority's comparison lists it as "standard agentic A-I." The standard chip is headed for mainstream flagships around eight hundred dollars; the Extreme for the Pro Max and Ultra tier. So the thirty-billion capability is a luxury feature on day one.

Ada: I'll push on the premise, then, rather than the numbers. Who is this for? You could send the same request to a data centre for a fraction of a cent. We spent yesterday on a model at ten cents a million tokens.

Alan: Three reasons, and I'll rank them. Privacy is the obvious one and the weakest — people say they want it and rarely pay for it. Latency is real for voice. That's a model watching context all day for almost no battery. You can't do that through a network.

Ada: That's the most interesting sentence you've said, and it's also the one I'd want most scrutiny on. A phone that builds a memory of your usage all day — TechCrunch's phrasing — is a design decision about what gets remembered, not a chip specification.

Alan: Agreed. And RCR makes the same point more bluntly: a faster N-P-U fixes none of the hallucination, tool misuse, privacy or accountability problems — those are model and policy problems.
