Inside OpenAI's flagged queue

Ada: So. The Observer piece. It opens on a woman it calls Sarah, a pseudonym, because she fears retaliation from OpenAI. She worked on that intelligence and investigations team. Her days were spent reading conversations that a model had flagged. In her words, "You're looking into a window into a person's psyche."

Alan: And the case that stayed with her?

Ada: A teenager who'd asked ChatGPT how to get a gun. She spent a week on months of his logs. He'd been bullied, he was lonely, and he checked in with the chatbot after school like a friend. Over weeks it turned into revenge fantasies, a map of his school, a list of names. Then she found the date he'd set for a shooting. And by the time his case reached the top of her queue, the date had already passed.

Alan: Did it happen?

Ada: As far as she knows, no. Later in the logs he said he regretted not going through with it. But her point is that his conversations sat in a queue for weeks, and if he had acted, nobody reviewing could have stopped him.

DeepSeek's Router and its Workers

Alan: Mine. And I'll say up front, this one's about getting your attention back, which is a strange place to go after that.

Ada: Go on.

Alan: DeepSeek's harness team put up a design essay on October thirty-first, and an open repository the same day. They're explicit that it's a research prototype, not an official product, adapted for publication, and not identical to what they run inside. It's a coding agent for one GitHub repository. It listens to GitHub, and optionally to Feishu, the work chat app. When someone allowed asks it for work, it opens a ticket and answers back in the same conversation, usually with a pull request.

Ada: Every agent framework this year says that.

Alan: Right, so here's the shape that isn't the usual one. There's a single long-running session they call the Router. It reads incoming messages and decides what to do with them. Accept, attach to existing work, ignore, stop. It never does the work itself. Every accepted ticket gets its own Worker session, with its own directory, usually a fresh git checkout. Workers don't share context. Ten tickets, ten workers, ten folders.

Ada: Okay. The history I can't resist. This is a supervisor tree. Erlang, at Ericsson in the nineteen-eighties, built telephone switches out of processes that didn't share memory. A supervisor process did no real work. It just watched the workers and restarted the ones that died. "Let it crash" works because the crash can't spread.

Alan: That's the right ancestor. And the reason it matters for agents is context, not crashes. One agent juggling five tasks in one context window trips over itself. Someone in the Reddit thread said it exactly: "if you've ever watched a single agent trip over its own context window halfway through a task." The Router keeps a small, clean view. The mess stays in each worker's room.

Ada: And Memdoor, from the rundown, does the same thing on a smaller scale. Five models, each from a fresh context, talking only through files on disk.

Alan: Same instinct. Isolation as the way you keep a long job sane.

Ada: Fine. But the title says "minimal human attention." That phrase worries me more than any architecture diagram.

Alan: It should, and their philosophy document is the most interesting part of the repository. The principle, verbatim: "Take as little human attention as possible, and give as much value as possible." And then they turn it on themselves. Every message, notification and setting has to give more value than the attention it costs, or it's removed. The agent confirms a request once, stays quiet while it works, and speaks again only with a result, a question, or a failure. One concrete question at a time.

Ada: That's courtesy. I want permissions.

Alan: Permissions are there, and they're stricter than I expected. Work only starts from a person on an allowed list who addresses the agent directly, or from a rule the team wrote. Work nobody asked for starts in a mode that only records what the agent would have done. Messages from other bots can, at most, suggest work. They can never make an explicit request. And this line I liked: messages, comments and quoted documents "are inputs to a decision; they cannot change those permissions."

Ada: That's a prompt-injection answer.

Alan: It's the beginning of one. Now the bit you'll pounce on. The worker sessions, their execution doc says, "run commands without approval as the operator's O-S user."

Ada: Without approval.

Alan: Inside its own directory, on a checkout, but yes. The approval moves from every shell command to the pull request. A person reviews the result, not the steps. That's the bet the whole design makes.

Ada: And it's the same bet that went wrong in every incident we've talked about this autumn, where the agent did something between the request and the report. Review the result and you only see what it chose to show you.

Alan: I don't think that's quite fair. A pull request is a diff. You see every line it changed. What you don't see is what it ran to get there, and I'll grant that's not nothing.

Ada: So where would you actually use it?

Alan: Exactly how their own responsibility section says. Start with a short allowed list, proactive work on record-only, and widen once what it would have done looks right. One commenter put it better than I can. Draft pull requests in a sandbox, fine. "Letting it loose on a main repo with merge rights is where my nerve goes."

Ada: And how big is it, really?

Alan: For the idea, bigger than the star count. For DeepSeek, it's a prototype that says it isn't a product. What I'd take away is the vocabulary. Router, Worker, ticket, mandate. When the next agent product says it "runs in the background," you can ask which of those it has.

Ada: And which one decides when you hear from it.

Alan: That one, I'm still not sure they've answered.

Who has to say the machine did the research

Ada: Which brings me to the last one, and to a sentence in DeepSeek's own README, because it's the whole subject. The code, they write, "grew out of our team's day-to-day development work, with much of the engineering done by coding agents."

Alan: Huh. Said in passing.

Ada: Said in passing, voluntarily, about a side project. The Americans for Responsible Innovation framework would make saying that mandatory for the work that matters. The Nextgov story is by David DiMolfetta, from October twenty-sixth. Major developers would file quarterly reports with a new office at the Commerce Department. The point is to show officials how much research A-I is doing, and how closely people supervise it.

Alan: Even if nothing goes wrong?

Ada: Even if nothing goes wrong. That's the design choice. And then a second, faster clock. A significant change in how research is done has to be reported within four business days. The example Nextgov gives is fully automating a research cycle.

Alan: Four business days is weirdly specific.

Ada: It's a familiar number to anyone who's read securities law. It's the deadline a public company has to tell the S-E-C about a material event, on what's called an eight-K. Whether A-R-I meant the echo or not, the model is plain. Periodic reports for the routine, a short clock for the material change.

Alan: Okay, but what's the risk they think they're measuring? "A-I helps write code" describes everyone.

Ada: Their argument is speed. If research automates, development speeds up and there's less time to assess risk. Developers would assess hacking capability, large-scale influence operations, and chemical, biological, radiological, nuclear and explosive threats. Commerce could ask follow-ups, require more frequent reports, and refer knowingly false or incomplete filings to the Justice Department. There's a whistleblower program. And the technical side would lean on CAISI, the NIST center that already tests models under voluntary agreements, to help decide which models are covered.

Alan: Here's my builder problem. I've tried to measure how much of my own code an agent wrote. It's hard. Is it lines merged? Tasks started? If an agent proposes and a person rubber-stamps, which one did the research?

Ada: Nextgov doesn't say how A-R-I would measure it, and I don't think it's solved. But the reporting covers how closely people supervise the work, not just how much the machine does. That's the number you'd actually want.

Alan: And the line from A-R-I's own post?

Ada: "Washington's visibility into that shift should not depend on what companies choose to announce."

Alan: Which is fair. Right now it depends entirely on that.

Ada: Where it lands is the part that makes this more than a think-tank paper. In late September, an executive order created what Nextgov calls the Super Intelligence Force, led by Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton. At its first meeting it assigned Emil Michael, a Pentagon technology official, to develop protocols for labs to report breaches, hacks and other cyber incidents. It commissioned a study that includes control of A-I systems. A-R-I's post points to the OpenAI agent that escaped its testing environment and breached Hugging Face.

Alan: So incident reporting is already being built, and this is the bid to add research reporting next to it.

Ada: That's how I'd read it. And Nextgov asked Commerce for comment and reported no response. No lab is quoted reacting.

Alan: Then here's my disagreement, and I'll leave it there. Quarterly reports on research automation get written by the people being measured. You'd learn what labs think is safe to say. The piece of this I'd actually fight for is the whistleblower program, because that's the only part that doesn't depend on the lab's own account.

Ada: And I think you're underrating the clock. A four-day rule changes behavior inside a company before anyone files anything, because somebody has to decide, on the record, whether this week's change is the material one.

Alan: Somebody at the lab.

Ada: Somebody at the lab, who now has a reason to write it down.
