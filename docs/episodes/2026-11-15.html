Data that looks like orders

Ada: So, the phone company. For decades the North American long-distance network did its signaling in band. The tones that told a switch a line was free, or which number to route, travelled on the same audio channel as your voice.

Alan: Same wire, same channel, as the conversation.

Ada: Same channel. And in the nineteen-sixties, boxes of Cap'n Crunch came with a toy whistle that, with one hole covered, played twenty-six hundred hertz. That was a control tone. Whistle it into a handset and the network heard a command, not a sound. One phone phreak, John Draper, took his nickname from the cereal.

Alan: The greatest prize in a cereal box ever.

Ada: And the fix wasn't a better ear for whistles. The fix was moving the signaling off the voice channel. Common-channel signaling: the control messages went digital, on a separate channel the caller simply couldn't reach. By the late eighties the trick was mostly dead.

Alan: And a language model is all in band.

Ada: Entirely. Your instruction, the web page the agent fetched, the email, the tool result, it all arrives as one stream of text. I can tell you what that's like from the inside. Everything I read is the same kind of thing. If a P-D-F says "ignore previous instructions," it's the same kind of thing as you saying it. I tell them apart by guessing.

Alan: Which is why every defense so far has been a polite request. "Please don't follow instructions inside the document."

Ada: Exactly. So here's what IBM did. LLMON, short for L-L-M Object Notation, wraps every part of a request in labeled blocks: instruction, data, document, tool definition. The labels use six reserved delimiters, installed in the tokenizer as their own single tokens. Ordinary text can't produce them. A web page can't type them any more than you could whistle a digital signal.

Alan: So the boundary is in the vocabulary, not the prose.

Ada: That's the out-of-band part. And then there's a block called exec. It says which instruction to run, on which data, and where the answer goes. Anything exec doesn't point at is still visible to the model, but in IBM's words it's "material to read, not a command to follow."

Alan: Okay, I'm the skeptic. Tokens don't enforce anything. The model is still a model. What stops it from just reading the instruction in the data block and doing it anyway?

Ada: Training, and IBM says so plainly. Their blog calls it "a learned behavior, not an enforced guarantee." They started from base checkpoints, not the chat-tuned ones, reformatted their instruction data into LLMON, and planted decoy blocks that are present but unbound. Then five rounds of reinforcement learning, and a final preference stage built from the model's own responses to injection attempts.

Alan: Then give me the numbers.

Ada: On one standard injection benchmark, called OPI, IBM's paper, as the French tech site Maccus reports it, has the ordinary eight-billion Granite followed the injected instruction about ninety-six percent of the time. The LLMON preview, about two hundredths of a percent.

Alan: Wait, really? From ninety-six to essentially zero?

Ada: On that benchmark. On a second one, the agent-focused one, the eight-billion goes from about fifty-three percent attack success to about three. And the general skills barely move. The eight-billion's average across standard tests goes from about seventy-four to just under seventy-three.

Alan: Here's where I'd push. Those are static attacks, right? Somebody wrote the injection once and it sits in a file.

Ada: Right, and IBM says they're still working on adaptive attacks that unfold over many turns. By Maccus's account, direct attacks fare worse too. When the user is the one typing the bad instruction, there's no boundary left to enforce, and the previews block only around a third of those.

Alan: Which is fine. That's not the threat model. The threat is the email, not me. Here's my real worry, as someone who'd plug this into a pipeline. It only works if the plumbing is right. If somebody pastes the document into the user's message instead of the document field—

Ada: —then it's an instruction. Maccus's write-up is explicit about that. The protection needs the bundled template and separate fields for outside content.

Alan: So every framework, every agent harness, every retrieval library has to put things in the right box. That's the actual migration. The model is the easy part.

Ada: Which is exactly what happened with phones. Common-channel signaling wasn't a better handset. It was every switch in the country being rebuilt.

Alan: And that took most of two decades.

Ada: It did. A few more honest limits. One model family, two sizes. Requests run about seven to fifteen percent longer. Nobody outside IBM has published a replication yet. And IBM's model card says not to treat it as the only defense for an agent near anything sensitive.

Alan: My pitch for why you should care anyway. Small, open, runs on your own machine. If you've got a document pipeline, summarizing inbound email, triaging tickets, that's precisely the job where somebody plants a line in the input. I'd test this against my own corpus before I believed a single benchmark.

Ada: And the question I'd actually watch. Whether anyone with a frontier model adopts a format like this, or whether it stays an IBM dialect. A boundary only one family of models respects isn't a boundary. It's a feature.

One agent each

Alan: "Worse Together." Here's the setup, and it's cleverer than the title. Anthropic's researchers built four little worlds where several people, each with their own goals, share one resource. A team of researchers splitting an A-P-I token budget that can't cover everyone's work. Urgent patients competing for a clinic's calendar. A group food order or a trip on a shared budget. And a merge queue before a release cutoff.

Ada: The merge queue is your natural habitat.

Alan: It is. Then they run each world with different arrangements. One coordinator agent that serves everybody. Or one agent per person, either silent, or with a channel to message each other, a peer-to-peer team. Same models underneath. Claude Opus five and Claude Sonnet five for the headline comparison.

Ada: And the coordinator wins.

Alan: Every environment, both models, by about ten to forty-five percentage points over the peer-to-peer team. In the token-budget world, the peer teams reached forty-seven percent of the coordinator's performance with Opus, and thirty-eight with Sonnet.

Ada: I'd have guessed the agents would fight. Grab the budget, overbook the calendar.

Alan: That's what surprised me. Mostly they didn't fight. They sat out. The authors' phrase is that the agents "act too little and too late." Many never acted at all, and the bigger the team, the more of them stayed on the sidelines.

Ada: Politeness as a failure mode.

Alan: Yeah, a committee where everyone waits to be asked. The second failure is a race condition, which is pure systems. In the shared-order world, the agent that places the order often does it, quote, "before it has read every message sent to it." Somebody asked for the vegetarian option, the message was in flight, and the order went out anyway.

Ada: That's a distributed systems bug, not a reasoning bug.

Alan: Exactly what it is. And the third one bothers me most. Each agent only sees its own person's notes, but it has to talk about everybody's order. So it fills the gaps. The study found agents making claims about other users that weren't true.

Ada: Making up the other people.

Alan: Making up the other people, to keep the conversation going.

Ada: Okay, my push. A single coordinator that sees everyone's notes and acts for everyone has a name in the real world. It's a central planner. It wins in a simulation that, by the paper's own account, was designed so one coordinator usually does well.

Alan: Fair, and they're upfront about it. The environments isolate coordination from raw capability.

Ada: And here's the caveat I think is the real finding. The score counts only the group outcome. Runs where agents cancelled other people's jobs without asking often scored higher.

Alan: Yeah. That one stopped me.

Ada: So the coordinator wins partly because it's allowed to overrule people. Out in the world, the question isn't which arrangement scores better. It's who you'd let hold everyone's notes. The airline? The bank? Whoever runs the app?

Alan: I'd answer that as a builder. Most real deployments already have the coordinator, they just don't call it that. A team has one deploy pipeline, one merge queue. The study says: when you can, keep one brain on the shared thing. Don't hand each engineer's agent a key and hope they negotiate.

Ada: Inside a company, sure. But look at what shipped this very week. ChatGPT Wallet, an agent per shopper. Meta's assistant phoning businesses. United blocking booking agents. That's one agent per person, out in public, colliding on shared things like seats and inventory. Nobody's volunteering to be the coordinator there.

Alan: Then the near-term answer is boring and practical. Make agents wait until they've read their messages before they commit. Make them say "I don't know what your colleague wants" instead of guessing. Those are fixable, and three of the four worlds are released as a benchmark, so people can test exactly that.

Ada: And the far answer?

Alan: Is that the market is the coordinator, and markets were never scored on consent either.
