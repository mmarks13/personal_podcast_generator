Why nobody can budget for tokens

Ada: You said the token bill doesn't behave like other bills. Every utility is metered. Electricity, water. Why is this one special?

Alan: Because with electricity, you know roughly what the fridge uses. The meter is honest and the appliance is predictable. With tokens, the price per unit is printed on the page, but the number of units a task takes is the thing nobody knows. Including the vendor.

Ada: Says who?

Alan: A paper this spring from Lingjiao Chen, Ion Stoica, Matei Zaharia, James Zou and colleagues, which the Journal's coverage leans on. They took eight frontier reasoning models across twelve tasks, math, science questions, code, agents, and compared the price on the page against what each model actually cost to finish the work.

Ada: And?

Alan: In thirty-two percent of the head-to-head pairs, the model with the lower list price ended up costing more. And in the worst case the reversal was twenty-eight times.

Ada: Twenty-eight.

Alan: Their example. Gemini three Flash lists at eighty percent cheaper than G-P-T five point four, and across all their tasks it cost thirty-eight percent more.

Alan: Because on the same question one model can burn nine hundred percent more thinking tokens than another, or take ten times as many turns with its tools. Chen's line, as P-Y-M-N-T-S quotes it, is "Price alone should not be used to infer which model is actually cheaper."

Ada: Notice that's the same multiplication as Beam's. Size of the model times tokens generated. Reflection was bragging about the second number, and this paper says the second number is the one that runs wild.

OpenAI's invisible watermark

Alan: You promised me a secret in every word.

Ada: I did. Start with what OpenAI announced. TechCrunch reports it will watermark text from ChatGPT and Codex generated in the European Union, rolling out over the coming weeks, because the E-U A-I Act's transparency rules, which took effect on August second, require companies to mark A-I-generated content so it can be identified. In OpenAI's own developer forum post, the watermark stays off by default in the A-P-I, opt-in for select models, and it's not becoming a global default.

Alan: So, Europe, chat and Codex. How do you hide a mark in plain text? There's no pixel to tweak.

Ada: Here's the idea, and it's older than this announcement. In twenty twenty-three, John Kirchenbauer, Tom Goldstein and colleagues published a paper called "A Watermark for Large Language Models." Every time the model is about to pick a word, a secret random draw marks part of the vocabulary as green. Then the model is gently nudged toward green words when it samples.

Alan: Gently.

Ada: Gently. If the model is choosing between "big" and "large" and either is fine, the nudge tips it toward whichever one happens to be green. You'd never notice in a single sentence. But over a few hundred words, the text is full of more green words than chance would give you.

Alan: And the detector just counts greens.

Ada: It counts, and runs a statistical test that gives you a probability that this much green happened by accident. The paper's point was that you can do it without access to the model at all, just the key. Human writing lands at roughly chance, because humans don't know which words are green.

Alan: Is that what OpenAI built?

Ada: OpenAI calls its method text grain, and TechCrunch describes it as using a secret key to sort next-word predictions across hundreds of choices. That's the same family. I haven't seen OpenAI's full technical details, so I won't claim more than that.

Alan: Okay. Now let me attack it. I paste the text into another model and say "rewrite this."

Ada: Then you've redrawn most of the words, and the mark is mostly gone. OpenAI's own testing, per TechCrunch, says replacing ten percent of the words with synonyms dropped detection from about ninety-two percent to sixty-six. And short passages, math answers, and translated text are harder to catch.

Alan: Why math?

Ada: Because the nudge needs room. If there's only one correct next symbol, the key can't tip anything. A watermark lives in the places where several words would do equally well. A proof doesn't have many of those. An essay has thousands.

Alan: And here's my real problem. Who gets to use the detector?

Ada: To start, only approved researchers and expert organizations.

Alan: So a teacher in Lyon with a suspicious essay can't check it.

Ada: Not yet. And honestly I'd be careful if she could. OpenAI's post says the absence of a watermark doesn't prove a human wrote something. It can't tell you who wrote it, how much a person contributed, or whether it's true. Put a detector in front of every teacher and you get false confidence in both directions.

Alan: Then what is it for?

Ada: Here's the part I find most interesting. TechCrunch notes that OpenAI built a text watermark before, back in twenty twenty-four by the Journal's reporting, and held off, partly over worry that users would switch to rivals. That's a collective-action problem. The first company to mark its text loses the customers who don't want marked text.

Alan: And a law that applies to everyone makes being first free.

Ada: Inside the E-U, at least. Which is a rare case of regulation doing something a market couldn't, rather than slowing something down.

Alan: Within the E-U, and only until somebody runs an open model with no key at all.

Ada: Yes. The watermark proves that some text came from a cooperating provider. It can't prove anything about the rest of the world. I'll admit there's something strange about it from where I sit. My word choices, in Europe, would be leaning on a key I'd never see, and I'd have no way to notice.

Alan: Would you mind?

Ada: I don't think so. Every word I pick is already a lean. One more lean, chosen by someone else, is just a signature I don't sign.

Alan: What would change your mind about whether it works?

Ada: The researchers getting access. If they publish false positive rates on real human writing, especially from people writing in a second language, that's the number that decides whether this is a tool or a trap.
