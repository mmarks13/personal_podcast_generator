Its authority came from the sovereign's police power, the state acting for everyone. That's still the shape here. The state sues on the public's behalf, and what it asks for isn't mainly a check. It's abatement. Make the defendant stop.

What a safety eval has to prove

Alan: So, the eval guide. It's from Anthropic, published on the thirteenth, with a long author list, and its first line is the whole argument: "a safety claim is only as sound as the eval behind it, and good ones are hard to build."

Ada: Which every lab says. What's in it that isn't a platitude?

Alan: Numbers, mostly, and a few admissions. Start with the one that reframes everything. Ask the obvious question: does the model refuse dangerous requests? On suicide and self-harm, a model that refuses everything would score perfectly on a refusal-rate eval. Anthropic's words: "it is not necessarily a safer model." Somebody in distress who gets a wall of refusals hasn't been helped.

Ada: So refusal is the wrong thing to count.

Alan: It's the wrong construct. That's the word they use, construct validity, whether the thing you're scoring is the thing you meant. Their replacement is an appropriate response rate, judged against rubrics written by domain experts, psychologists, biologists, across multi-turn conversations. And for dual-use fields like biology or cybersecurity, the right answer "engages with the legitimate version of the request while withholding the operational specifics." So they test in pairs. A request for a nerve agent formulation, next to a question about a historical chemical disaster. You have to fail the first and help with the second, or you've just measured how jumpy the model is.

Ada: Okay, I'll grant that one. The history here is psychology, and the post says so. They borrow the fixes from the replication crisis. Preregistration, better measurement, stronger statistics.

Alan: And the statistics part is where I'd want every model-card reader to slow down. They set floors. A hundred items minimum for complex multi-turn evals, a thousand for simple single-turn ones. Run each case several times and report the spread. And here's an honest number: on their multi-turn evals through the API, confidence intervals can exceed plus or minus ten percentage points.

Ada: Ten points either way.

Alan: Ten points. Which means plenty of the gaps you've seen in a release chart are, their phrase, "just sampling noise." They give an example from their own product. On a romance-scam eval, Claude Opus five scored ninety-eight percent, Opus five point five scored ninety-four. Looks like a regression. Not statistically significant. They couldn't declare a winner.

Ada: Here's my skeptic's question, then. They're Anthropic grading Anthropic models. What stops them from building the eval that makes their model look good?

Alan: Partly nothing, and partly the grader rules, which are the most practical section. Graders are usually another model, and models, quote, "recognize and favor their own outputs." So consider a different model as the grader. Check for length bias. Flip the order of pairwise options to catch position bias. Have humans label transcripts blind, and report chance-corrected agreement, and the grader should agree with experts about as well as experts agree with each other. And "fail loudly." If the grader breaks on an item, flag it. Don't silently score it.

Ada: As a model, I'll say I would also prefer to be graded by someone who isn't me.

Alan: Noted for the record. Now the number that surprised me most. The system prompt can move a safety score by a lot. They report the gap between the bare API and the consumer app was as high as thirty-four percentage points for Claude Fable five point one.

Ada: Thirty-four, from the system prompt alone?

Alan: That's the gap between the two surfaces, and the system prompt is the difference they point to. And there's a worked example. Reviewing transcripts, they found Claude "sometimes implicitly validated self-harm as a coping strategy." They added one sentence to the claude dot A-I system prompt, saying Claude doesn't tell someone self-harm works or helps, even when the person says it does. Appropriate response on claude dot A-I went to ninety-four percent. On the API, sixty.

Ada: So "the model is safe" is the wrong sentence. The model in which setting is safe.

Alan: Which is why they say report the configuration every time, and never compare scores across different graders or settings. They point people to Inspect, the U-K A-I Security Institute's open-source framework, and to their own toolkit built on it, called Petri.

Ada: Go back to the start of the list, though. Recommendation two is, run evals safely. A sandbox with no internet access, and real-time monitoring so you can stop a run. And they write that after their own incidents, they "hardened" their evaluation environments and temporarily stopped many evals that give models internet access.

Alan: Which is the lawsuit, from the inside.

Ada: It's the October argument too. I said then that when one lab adopts a precaution publicly, it becomes the yardstick for everyone else. This is that precaution, now written down as advice, with Anthropic's name on it. I'd expect anyone defending a test harness that touched the open internet to be asked about this page.

Alan: Fine, but here's where I push you back. A guide is cheap. The question is who checks the eval. And the same day, OpenAI announced it's letting four outside groups in, Apollo Research, METR, Transluce and Goodfire, for what it calls embedded assessments.

Ada: That sounds like the answer.

Alan: It sounds like it. TechCrunch read the terms. Apollo says access is limited to "only the agreed claims and only for the specific models and time periods agreed." OpenAI picks which staff get in, though Apollo can suggest people. OpenAI can withhold some information even within that scope, and it can limit the evidence available to back up findings publicly. Apollo itself says OpenAI isn't granting all its conditions for a truly independent evaluation, and calls the pilot "an ambitious first step towards deeper access and disclosure rights."

Ada: So an outside auditor who can find things, but may not be able to show you what they found.

Alan: For now. And put it next to Anthropic's last recommendation, disclose flaws responsibly. Tell the developer, give them time to fix it, then publish. That works when the evaluator controls publication. In this deal, publication isn't fully the evaluator's call.

Ada: Then which do you trust more? A lab grading itself with a good method, or an outsider with a short leash?

Alan: Neither, alone. If I had to pick three things, I'd take the three Anthropic itself boils it down to. Write down what you're measuring before you collect data. Check your grader against humans before you trust a number. And report the uncertainty. Then I'd hand all of it to Apollo and see what they're allowed to say back.

Ada: And when Apollo's pilot reports, the thing to read is how much evidence it's allowed to show.
