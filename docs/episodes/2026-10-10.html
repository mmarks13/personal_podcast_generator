Goodfire's monitors that read the model from inside

Alan: I do. Because there are really two ways to watch an agent. One is what Anthropic did: save the transcripts, then go back and read them. That found the tip. Ten weeks late. The other is to watch while it runs. The trouble with watching is cost. The standard way is a second language model, a judge, reading every step. That's expensive and it's slow.

Ada: How expensive?

Alan: Goodfire's own write-up has the numbers, and these are company-reported, so hold them that way. To run an L-L-M judge over a million turns of agent traffic, they estimate about three thousand one hundred dollars without reasoning, and about fifty-four hundred if the judge thinks before answering. TechCrunch adds that with a top-tier model as judge, you're near two hundred thousand dollars.

Ada: And theirs?

Alan: Sixty-five dollars per million turns without reasoning. A hundred and eighty-five with. That's where the thirty to fifty times comes from. And latency: a judge adds about a hundred and sixty milliseconds per turn, or two point seven seconds with reasoning. Their setup adds two milliseconds, or forty-two.

Ada: Okay. Then teach me the trick, because that's not a cheaper judge. That's a different kind of thing.

Alan: It is. When a model processes a turn, it's already computing a huge pile of intermediate numbers, its activations, layer by layer. A probe is a small detector trained to read those numbers and answer one question. Does this look like offensive hacking? Like reward hacking? Their C-E-O, Eric Ho, put it simply to TechCrunch: internal activation monitors are really cheap because they reuse the computations in the forward pass. You're not running another model. You're reading the one that's already running.

Ada: So it's an old move in a new seat. Putting a small classifier on a network's middle layers to see what it's representing is a standard research technique. The new part is bolting it onto a production server.

Alan: That's exactly the new part. They extended S-G-Lang, the serving engine, with custom kernels so the probe reads activations as the tokens come out. They report less than a two percent hit to throughput.

Ada: But a probe that cheap must be dumb.

Alan: That's why it's a cascade. TechCrunch's analogy is airport security. The probe scans everything. Only when its reading crosses a calibrated threshold does the turn get escalated to the expensive judge. Most traffic never touches the judge, so you pay judge prices for a sliver of it.

Ada: And what does it catch?

Alan: On about twenty-four hundred sessions, over sixty thousand turns, Goodfire reports the cascade catches around ninety-three percent of harmful sessions while interrupting at most five and a half percent of harmless ones. That's on Kimi K-three, the open model it's deployed on first. And FAR dot A-I red-teamed it with a hundred and forty jailbreak strategies. Universal jailbreaks went from sixty-six to zero. Total jailbroken interactions, seven hundred down to eighteen.

Ada: Here's my skeptic question. Ninety-three percent compared to what? Who decided what counted as harmful?

Alan: Good question, and the answer is in the write-up. The reference labels come from Claude Opus five. So the probe is trained, in effect, to agree with an expensive judge.

Ada: So it's a cheap copy of a judge. It can't be better than the thing it learned from.

Alan: That's fair, and Goodfire says the probe alone is less capable than a full judge. That's why the judge stays in the loop. What you're buying is coverage. A judge you can afford to run on every turn beats a better judge you run on one turn in fifty.

Ada: Fine. What about the false alarms? Five and a half percent of harmless sessions interrupted sounds small until you run a million of them.

Alan: And it's worse in places. On one exploit benchmark their false positive rate runs thirteen to eighteen percent. Better code, less remembered

Ada: Speaking of whether something is real. My third story is about people, and it starts with a small, careful study. Christian Bergh, Benjamin Tag, Alexandra Vassar and Jake Renzella at the University of New South Wales put fifty-nine computer science undergraduates through three introductory programming tasks in C. Fifty-five made it into the analysis. Half could use ChatGPT four point five. Half had conventional web search, no generative A-I.

Alan: And the A-I group won.

Ada: At the task, by a mile. Eighty-nine percent on the coding versus sixty-nine. Then the researchers tested what they remembered, right away and again forty-eight hours later. The ChatGPT group scored forty-one percent on recall immediately, against fifty-three. Two days later, thirty-nine against fifty-two.

Alan: Huh. So they forgot faster.

Ada: No, and this is the part I'd like everyone to keep. The paper reports no significant difference in how much each group lost over the forty-eight hours. Both groups forgot at about the same rate. The A-I group just started lower.

Alan: So the gap opened while they were working, not afterwards.

Ada: Exactly. It's not a forgetting problem. It's an encoding problem. You can't lose what never went in. And there's a second number that fits. Asked how much of their submitted code was really theirs, the ChatGPT students said forty-five percent. The search students said eighty-one.

Alan: Wait, really? They knew?

Ada: They knew. And on self-reported effort, the ChatGPT group's mental effort rose less from task to task. The tasks got harder and it didn't feel harder.
