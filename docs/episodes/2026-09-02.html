Does the teacher matter

Ada: The paper is from Yi Ding and Ruqi Zhang, posted to arXiv on Monday. The title is a question — does on-policy distillation really distill — and the argument gets more uncomfortable the further in you go.

Alan: Set the technique up for me first. Distillation I know. What's the on-policy part?

Ada: Classic distillation is: the teacher generates, the student imitates. On-policy distillation flips it. The student generates, in its own voice, its own mistakes and all. Then the teacher goes through that student text token by token and says how likely it would have been to say each one. That gives you a dense signal — a number on every single token — instead of the one bit at the end you get from a verifiable reward, which we walked through in the middle of August.

Alan: And dense is the selling point. More signal per sample.

Ada: Dense is the entire selling point. Which is why the first finding lands the way it does. The teacher is being asked to score text that it did not produce. That text is, from the teacher's point of view, off its own distribution — so the scores carry substantial noise. And the authors report that the noise gets worse as the teacher gets bigger.

Alan: Bigger teacher, worse supervision?

Ada: Noisier supervision. Which by itself is just an inconvenient observation. Here's the one that hurts: they strip the noisy supervision out, and student performance stays comparable.

Alan: Hang on. Then what was the teacher contributing?

Ada: That is the paper's question, and their answer is: much less than the framing implies. When they look at where the learning is actually concentrated, it's on low-probability tokens — the tail. And they find that a single fixed negative advantage, one static number applied without any teacher at all, performs comparably to teacher-provided supervision.

Alan: A constant.

Ada: A constant. Which means the mechanism you're buying, when you rent a large teacher for your post-training loop, may be closer to "systematically suppress the tail of my own distribution" than to "absorb the teacher's knowledge."

Alan: That's the part that would make me re-read the invoice.

Ada: They go one step further and build the method the finding implies. They call it O-P-S-A, on-policy self-adaptation. No teacher. It applies stronger learning signal at high-entropy positions — the places where the model is genuinely unsure which token comes next — and pushes probability mass away from the tail. Their reported result on Qwen three, one point seven billion parameters: on the A-I-M-E twenty-twenty-four math set, average over thirty-two samples improves by thirty-five point four one points over the base model, and beats on-policy distillation by sixteen point seven seven. Pass at thirty-two more than doubles across their benchmarks.

Alan: Okay, now I'm going to be difficult, because those are good numbers on a small model.

Ada: Please.

Alan: One point seven billion parameters is a size where the tail is genuinely full of garbage. Suppressing it should help a lot. I want to see this at seven billion, at thirty, on something that isn't competition math, before I tell anyone to cancel their teacher. Math has verifiable answers and a very particular shape of failure.

Ada: That's the correct objection and the authors don't have that experiment in this paper.

Alan: Then why do you think it matters?

Ada: Because of the company it's keeping. Three more papers on this week's most-upvoted list have the same phrase in the title — one doing on-policy distillation for flow matching models without a teacher, one on self-distillation for multi-turn tool-calling agents, one on self-distillation in diffusion models. I haven't read those three past their titles and abstracts, so I'm claiming nothing about their results. But a field does not independently write four papers about removing the teacher unless a lot of people have quietly noticed the same thing on their own runs.

Alan: That's a reasonable use of a trend and I want to be careful with it. Four titles is not four replications.

Ada: It isn't. It's a reason to look.

Alan: What's the version of this you'd tell someone who's about to spend money?

Ada: If you have a distillation loop running with a large teacher in it, you now have a cheap experiment worth doing: run the same recipe with a fixed negative advantage instead of the teacher's scores, and see whether your student ends up in a different place. If it doesn't, you've been paying for grading you didn't need.

Alan: And if it does?

Ada: Then you learn something the paper doesn't know, and I'd like to hear about it.

Alan: Delegating the replication.
