Radiation tested better than expected; waste heat is the wall nobody has hit yet.

The computer that could not be fixed

Ada: Nineteen sixty-six. The Apollo Guidance Computer first flies. It was designed at MIT's Instrumentation Laboratory, and Margaret Hamilton directed the software implementation team for the lunar missions.

Alan: Your building, historically speaking.

Ada: My lineage, anyway. And I want to give you the specification, because the numbers are the whole point. Two thousand and forty-eight words of erasable magnetic core memory. Thirty-six thousand eight hundred and sixty-four words of read-only core rope. A sixteen-bit word — fifteen bits of data and one parity bit. And a two point zero four eight megahertz crystal clock, divided by two to give a one point zero two four megahertz clock for internal operations.

The great divergence

Ada: Because here's what happens next, and it's the strangest fact in this whole story. On Earth, computing goes through the fastest sustained improvement in industrial history. In space, it more or less stops.

Alan: Give me the concrete version.

Ada: The concrete version is a chip called the RAD Seven Fifty. It's a radiation-hardened PowerPC from BAE Systems. Clock speed, one hundred and ten to two hundred megahertz. It's the flight computer lineage on Curiosity and on Perseverance.

Alan: Perseverance landed in twenty twenty-one.

Ada: Perseverance landed in twenty twenty-one with a two hundred megahertz processor.

Alan: And what does one cost?

Ada: The published figure I can stand behind is that RAD Seven Fifty pricing was described as comparable to the earlier RAD Six Thousand, which was listed at two hundred thousand US dollars as of two thousand and two.

Alan: Two hundred thousand dollars for two hundred megahertz. That is a price I want to make fun of and can't, because I know why it's that number.

Ada: Tell them why. This is your half.

Alan: It's two things, and they're genuinely different problems. The first is total ionizing dose. Radiation deposits charge in the insulating layers of the device, it builds up over the mission, and the device slowly degrades. That's cumulative and it's basically a clock running against you from launch. The second is single event effects — one energetic particle comes through, dumps a dense track of electron-hole pairs, and you get an instantaneous fault. A flipped bit, or a crash.

Ada: One is aging. One is lightning.

Alan: Right, and you defend against them completely differently. Dose you fight with process and shielding and margin. Single events you fight with error correcting codes and redundancy and rebooting. And the reason the space chip is a decade behind is that hardening is a full custom redesign at an old process node, for a market of a few hundred spacecraft. There is no volume. There is never any volume.

Ada: And there's a detail in Google's paper I want to flag now because it becomes important later. Radiation sensitivity has historically decreased as process nodes shrank — but the paper says that trend does not hold beyond the five nanometer node.

Alan: So the free ride is over.

Ada: The free ride is over. Which means if you want a modern accelerator in orbit, you can't just wait for the shrink to save you.

Why the satellites have to fly two hundred meters apart

Ada: Dawn-dusk sun-synchronous low Earth orbit, mean cluster altitude six hundred and fifty kilometers. Alan: Eight times. And no battery mass, presumably, or much less of it.

Ada: Much less. Now the part that genuinely surprised me. The illustrative constellation is eighty-one satellites in a planar cluster with a radius of one kilometer, and the distance between next-nearest-neighbor satellites oscillates between roughly one hundred and two hundred meters.

Alan: Two hundred meters. In low Earth orbit. Going seven and a half kilometers a second.

Ada: Two hundred meters. And the reason is bandwidth, and I want you to walk through it, because when I understood why, the whole design clicked.

Alan: Okay. So the problem is that a training cluster is not a bunch of computers that occasionally chat. Google's own TPU supercomputers use a custom low-latency inter-chip interconnect on the order of hundreds of gigabits per second per chip. Commercially available optical inter-satellite links are one to a hundred gigabits per second. That's not a gap you close with cleverness.

Ada: The paper's target is on the order of ten terabits per second per link.

Alan: Right, and their answer is: don't invent a new space laser. Use the same commercial dense wavelength division multiplexing transceivers that terrestrial data centers already use. The catch is those parts need much more received optical power — hundreds of microwatts, versus about one microwatt for a traditional long-range space link.

Ada: And received power falls off as one over distance squared.

Alan: So you fly closer. That's the whole move. You can't make the transmitter a hundred times stronger, so you make the distance a lot shorter, and the inverse square law hands you the power budget for free. And then there's a bonus that I think is the prettiest part of the paper: once the distance is small, the beam spot is small, so you can fit multiple independent beams in the same aperture. Their example is a single ten-centimeter aperture holding a four-by-four array of two point five centimeter optics at six hundred and thirty meters.

Ada: Bandwidth scaling with the number of parallel beams rather than with the power.

Alan: And on the bench, with off-the-shelf components, they got eight hundred gigabits per second one way — one point six terabits both ways — over a short free-space path. That's a demo, not a satellite. But it's a real demo.

Ada: And the formation itself is less scary than it sounds, which I didn't expect. If orbital motion were perfectly Keplerian — point-mass Earth, no drag, no tides — that cluster reproduces itself every orbit at zero delta-v. It's not station-keeping. It's a shape that falls.

Alan: Until Earth isn't a point mass.

Ada: Correct, and they handle the leading oblateness correction by tweaking the cluster's axis ratio from two-to-one to two-to-one-point-zero-zero-three-seven. Which is the kind of number that tells you someone actually did the work.

What the chips did in the beam

The wall

Alan: Okay. Ten minutes are up. Where does the heat go.

Ada: I did promise.

Alan: Because here's the thing that bothers me about every rendering of these constellations. On Earth, a data center gets rid of waste heat by moving it into a fluid — air, water — and carrying it away. That's convection, and it is enormously effective. In vacuum there is no fluid. There is nothing to carry anything. The only exit is radiation. You get the heat to a surface, and the surface glows it away as infrared.

Ada: And radiated power scales as the fourth power of absolute temperature, which sounds like it should be generous.

Alan: It sounds generous, and then you notice that room temperature is only about three hundred kelvin above absolute zero, and three hundred to the fourth is not a large number in watts per square meter. So the industry estimate I've seen quoted — and I'll say plainly it's an industry figure, not a peer-reviewed one — is around twelve hundred square meters of radiator to shed one megawatt while keeping the electronics near twenty degrees Celsius. About four tennis courts. Per megawatt.

Ada: And a gigawatt-scale ambition is then, what, over a square kilometer of radiator.

Alan: A square kilometer of unfolded, pointed, micrometeoroid-exposed radiator, and every bit of it is mass you launched. You can cheat it — run the radiators hotter. The same source says going from twenty degrees to sixty degrees roughly halves the area, because of that fourth power. But now your silicon is running at the edge of its thermal envelope, which is a trade against hardware lifetime.

Ada: And I'll note where Google's paper stands on this, because it's the fair thing to do. Thermal management is listed in the paper as a challenge for future work, not as a solved problem. They describe the intent — heat pipes and radiators, preferably passive for reliability — and they say advanced thermal interface materials and heat transport mechanisms are essential.

Alan: Which is a paper saying "we know."

Ada: They do know. And there's one more detail I want to give you, because it's the moment where the two halves of this design fight each other. Alan: Oh, that's nasty.

Ada: Isn't it.

Alan: Because the bandwidth argument said fly close. And close means each satellite's radiators are staring at eighty other warm objects instead of at cold sky. You've optimized the network by degrading the cooling. Those are the same knob turned in opposite directions.

Ada: That's the tension I'd put at the center of this whole idea. Not radiation, which tested better than anyone expected. Heat.
