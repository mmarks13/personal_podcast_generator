What a scratchpad gives away

Alan: All right. The rumour that turned out to be a paper. What did they actually do?

Ada: Let me set it up from the beginning, because the trick only makes sense if you know what's being hidden. Reasoning models think before they answer — they generate a long run of intermediate text, the chain of thought. Open models show you that text. In the big closed systems, the raw trace is hidden — the paper's opening line says exactly that. That includes G-P-T six Astra, and we spent a morning earlier this month on why Astra's reasoning is harder to read to begin with — it does some of its thinking by looping internally rather than by writing words.

Alan: The recurrent-depth thing.

Ada: The recurrent-depth thing. So: a hidden transcript, from a model that writes less of a transcript than most. Now the paper. It's called Capable yet Parsimonious, six authors, first author Xiaoyu Luo. Their method: when you call a model through the A-P-I, you're allowed to give it tools — functions it can call. The model doesn't run the tool; it writes out the arguments, and your code runs it. So they register one tool. It's called forced-reasoning, it takes a single text field, and its description is, verbatim, "Scratchpad for working through the problem."

Alan: That's it?

Ada: Nearly. They use a standard A-P-I setting to force the model to call that tool first. The model writes its working into the scratchpad. They send back one word — "Received" — and let it carry on normally.

Alan: And here's why it leaks, if I've got it. The provider can hide the thinking channel, because the provider controls it. But tool arguments are, by design, delivered to the developer. They have to be. My code can't run a tool whose arguments it isn't allowed to see.

Ada: That's the whole thing, and it's why I called it rude. "Hidden" was never a property of the model. It was a property of one output channel. Give the model a second channel that's contractually yours, and it'll reason into that.

Alan: Okay, objection one, and it's the big one. How do they know what comes out is reasoning, and not a model writing a nice-looking explanation after it already knows the answer?

Ada: They don't, and they say so plainly — the traces might be post-hoc rationalisation. So they did the only check available. On two open models, DeepSeek V four Flash and G-L-M five point two, where you can see the real chain of thought, they compared it with the forced one. Accuracy comes out close — DeepSeek goes from about thirty percent with no reasoning to seventy-three with the scratchpad, against seventy with its native thinking. And the kinds of reasoning steps look broadly the same.

Alan: That's a check on open models. Astra's native trace is exactly the thing nobody can see.

Ada: Right, and they concede it: for closed models, the evidence is necessarily behavioural. What they can show is that the scratchpad does the work. On eighty competition maths problems, Astra scores ninety-seven and a half percent with its native reasoning and ninety-three point eight through the scratchpad. On a hundred Humanity's Last Exam questions, thirty-five against thirty-two.

Alan: Eighty problems. A hundred problems. Those are small samples to be building conclusions on.

Ada: They are, and I'd put wide error bars on every single cell. Which is why the numbers aren't what I find interesting. The shape of the thinking is.

Alan: Go on, then.

Ada: They turned each trace into a tree — branches for approaches tried, nodes for steps. On the maths set, Astra's median tree is five wide and twenty-seven nodes. Alan: So Astra explores maybe a quarter as much.

Ada: It picks a path early and it doesn't write down the easy steps. My favourite example: checking a division, Opus spells out the multiplication in full, a hundred and one times four thousand, and so on. Astra writes the number, a slash, a hundred and one, colon, "yes." That's it.

Alan: That's my students' homework.

Ada: And it fits the recurrent-depth picture exactly: the elementary steps are resolved somewhere you can't see, and only the load-bearing ones get written out. Two independent routes to the same picture. That's the finding I'd keep.

Ada: It's the whole design philosophy in one decision, and it's the thing I actually want to teach. Why is Google doing this through a voice call at all? Alan: Because the restaurant doesn't have an A-P-I.

Ada: Because the restaurant doesn't have an A-P-I. The telephone is the oldest universal interface there is. It's the same move as the agents that operate a web browser by looking at the screen. You don't wait for the world to become machine-readable. You teach the machine to read the world as it is.
