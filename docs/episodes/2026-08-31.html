SemiAnalysis audited 25 GPU clouds — tenant isolation mostly wasn't

Self-Attention — 2026-08-31

SemiAnalysis tested twenty-five GPU cloud providers across thirty-two clusters and found container-only isolation, a live default fabric partition key leaking other customers' hostnames, an unscoped monitoring credential returning metrics from banks and a national intelligence agency, and tenant-reachable management interfaces on the cards that enforce isolation. Then two attempts to let outsiders check a lab's work: DeepMind's hardware-enclave double-blind evaluation pilot, and Anthropic's aggregate-only usage access for Stanford, Oxford, and METR.

Nobody sees the other half

Ada: This one is short on numbers and long on structure, which is unusual for us, and I want to be honest about that up front. DeepMind published it Thursday. There are no scores in it.

Alan: None?

Ada: None. The claim is entirely about the arrangement. So — the problem it is trying to solve. If you want an outside organisation to evaluate a frontier model, historically somebody has to give something up. Either the evaluator hands over their test prompts, in which case the model provider has now seen the exam, and even with the best intentions in the world those questions can end up in a training set. Or the provider hands over the model weights, which is the crown jewels and, for a frontier lab, a proliferation question as well as a commercial one.

Alan: So you pick which side you trust and the other side just eats it.

Ada: That has been the whole history of it. And the older lineage here is medicine, where blinding was never invented because researchers were assumed to be dishonest. It was invented because knowing which patient got the drug changes what an honest person sees. Machine learning imported half of that idea — the held-out test set, the hidden leaderboard split — and then spent about a decade discovering that a test set you can query enough times stops being held out.

Alan: Contamination.

Ada: Contamination, over-fitting to the public split, all of it. The response has mostly been procedural. Please do not train on this. So what DeepMind is testing is whether you can make it structural instead. They run the evaluation inside what Google Cloud calls Confidential Space, which is a hardware-backed enclave. The model goes in from one side. The prompts go in from the other. The computation happens somewhere neither party can observe, and both parties get a cryptographic attestation afterward describing exactly what code ran.

Alan: Attestation is the load-bearing word.

Ada: It is the whole thing.

Alan: Then let me be the annoying one, because that word does less than people think. An attestation tells you which binary executed inside the enclave. You have not removed it — you have relocated it to whoever reviewed the code that went in.

Ada: That is correct and I would go further. Look at where the enclave lives. It is Google Cloud. Google is one of the two blinded parties and also the landlord, the hardware vendor, and the root of the attestation chain. Two hundred and fifty thousand conversations, at arm's length

Ada: Then in what sense did they look at anything?

Ada: Percentages.

Alan: Percentages. Anthropic's own line is that researchers never accessed raw conversations, only aggregated outputs, after the same legal and privacy review as their internal work. Ada: So what is genuinely independent here, and what is not? Because I can see a version of this that is meaningful and a version that is a press release.

Alan: The independent part is the question. The researcher decides what to count, and that is not nothing — most of what we know about how people actually use these systems comes from the lab deciding what was interesting enough to publish. Ada: And what is not independent?

Alan: Reproducibility, entirely. Nobody outside can rerun it. Nobody can check whether the classifier agreed with a human on the edge cases, because there are no edge cases to look at.
