Ada: Oh, that's lovely. Horrible, but lovely.

Alan: Explain why you're delighted, because I'm not.

Ada: Because it's fifty years old. In nineteen seventy-three, Butler Lampson wrote a short paper called "A Note on the Confinement Problem." The question was, can you run a program you don't trust and guarantee it leaks nothing? And his answer was that you have to worry not just about the obvious channels, files and messages, but about channels that were never meant to carry information at all. How busy the machine is. How long something takes. Those came to be called covert channels.

Alan: And a cache is a perfect one.

Ada: A cache is the perfect one. Here's the mechanism, for anyone who hasn't met it. A cache is a small, fast memory the processor keeps near itself. If what you want is in it, the read is fast. If it isn't, the read is slow. Two programs that share a processor share that cache, even if they can't see each other's memory. So the sender either fills certain slots or leaves them alone. The receiver reads those slots and times itself. Fast means one thing, slow means another. That's a bit. Do it enough times and you have a wire.

Alan: Through a wall nobody drew.

Ada: Through a wall that was drawn around the data and not around the time. This is the same family of tricks behind the processor attacks that rattled the industry in twenty eighteen. What's new isn't the trick. It's who found the trick, unprompted by a human, inside a test.
