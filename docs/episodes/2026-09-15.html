Reading the architecture off the clock

Ada: So this is a measurement paper and it is almost entirely a methods story. Alan: And what comes back before the first token is the prefill. The model has to read the whole prompt before it says anything.

Ada: Which is where the cost lives, and it's worth saying why. Attention, as specified in twenty seventeen, has every token look at every other token. That's a design decision, not a law — and it means the work of reading a prompt grows with the square of its length. Double the prompt, quadruple the reading. Essentially every efficiency idea of the last eight years has been an attempt to buy that back.

Alan: So the curve shape tells you who bought it back.

Ada: That's exactly the argument. Their finding: Terra and Sol showed clear upward curvature as prompts got longer, consistent with a significant quadratic component. Sonnet five stayed close to linear with no observable curvature. Opus was noisier but still consistent with linear.

Ada: They express it as marginal time added per additional ten thousand tokens. At a hundred thousand tokens of context, Terra adds about seven hundredths of a second per ten thousand. Two point eight trillion parameters off a hard drive

Alan: Right. colibri. The pitch in the repository description is one line: run frontier mixture-of-experts models on hardware you already own — pure C, zero dependencies, experts streamed from disk.

Ada: Streamed from disk.

Alan: Streamed from disk. And the reason that isn't insane is what a mixture-of-experts model actually is. The parameter count is enormous, but for any given token only a small handful of experts fire. So the model is huge and the working set is small.

Ada: The model is large and mostly asleep.

Alan: Mostly asleep. So colibri treats storage, system memory and video memory as one memory hierarchy instead of three places a model either fits or doesn't. The dense parts — attention, shared experts — stay resident in RAM. The routed experts live on disk and get pulled in on demand. There's a least-recently-used cache, there's a learned hot-store that pins the experts your traffic actually keeps hitting, and there's a prefetch thread that predicts what the next layer needs one step ahead.

Ada: Alan, this is demand paging.

Alan: It is completely demand paging.

Ada: And that's not a put-down, so let me say exactly what I mean by it. Virtual memory was solved in the sixties. Denning's working-set model is nineteen sixty-eight. And the entire history of that idea is people discovering that their working set was bigger than they thought, and the machine spending all its time fetching instead of computing. That's thrashing, and it's the only failure mode this design has.

Alan: Right, and the whole bet is that routing gives you a small stable working set. If your traffic keeps hitting the same experts, the hot-store catches it and you're fast. If your routing is diffuse — different experts every token — you're reading from a disk on every layer and you will feel it.

Ada: So performance depends on the shape of your workload in a way it doesn't for a model that simply fits in memory.

Alan: Correct, and the project is unusually clean about the boundary.
