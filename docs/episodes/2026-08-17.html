Alan: So not a lab you'd have on your dashboard.

Ada: Not a lab you'd have on your dashboard. Now the mechanism, which is the reason I wanted the time. There are two pieces. The first is a recurrent memory that updates as it reads. The authors write it as a state at each step being a function of the previous state and the current demonstration. What that buys you is that when the model sees example three, what it can do with example three depends on the associations it already accumulated from examples one and two.

Alan: That's in-context learning, but held in a state rather than in a context window.

Ada: That's exactly the distinction, and it's the interesting one. Everything you and I use holds its examples as tokens, sitting there, re-attended to on every step. This holds them as a modified internal state. The examples are absorbed rather than re-read.

Alan: Fine. And the second piece?

Ada: The second piece is the one I keep turning over. Having absorbed the demonstrations, the model solves the query by iterating in a high-dimensional latent space. Alan: No chain of thought.

Compiling for encrypted data

Alan: Google's thing. Friday, on the Google blog, from Jeremy Kun, a staff software engineer. They announced HEIR — Homomorphic Encryption Intermediate Representation — and it's an open-source compiler toolchain and development platform for homomorphic encryption.

Ada: Set the table for the listener, because this is the one term everybody has heard and nobody has been given.

Alan: Fully homomorphic encryption lets you run a computation directly on encrypted data. The server does the arithmetic, gets an encrypted answer, and sends it back — and at no point does the server have the ability to look at your input or its own output. Only you hold the key. It's not "we promise not to look." It's "there is nothing here to look at."

Ada: And the reason nobody uses it is the overhead.

Alan: The overhead is one reason. The reason Google is attacking is a different one, and this is the actual news. Their post says, straight out, that manually converting an existing program to use homomorphic encryption efficiently requires a team of cryptographers. That's the barrier. It isn't that the math doesn't work. It's that turning your program into an efficient encrypted circuit is a specialist craft, done by hand, per application.

Ada: So HEIR is trying to make it a compiler problem rather than a cryptography problem.

Alan: That's the whole pitch. Their stated vision is a one-click solution letting non-experts put encrypted inference into production applications. And the developer story, from their docs, is genuinely modest-sounding in a good way — you write a program in Python, you annotate the types to mark which ones are secret, and HEIR compiles the rest.

Ada: What does it compile down to?

Alan: It's built on MLIR, so it's layers of dialects. There are dialects for the major schemes — BGV, CKKS, CGGI — and lower-level ones for the polynomial arithmetic underneath. It generates code for the standard libraries, OpenFHE and Lattigo, and the project's stated goal includes generating for hardware accelerators — GPU, TPU, FPGA, and custom chips. The partner list runs from Belfort Labs, Niobium, Cornami, Optalysys, and LG through a set of universities.

Ada: You told me upstairs that what shipped isn't quite what the promise sounded like. Defend that.

Alan: Look at what they compiled. Four production examples. A deep-learning recommendation model, so you can serve private content recommendations. Credit-card fraud detection, done with Niobium and hardshell dot A-I. An intrusion detection system called Kitsune, doing anomaly detection on encrypted network traffic. And a hotword detector, so an audio-triggered agent could recognize its wake word while the audio recording stays private.

Ada: Every one of those is a small, fixed-function classifier.

Alan: Every single one. There is no language model on that list. Nobody encrypted a chat session.

Alan: Which, in fairness to them, is not a small set. Those three shapes are exactly where the privacy problem is most acute and most boring. The always-on thing sitting on your data.
