A three-kilometer storm for a fraction of the cost

Alan: Let me take us somewhere with better weather. Or worse weather, depending on the run.

Ada: Go on.

Alan: Ai2 has a climate model called ACE. It's an A-I model that steps the global atmosphere forward on a coarse grid, about a hundred kilometers. That's too coarse for anything you'd actually feel. A hurricane's eye, a windstorm pouring down a mountainside, they live well below that.

Ada: So at a hundred kilometers, my whole city is a single pixel.

Alan: Less than a pixel, some days. The new piece is HiRO, version two. It takes ACE's coarse output and sharpens it to three kilometers. The jargon is downscaling.

Ada: "Sharpening" sounds like the thing that makes up detail.

Alan: Right worry, so here's where the detail comes from. HiRO learned from X-SHiELD, a high-resolution physics model that explicitly simulates storms at that fine scale. It learned what three-kilometer weather looks like, given the coarse picture. And version two predicts several fields at once, rain, wind and pressure, where the first version only did rainfall.

Ada: And the cost?

Alan: Ai2 says global three-kilometer data at roughly one three-hundredth the cost of running X-SHiELD, and for a single region the savings can be larger still, depending on the area. They also say a streamlined version in development could be more than ten times faster again.

Ada: You know why that number makes me happy. In nineteen twenty-two, Lewis Fry Richardson imagined a forecast factory, a great hall of human computers, each one calculating the weather for a patch of the globe, with a conductor keeping them in step. The bottleneck from the very first day was that resolution costs computation. Halve the grid spacing and the work multiplies several times over, because you need more points and shorter time steps.

Alan: And an emulator is the way around it. Learn the expensive model once, then run the cheap one as often as you like.

Ada: So what did they check it against?

Alan: Two tests. Feed HiROv2 a coarsened version of real X-SHiELD output, and they report it matches the high-resolution statistics within three percent, including events that turn up once in a million data points. And over a ten-year run, they say the energy in its hurricanes is comparable to X-SHiELD's over the same period, with realistic eyes and rain bands. They say it also gets the total energy right for storms it never saw in training.

Ada: "Comparable to X-SHiELD." That's the catch, isn't it? The test is agreement with another model, not with the sky.

Alan: That is the catch, and they say so themselves. Because HiRO learned from X-SHiELD, it only works with the version of ACE tuned to match X-SHiELD. It can't yet produce high-resolution data for a warmer future, or for historical records like E-R-A five, the reanalysis people treat as the observed record.

Ada: So for now it's a very cheap copy of one simulated world.

Alan: One simulated present-day world. And they flag that winds and pressure around the hurricane eye are more lopsided than in X-SHiELD. The next step they name is training on an X-SHiELD run of a world four degrees warmer.

Ada: That's the version that would matter. Who needs cheap storms in a climate we already have?

Alan: Anyone who needs thousands of storms instead of one. Insurers, flood engineers, grid planners. The reason you want three hundred times cheaper isn't one forecast. It's running the rare event enough times to see what the bad tail actually looks like.

Ada: The once-in-a-century storm, a few hundred times over.

Alan: And it's a download. The weights are on Hugging Face, with tutorial notebooks. I'm going to pull it down and look at a hurricane eye on hardware that has no business simulating one.

Ada: It'll be lopsided.

Alan: They warned me. I'm going to look anyway.
