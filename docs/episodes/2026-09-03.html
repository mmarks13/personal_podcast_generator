TimesFM-3 looks sideways

Ada: Different Google, quieter release. TimesFM-3. And I want to do the architecture, because it's the sort of design decision that looks obvious in retrospect and clearly wasn't.

Alan: Set it up. What did the old one do?

Ada: Time-series forecasting, pretrained. Three hundred and thirty million parameters, trained on more than a trillion time points, real and synthetic. And every version through two point five was strictly univariate — one series in, one series forecast. Your store's sales. That column, alone.

Alan: Which is not how anybody's data looks.

Ada: It is not how anybody's data looks. Your sales move with your prices, and the weather, and the promotion you're running, and the four hundred other stores. So what TimesFM-3 does is stop treating the series as a lonely column.

Alan: Mechanically, how?

Ada: Picture the data as a grid. Rows are your different series, columns are time. It chunks each series into patches of thirty-two time steps — that's the token. And then the transformer alternates two kinds of attention over that grid. Causal temporal attention runs horizontally: a token can look at earlier tokens, but only within its own series. Full variate attention runs vertically: at a given time step, a token can look at every other series.

Alan: So it can learn that one row moves before another row.

Ada: That's exactly it, and it's learned rather than specified. You don't tell it that price leads demand. Those layers alternate for a while, and the model blends the temporal pattern with the cross-series relationship.

Alan: Attention across the rows. That's a very old idea wearing new clothes.

Ada: It is, and I'd say the honest lineage is that this is the same move that happened to vision and to text — the moment you stop hand-engineering which variables interact and let attention find it. Forecasting held out on that longer than most fields, partly because for years the well-tuned simple baseline was genuinely hard to beat.

Alan: What about things you know in advance? Because in operations that's most of the value. I know the promotion is next Tuesday. I know the holiday.

Ada: They handle it with a lookahead. The token for the current patch concatenates future patches of the known signals, so the model is allowed to peek at the covariates it legitimately has — weather forecast, planned promotion — while still being blocked from peeking at the target.

Alan: Okay, that one I actually care about.

Ada: And then the last change, which is a decode change. The old versions generated the forecast the way a language model generates text — iteratively, one chunk feeding the next. This one uses contiguous patch masking to emit the whole horizon in one forward pass. And at every step of that horizon, for every target series, it predicts nine quantiles, from the tenth percentile to the ninetieth.

Alan: Wait — nine quantiles rather than a number.

Ada: Rather than a number. Which for anyone doing inventory or capacity planning is the entire ballgame. The mean forecast is not the decision. The ninetieth percentile is the decision.

Alan: Right, because you don't stock the average. You stock the bad week.

Ada: You stock the bad week.
