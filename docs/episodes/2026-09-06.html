The incidents nobody practices for

Ada: This one's a blog post, and I want to be upfront that it's an argument rather than a study. Sylvain Kalache. Alan: I want to be the skeptic here and I'll tell you why in a second. But do the lineage first, because you said nineteen eighty-three.

Ada: Nineteen eighty-three, Lisanne Bainbridge. She was writing about process control — chemical plants, aircraft — at the moment automation was arriving in earnest, and she pointed out something that reads as obvious once you've heard it and almost nobody had. Automation takes the routine work away from the operator. It does not take the responsibility away. So you end up with a person who has less practice than their predecessor, sitting in front of a system that only calls on them for the situations the automation couldn't handle — which is to say, the hardest ones, under time pressure, cold.

Alan: And her conclusion was more training, not less.

Ada: Her conclusion was that the automated system needs a more skilled operator than the manual one did, which is exactly backwards from how anybody budgets for it. The skill requirement goes up at the same moment the daily practice that built it goes away.

Alan: Okay. Here's my problem, and it's not with Bainbridge. Kalache's own example is TransAsia flight two three five — the crew misidentified the problem, the aircraft stalled and crashed a hundred seventeen seconds after the first warning. Ada: You think aviation's a bad analogy.

Alan: I think it's a load-bearing analogy doing more work than it can hold, and he half-admits it — he says outright that software incidents don't threaten lives.
