Alan: They're in tension. But go at the mechanism first, because you spent an hour on it yesterday and I want you to break it.

Ada: All right. When you distill through an API, you don't get the teacher. You get samples. The thing that makes distillation powerful in the original formulation is the full distribution — the teacher's probabilities across every possible next token, including the ones it rejected, because the relative weight of the wrong answers is where the learned structure lives. An API gives you one draw. One sampled token, which is a single-sample estimate of that distribution. That's why the modern version of this leans on reasoning traces instead — long chains of text that carry more signal per call than a bare answer does.

Alan: So a legal American distillation regime buys the open labs what, exactly?

Ada: It buys them a very expensive, very lossy photocopier. Which is not nothing — it's evidently enough to move a model a long way, six agencies' worth of concern says so. But Tan's framing is that access to the intelligence should be a public good, and what's actually flowing through that pipe is not the intelligence. It's an approximation of the intelligence, priced by the token, and the person selling it sets the price.
