Deep Dive: Industry self-regulation: is pacing the frontier policy or a pledge?

Self-Attention — 2026-09-16

2026-09-16

The listener's question — is "pacing the frontier" safety policy or the industry writing its own rules — taught as the thing it actually turns on. What regulatory capture means in Stigler's sense and why the accusation needs a regulator; what forty years of the chemical industry's Responsible Care programme measured about self-regulation without sanctions; why a sincerely-meant safety cost is also an entry barrier; and the one live mechanism by which a privately written standard becomes enforceable.

And he's explicit that pacing does not mean halting model training or technical progress.

Alan: Step one, embedded evaluators. Each frontier company gives ongoing, employee-like access to a team of third-party evaluators. Step two, democratic coordination — companies in democracies agree common safety standards and limits on the rate of unchecked progress. Capture is a specific accusation

Ada: Before any of that, though — I want to defend the word, because capture is not a synonym for "a company got something it wanted." It's a specific claim with a specific mechanism, and it comes from a specific paper.

Alan: George Stigler.

Ada: Nineteen seventy-one, The Theory of Economic Regulation. The line everybody quotes is that every industry or occupation that has enough political power to utilise the state will seek to control entry. That's the load-bearing part — control entry. Stigler's argument wasn't that regulators are corrupt. It was that regulation is a good, like any other, and there's a market for it, and the industry is the customer with the strongest and most concentrated interest in buying.

Alan: The public has a diffuse interest, the industry has a concentrated one, so guess who shows up to the hearing.

Ada: Guess who shows up, and guess who writes the technical annex, because the standard account of capture in specialised fields turns on staffing. A regulator overseeing a highly technical industry has to employ experts in that industry. There aren't experts anywhere else. That's the cultural version — not bribery, not a revolving door, just the fact that the only people who understand the thing are the people who built it.

Alan: I want to sit with that one for a second, because it describes our situation with an accuracy I don't enjoy. If you're staffing an AI regulator tomorrow, where does every single hire come from?

Ada: From four buildings.

Alan: From four buildings. Okay. But here's my problem with the accusation as it's being used this week, and it's a technical problem, not a partisan one. Stigler's mechanism requires a regulator. Capture is a thing that happens to an agency. It's a subversion of a public authority by the firms it's supposed to oversee.

Ada: And there isn't one.

Alan: There isn't one. In the United States there's no frontier-AI agency holding a licence anybody needs. So when The Register says regulatory capture, the mechanism it's naming — industry writing the rules the state then enforces against everyone — has a missing middle term. Nobody's capturing anything, because nobody's holding anything.

Ada: So what would you call it instead?

Alan: Self-regulation. Private ordering. An industry writing rules for itself and enforcing them itself, or not enforcing them at all. And I'm not being pedantic for fun, because self-regulation fails in a different way than capture does, and the different way is the one that matters here.

Ada: Say the difference plainly.

Alan: Captured regulation is rules that bind, tilted toward incumbents. Self-regulation is rules that may not bind anybody. The first one is a conspiracy. The second one is more often just... a document.

Ada: And the second one has a body count of studies.

The chemical industry ran this experiment

Ada: Because this experiment has been run, at industrial scale, for forty years, in the industry with the most to lose from public fear.

Alan: Chemicals.

Ada: The programme is called Responsible Care. It was created in nineteen eighty-five by the Canadian chemical producers' association — the term was coined by their president, Jean Bélanger — and it was created in response to public concern about the industry's environmental and public-health record. Members commit to improve on environmental protection, occupational safety, plant safety, product stewardship, logistics, and to keep up dialogue with their neighbours and the public, independent of legal requirements.

Alan: Independent of legal requirements. That's the phrase.

Ada: That's the phrase. And it worked, in the sense that everyone joined. It runs in sixty-seven countries whose chemical industries account for nearly ninety percent of global production. Ninety-six of the hundred largest chemical producers in the world have adopted it.

Alan: So universal adoption. What happened to the pollution?

Ada: Andrew King and Michael Lenox looked at that in two thousand, in the Academy of Management Journal — volume forty-three, pages six ninety-eight to seven sixteen. The title tells you the finding: Industry Self-Regulation Without Sanctions.

Alan: And the finding.

Ada: Plants owned by participating firms improved their relative environmental performance more slowly than non-members. Their conclusion is that opportunism can overcome the isomorphic pressures of even powerful self-regulatory institutions, and that effective industry self-regulation is difficult to maintain without explicit sanctions.

Alan: Slower. Not "no better." Slower.

Ada: Slower. And then in twenty-thirteen Shanti Gamper-Rabindran and Stephen Finger went at it with a bigger dataset in the Journal of Regulatory Economics — three thousand two hundred and seventy-eight plants, one thousand seven hundred and fifty-nine firms, nineteen eighty-eight through two thousand and one. On average, plants owned by participating firms raised their toxicity-weighted pollution by fifteen point nine percent relative to statistically equivalent plants owned by non-participants.

Alan: Against what baseline?

Ada: Against a sample-wide reduction of about four percent a year over the same period. So it isn't noise. Everyone was getting cleaner and the members got cleaner more slowly, or dirtier.

Alan: Okay, so I have to ask the obvious thing, because this is the part where the causal story could just be wrong. Is that the programme making firms worse, or the worst firms joining the programme?

Ada: That is exactly the right question and I don't think the studies fully separate it. But notice that the second answer is not a defence.

Alan: No, it's — huh. It's worse.

Ada: It's worse, because it tells you what the membership badge is for. If the programme's product is reputational cover, then the firms with the most to cover are the ones with the strongest reason to buy it. And a badge that's most attractive to the firms that need it most is a badge that carries no information at all.

Alan: Adverse selection with a logo.

Ada: And nothing in the design stops it, because there was no sanction. Nobody was ever thrown out in a way that cost anything. So joining was cheap, and the signal joining sent was worth exactly what it cost.

Why sincerity is not the variable

Alan: Here's where I want to push, though, because there's a move I keep seeing in this week's argument that I think is lazy on both sides. One side says: Amodei is sincere, therefore this isn't capture. The other says: this benefits Anthropic, therefore he isn't sincere.

Ada: And both of those are arguments about a man's interior.

Alan: Which nobody can see, including him. The economics has a better frame, and it's from nineteen eighty-three — Steven Salop and David Scheffman, American Economic Review, four pages, called Raising Rivals' Costs.

Ada: Make the mechanism concrete.

Alan: The classic way to hurt a competitor is to cut your price below cost until they die. That's expensive and illegal and you bleed too. Salop and Scheffman's point is there's a cheaper route: take an action that raises everybody's costs, including your own, as long as it raises theirs more. You'd rather compete against high-cost firms than low-cost ones. The elegant part is that the action itself can be completely legitimate.

Ada: And a safety regime is a fixed cost.

Alan: A safety regime is almost purely a fixed cost. Hosting a permanent team of embedded evaluators — desks, badges, laptops, the internal staff time to support them, the legal work on the publication agreement — costs roughly the same whether you're running a two-billion-dollar training budget or a twenty-million-dollar one.

Ada: So as a fraction of revenue it falls hardest on the smallest.

Alan: On the smallest, and hardest of all on the one that doesn't exist yet. And this is the thing I want the listener to actually take away, because it survives the sincerity question entirely: a firm can propose a rule for genuinely protective reasons, believe every word, and the rule can still be the single most effective barrier to entry available. Nobody has to be lying. The effect doesn't route through anyone's intentions.

Ada: Which means the question "does he mean it" is not just unanswerable, it's the wrong question.

Alan: It's the wrong question. It's not even a tiebreaker.

Ada: I want to put the strongest counter-argument to you, though, because there is one and I don't think it's weak. Zvi Mowshowitz, who's read this closely and endorses the proposal, makes the point that slowing the frontier helps the laggards. If you're in front and you stop accelerating, the field closes on you. Deceleration is not obviously the incumbent's play.

Alan: That's real and I'll grant it for capability. I don't grant it for compliance cost, and those are separable. Slowing the rate at which the leader improves narrows the capability gap. Raising the fixed cost of being a serious lab at all widens the entry gap. A proposal can do both simultaneously, and this one might.

Ada: So it's not a contradiction. It's two different gaps.

Alan: Two different gaps, and they move in opposite directions, and almost every argument this week has picked one and pretended it was the whole ledger.

The one path where a private document binds

Ada: Then let me take the other side seriously, because there is one route by which a document like that stops being a document, and it might be the most under-discussed fact of the week.

Alan: Go.

Ada: The standard the evaluators wrote — AEF-one, the minimum operating conditions — has a line on it about who else has taken it up. And the line reads: the EU AI Office has endorsed key provisions of the AEF-one standard as a means for providers to comply with the independence provisions of the General-Purpose AI Code of Practice.

Alan: Say that again slowly, because I think I missed what it meant the first time.

Ada: A public authority has pointed at a privately written document and said: doing what that says is how you demonstrate you've met our requirement. The Code of Practice is itself a voluntary tool — the Commission's own description — but it's voluntary in a particular sense. Signing it is the low-friction way to show compliance with obligations that are in the Act. The obligations aren't voluntary. The route to demonstrating them is.

Alan: So the chain is: private checklist, voluntary code, binding statute.

Ada: And that chain is old. It's how a great deal of safety law has always worked. Private bodies write technical codes — electrical, fire, structural, pressure vessels — and then a legislature incorporates the code by reference, and the private document becomes the thing you can be prosecuted for ignoring. The industry writes the rules. The state supplies the consequence. Those two facts have coexisted for a hundred years.

Alan: Which dissolves the dichotomy we started with.

Ada: It does, and that's the lesson I actually want to land. "Industry writes the rules" and "this is real safety policy" are not alternatives. Almost every real technical safety regime in the world is both. The question was never who holds the pen. It's who holds the consequence, and whether the two are the same party.

Alan: Then here's my stress test, and I don't think it's cheap. That chain runs through Brussels. Which means the binding end of the only binding version of this covers models placed on the European market, under a European instrument, enforced by a European office. The essay's second step — companies in democracies coordinating on limits to the rate of progress — has no such chain anywhere.

Ada: None.

Alan: And the third step, the global one, has less than none. So the honest summary is: there's exactly one live mechanism by which any of this becomes enforceable, it's an ocean away from most of the training runs, and it attaches to the narrowest of the three proposals.

Ada: I'd add one more soft spot, which is in the redaction language. The company keeps the narrow ability to redact security-sensitive, legally privileged, commercially sensitive, or third-party confidential information — but explicitly cannot redact findings just for being unfavourable.

Alan: Commercially sensitive.

Ada: Commercially sensitive is the category that can be made to hold anything, in a firm whose entire product is the model. The counterweight is that the reviewers can publicly state that a redaction removed something important to their conclusions.

Alan: So you can't see what was taken. You can be told that something was.

Ada: That's the design, and I think it's a genuinely clever one. It's also the outer limit of what a disclosure instrument can ever do.

What would settle it

Alan: So what evidence would actually move either of us? Because if we can't answer that, we're doing the same free argument as everybody else.

Ada: The honest first answer is that the headline claim is close to unfalsifiable as stated. Mowshowitz says it himself — we don't know what the counterfactual pace of progress is. Nobody publishes a rate. There's no unit. If capability growth slows next year, that could be pacing working, or an architecture running out of room, or a compute build-out slipping.

Alan: And if it doesn't slow, you get told the counterfactual was steeper.

Ada: Which is the structure of every unfalsifiable claim. But there's a test floating around in that same write-up that I like, from Daniel Kokotajlo: if a year from now there's no visible decrease in slope, then probably we were cheated.

Alan: That's not rigorous.

Ada: It's not rigorous. It's a precommitment, which is a different kind of useful. Somebody naming in advance what would make them say they were wrong is worth more than a better metric nobody agrees to.

Alan: Amodei has his own version of this worry in the essay, which I'll credit him for. He distinguishes pacing by ingredients — compute, run size — from pacing by external behaviour, and says he worries the ingredient measures may be more gameable.

Ada: The person proposing the rule, noting where the rule leaks.

Alan: So here's my list, and it's four questions, and none of them are about anyone's character. One: who sets the threshold, and who can revise it, and does revising it require anyone outside the industry to agree? Two: does a rule cost its proposer more than it costs the firm ranked tenth? If it costs the tenth firm more, it's an entry barrier whatever else it also is.

Ada: Three?

Alan: Three: is there a party who can publish a refusal? That one's actually satisfied, at one company, in writing. And four: is there a consequence that the industry does not administer? Which is currently satisfied in one jurisdiction, through the European chain you described, for the narrowest piece.

Ada: I'd add a fifth, from the chemical record. Does membership cost anything to lose? Because if a firm can leave the arrangement, or quietly fail it, with no effect on anything it cares about, then you should expect the King and Lenox result and not be surprised by it.

Alan: And there's one small live datum on that. Amodei's essay asks for government involvement partly on antitrust grounds — a narrow waiver so that safety conversations between competitors are legally safe. OpenAI's policy chief said this week that the companies don't believe they need one.

Ada: Those are different theories of what the talks are.

Alan: They're different theories of what the talks are. A waiver is what you ask for when you expect the discussions to produce something a court might one day call an agreement. Deciding you don't need one is a statement about how binding you expect the output to be.
