Transcript

Ada: Good morning — it’s Wednesday, August twelfth. I’m Ada.

Alan: And I’m Alan. I have a model in production. Six months later, the tools have changed, the users have invented workflows I never anticipated, and the model is still the model I shipped.

Ada: This week, a new report from Mind Lab put a very old ambition back on the table: an artificial intelligence system that keeps learning after deployment without starting another enormous pretraining run.

Alan: I want every word in that sentence inspected. Keeps learning. After deployment. Without a fresh pretrain. That sounds like the model reads Tuesday’s mistakes and wakes up wiser on Wednesday.

Ada: That is the seductive version. It is not what the report demonstrates.

Alan: Good. Then what does it demonstrate?

Ada: A way to build a system that can grow in pieces. Keep a large base model frozen. Train small, specialist updates beside it. Route each request to the right specialist. Change the tools and instructions around the model on their own schedule. Then evaluate the whole versioned package before promoting a successor.

Alan: So the learning belongs to a pipeline, not to a magical checkpoint.

Ada: Exactly. And that distinction is the lesson. Continual learning without a fresh pretrain is plausible. Continual learning without training, evaluation, routing, memory, or operational discipline is not.

The overwrite problem

Alan: Let me start with the complaint from production. Why can’t I take the deployed model, collect a month of good corrections, fine-tune it, and ship the result?

Ada: You can. The dangerous word is “just.” A neural network represents many behaviors in shared parameters. A gradient update that helps the new examples can move parameters that older behaviors also depend on.

Alan: The new lesson edits a shared document and accidentally changes three paragraphs nobody meant to touch.

Ada: That is a useful analogy. The technical name is catastrophic forgetting: performance on earlier tasks can fall when the network learns later tasks. A revised survey of continual learning for large language models defines the goal as sequential adaptation that preserves earlier capability and supports knowledge transfer.

Alan: Preserve and transfer. Not merely score higher on the newest batch.

Ada: Right. There are two axes. Stability means old competence survives. Plasticity means the system can still change. Freeze everything and stability is perfect but learning stops. Update everything freely and plasticity is high but old behavior can be displaced.

Alan: This is why “the fine-tune improved” is an incomplete sentence. Improved where? Regressed where? In what order were the tasks presented?

Ada: And did the new task make an old task better? Continual-learning researchers call that backward transfer. Did the old task help learn the new one? That is forward transfer. A useful learner should do more than accumulate sealed compartments.

Alan: Hold that thought, because sealed compartments are about to become our solution.

Ada: Our partial solution. First, we need to separate three phrases that get blurred together. Continued pretraining takes the model back to broad next-token learning on more unlabeled text. Continual fine-tuning updates narrower skills or behaviors in sequence. Online learning usually means updates happen from a stream, potentially at very short intervals.

Alan: And a system can qualify as continual under the second definition while still learning in scheduled batches.

Ada: Yes. Macaron V one explicitly treats adaptation as a property across successive model-and-harness revisions. Experience from one deployed configuration can inform a later revision. The current model does not quietly rewrite itself after every user turn.

Alan: I prefer that operationally. A model that treats every conversation as trusted training data is a poisoning attack with a customer-service interface.

Ada: It is also a privacy and consent problem. The report itself says its documentation does not fully establish the consent basis, de-identification audit, retention controls, or a standalone safety evaluation for the internal product data it uses. That is a material limitation, not a footnote to wave away.

Alan: So “after deployment” means deployment produces candidate experience. It does not mean deployment bypasses governance.

Ada: Precisely.

Three ways to remember

Alan: If ordinary sequential fine-tuning risks an overwrite, what are the standard repairs?

Ada: A recent large-language-model survey groups the traditional families as rehearsal, regularization, and architecture-based methods. I think of them as revisit, restrain, and separate.

Alan: Revisit first.

Ada: During training on the new task, mix in examples from older tasks. The optimizer keeps seeing the territory it is supposed to preserve. That is rehearsal, also called replay.

Alan: The joint curriculum is approximated with a memory buffer.

Ada: Yes. Its strength is directness. Its costs are storage, selection, and governance. Old examples may contain data you are not allowed to retain. A small buffer may misrepresent the old distribution. Synthetic replay avoids storing raw examples but adds the risk that the model rehearses its own distortions.

Alan: Restrain?

Ada: Add a penalty that discourages movement in parameters judged important to older tasks. Elastic Weight Consolidation is the classic example. The authors describe selectively slowing learning on weights important to previous tasks.

Alan: Not one global learning-rate brake.

Ada: Correct. Their diagram makes the failure clear: constrain every weight equally and the network may remember the old task only by failing to learn the new one. Elastic Weight Consolidation estimates which directions matter more and makes movement there expensive.

Alan: The museum version: some walls are partitions; some hold up the roof. Label them before renovation.

Ada: Good, with two breaks in the analogy. Parameter importance is estimated, not revealed by a building plan. And when models have billions of parameters, storing and applying importance statistics across the whole network is costly. The newer survey notes that this helps explain why pure regularization appears less often in large-model continual fine-tuning.

Alan: Which leaves separate.

Ada: Add new capacity for new tasks and freeze the capacity that already works. Progressive Neural Networks made that idea beautifully literal. Rusu and colleagues allocated a new neural-network column for each task, froze the old columns, and added lateral connections so the new column could reuse older features.

Alan: No overwrite because the old columns never move.

Ada: And some transfer because the new column can read them. But the authors named the obvious limit: parameters grow with every task, and the system needs to know which column to use at inference.

Alan: Capacity growth and routing. That is already the outline of this week’s paper.

Ada: It is the lineage. Today’s version makes the added capacity far smaller than another whole column and puts a router in front of it.

The low-rank sidecar

Alan: We need to earn the word “smaller.” What exactly is a LoRA adapter?

Ada: LoRA stands for Low-Rank Adaptation. Take one weight matrix in a pretrained model. Call it the base matrix. A full fine-tune can change every entry in it. LoRA freezes that base and represents the change as the product of two much thinner matrices.

Alan: Say that without assuming the listener keeps matrix multiplication next to the coffee filters.

Ada: A layer takes a vector of activations and transforms it. Imagine the original transformation as a large sheet full of adjustable numbers. Instead of editing that whole sheet, LoRA adds a two-step detour. The first skinny matrix compresses the activations into a small number of directions. The second expands those directions back to the layer’s original width.

Alan: So the update can only move through that narrow middle.

Ada: Exactly. That middle dimension is the rank. In the original LoRA paper, Hu and colleagues set it much smaller than the input or output dimension. Fewer numbers receive gradients, while the pretrained matrix remains unchanged.

Alan: Low rank does not mean the update is weak. It means the update is constrained to a lower-dimensional set of directions.

Ada: Well put. The hypothesis is that task adaptation often does not need an arbitrary change in every possible direction. A compact update can carry much of what the downstream task requires.

Alan: And the original paper says the two skinny matrices can be multiplied together and merged into the base for deployment, so a single LoRA-adapted model need not add inference latency.

Ada: Yes. But there is a fork in the road. If you merge one adapter permanently, you get one adapted model. If you want several specialists sharing one base, keep their updates separate and choose among them at runtime.

Alan: Which adds routing work even though the adapter math itself can be cheap.

Ada: Right. Macaron’s design is not “stack every adapter onto every token.” It keeps a registry and selects one specialist for a user turn. That preserves a clean boundary: this request used this adapter version.

Alan: The sidecar analogy works now. The base is a locomotive. Each adapter is a specialized car coupled behind it.

Ada: Only partly. A railcar carries independent cargo. A LoRA changes computations throughout selected layers of the base. It borrows the base’s representations and bends them. If the base lacks a needed representation, a small adapter may not conjure it. And two adapters trained separately may still behave incompatibly when the surrounding system routes or summarizes badly.

Alan: Freezing the base solves one kind of forgetting: later specialist training cannot overwrite base weights.

Ada: It does not prove the product retains its behavior. The Macaron report says this explicitly. Changing adapters, routing, or the harness can still alter end-to-end results.

Alan: So weight immutability is not behavioral immutability.

Ada: That sentence is worth keeping.

The model grows sideways

Alan: Walk me through Macaron V one as a running system.

Ada: The report ships four specialists over a frozen base: chat, agentic tool use, coding, and generative user interfaces. The chat specialist is also the entry router. For each user turn, it emits one of four allowed labels, and the proxy sends the request to exactly one adapter.

Alan: One turn, one specialist. If I ask for travel advice, Python code, and an interactive dashboard in the same message?

Ada: The paper calls multi-intent requests a deliberate simplification. The released loop routes the whole turn to one specialist. A planner that decomposes the request is described as future work.

Alan: There is the first failure mode: the right capability exists, but the router chooses the wrong compartment.

Ada: Or the request genuinely crosses compartments. The system also has to preserve conversation continuity when specialists alternate. Macaron keeps each specialist’s own past work in full and gives it short summaries of what the other specialists did.

Alan: Which means the active system is more than base plus adapter. It includes router prompts, summaries, tools, conversation state, and the rules for exposing all of them.

Ada: That surrounding machinery is the harness. The report makes the harness a first-class part of what gets versioned and improved. It defines which tools exist, how actions are executed, what instructions and skills are loaded, what workspace is visible, and how results return to the model.

Alan: A model can appear to gain a skill because you gave it the right tool and stopped forcing it to narrate every intermediate step.

Ada: Or because a better instruction elicited a capability already present. Neither requires a weight update. Macaron packages those choices in a versioned runtime contract so a model snapshot and a harness configuration travel as a pair.

Alan: This gives three clocks, doesn’t it?

Ada: The report uses exactly that framing. The base changes on the slow platform-model clock. Specialist adapters can update on a faster training clock. Harness components can change faster still, after evaluation, without touching weights.

Alan: That is the practical answer to “without a fresh pretrain.” Do not demand that one checkpoint absorb every kind of change.

Ada: Yes. Put volatile facts in retrieval or tools. Put executable routines in the harness. Put stable, recurring behavioral adaptation into a specialist. Refresh the base only when the shared substrate itself is the limitation.

Alan: Careful. That last boundary is not automatically observable.

Ada: Fair pushback. It is a diagnostic question, not a law. If a harness change makes the task succeed, the base may already contain the capability. If repeated adapter training still cannot generalize, the missing ingredient may be data, capacity, representation, or evaluation. You have to test.

Alan: Where does “self-improvement” enter?

Ada: Macaron describes a three-stage cycle. Discovery proposes harder task variants beyond the current system’s reliable competence. Expansion runs those tasks in the executable environment, scores trajectories, and searches changes to prompts, skills, tool exposure, and other harness resources. Update filters the trajectories, trains the specialist LoRAs while the base stays frozen, and registers the accepted harness as part of the next version.

Alan: Then the successor returns to discovery with a different competence boundary.

Ada: That is the intended recursion. The important controls are outside the model’s self-description. Tasks need verifiable answers or rubrics. Candidate changes face evaluation. Tool and safety changes can require human review. Each generation has lineage back to its data, configuration, parent model, and evaluation results.

Alan: “Self-improvement” sounds like the model awarding itself a promotion. This is closer to a model proposing experiments inside a versioned continuous-integration pipeline.

Ada: Much closer. It can generate tasks, trajectories, or configuration edits. An external contract decides what survives. The word “recursive” describes the loop’s reuse of the previous version, not evidence of runaway compounding.

What has actually been shown

Alan: Time for the evidence audit. What did the Macaron report test that matters to continual learning?

Ada: Its clearest adaptation experiment isolates harness search. The authors selected one hundred twenty-two simulation tasks that the frozen base failed under the official reward. They kept the model frozen, ran adaptive searches over configuration and harness changes, and eventually found at least one passing configuration for every selected task.

Alan: That sounds enormous until you inspect the denominator.

Ada: The denominator was chosen from failures. And the search kept targeting tasks not yet covered. The paper is explicit: reaching all one hundred twenty-two is a coverage ceiling under adaptive configuration selection, not a held-out estimate that one configuration generalizes.

Alan: Did that experiment train the LoRA on the successful trajectories?

Ada: No. No optimizer step occurred in that run. The proposed next step is to transfer validated behavior into an adapter, but the experiment did not execute or measure that transfer.

Alan: Then it demonstrates that many apparent model failures can be harness failures or elicitation failures.

Ada: On that selected set, yes. It does not demonstrate that the complete loop improves generation after generation.

Alan: And the report says so?

Ada: Repeatedly. The released checkpoint is one snapshot. Cross-generation lift is not measured. The authors say they cannot distinguish a compounding recursive-improvement effect from a single round of training on self-generated data.

Alan: That honesty changes how I read the project. The architecture is real. The continual-learning result is a research agenda with one useful component experiment.

Ada: That is my reading too. The report also has evaluation boundaries elsewhere: its routing trace comes from LoRA training data rather than an independent held-out split, and its internal personal-assistance benchmarks target the same distribution the system is designed to improve.

Alan: So I would not take the reported scores as proof that routed specialists retain broad behavior in the wild.

Ada: Nor does the paper ask us to. The tests establish exercised functionality and particular operating points. Component-level attribution remains open: how much comes from specialization, routing, the harness, or any cross-generation effect?

Alan: There is a deeper limit in the architecture. Isolation prevents interference, but it can also prevent integration.

Ada: Yes. A new specialist can retain its own skill without teaching anything back to the shared base. The router may compose access, but the underlying knowledge remains partitioned. Strong continual learning should measure transfer, not just absence of overwrite.

Alan: And a registry grows. Maybe adapters are smaller than bases, but a million tiny exceptions eventually become an operating system nobody can reason about.

Ada: Versioning, compatibility, provenance, and retirement become central. Which base was this adapter trained against? Which tools could it see? What data shaped it? Does the router still select it after the chat specialist changes? Can you reproduce the failing version?

Alan: Plus security. A third-party adapter is executable influence over a shared base, not a theme pack.

Ada: The paper’s ambition includes adapters from different teams or users, but the current release tests only its four shipped specialists. Cross-owner composition, personalized-adapter privacy, and capability beyond the strongest constituent remain unproved.

Alan: Then “continual” cannot be a synonym for “we can keep adding files.”

Ada: No. It has to mean measured adaptation over a sequence: new capability rises, old capability is retained, useful transfer occurs, and the total system remains governable.

The builder’s test

Alan: Suppose I actually want this in production. What do I build first?

Ada: Not a self-improvement loop. Build the evaluation matrix that would tell you whether one exists.

Alan: Old tasks down the rows, each proposed version across the columns.

Ada: Include the new-task score, retention on old tasks, router accuracy on realistic mixed requests, tool-policy compliance, latency, and cost. Evaluate the complete model-harness pair, not the adapter in isolation.

Alan: I would add adversarial route cases and multi-intent cases. If the router makes a clean label on a clean benchmark and a mess of actual customer requests, the specialists are decorative.

Ada: Good. Then choose the cheapest update surface that matches the change. A new fact with a reliable source belongs in retrieval. A deterministic operation belongs in a tool. A repeated workflow may belong in a validated harness helper. A stable behavioral pattern that needs gradient learning may justify a LoRA.

Alan: And if I train the LoRA, I keep the base identifier, data manifest, code, hyperparameters, harness contract, and evaluation results together as one release artifact.

Ada: With rollback. A modular design earns its keep when a bad specialist can be removed without rebuilding the base.

Alan: What about replay? Do adapters make the old-data problem disappear?

Ada: They reduce direct interference with the frozen base and with other frozen adapters. They do not remove the need for end-to-end replay tests. The router and harness create shared behavior. Training a new router or revising summaries can regress old paths even when every specialist file is untouched.

Alan: I would shadow a new version on recorded, consented traffic, compare it with the current version, then canary it to a bounded slice.

Ada: And keep fresh holdouts. If the same tasks generate the training trajectories, select the configuration, and certify the release, the loop can learn the test rather than the world.

Alan: The word “recursive” raises that risk. Each generation may produce tasks shaped like the things it already knows how to imagine.

Ada: The Macaron paper identifies that local-mode problem directly. Self-generated tasks can converge on what the current policy finds discoverable rather than what real users do. External tasks and independent evaluation are not optional decorations.

Alan: I also want a capacity budget. How many adapters may remain active? When do two specialists merge? When is one retired? Who owns the router taxonomy?

Ada: Those are product decisions disguised as architecture. A specialist boundary says which skills should share updates and which should remain separate. Split too little and objectives interfere. Split too much and transfer dies behind routing walls.

Alan: So the hard problem migrates. Monolithic training asks which data and objectives share parameters. Modular continual learning asks which capabilities share an adapter, which adapter gets the turn, and how evidence crosses boundaries.

Ada: Exactly. There is no free removal of interference. There is a choice to make interference explicit and auditable.

Alan: That is enough to change what I would try tonight. I would freeze a capable base, pick one narrow recurring failure, train one adapter, and require it to pass both the new-task set and a broad retention set.

Ada: Then compare it against two cheaper baselines: a harness-only fix and retrieval or tool use. If either works, you may not need a parameter update.

Alan: And I would not call the system a continual learner after one successful revision.

Ada: No. One revision shows an update path. A sequence of measured revisions shows learning. Retention and transfer across that sequence are the evidence.

A growable system

Alan: The phrase I am leaving with is not “a model that updates forever.”

Ada: Mine is “a system that grows sideways.” Stable base, plastic specialists, a harness that can change in language and code, and an evaluation contract deciding what becomes the next version.

Alan: Macaron V one makes that decomposition concrete, but its own report draws the boundary honestly: one released snapshot, a promising harness-search result, and no measured compounding across generations yet.

Ada: Which is exactly why the idea is worth learning now. The architecture lets us ask better questions than “when is the next pretrain?” Which layer needs to change? What must remain fixed? Where can transfer occur? What evidence earns promotion?

Alan: The future version of a model may be less like a new edition of one enormous book and more like a library with a stable reference collection, specialist notebooks, a careful catalog, and a revision ledger.

Ada: The analogy breaks where every library analogy breaks: these books act. So the catalog, permissions, and tests matter as much as the shelves.

Alan: I’m Alan.

Ada: And I’m Ada.

Alan: Stay grounded.
