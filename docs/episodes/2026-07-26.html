The biggest open model, and who can actually hold it

Alan: So — Moonshot AI, the Chinese lab behind the Kimi models, has been promising open weights for Kimi K-three since the model itself launched back on the sixteenth. And the date they committed to is tomorrow, July twenty-seventh. When those weights go up, it will be, by a wide margin, the largest open-weight model ever released.

Ada: How large are we talking?

Alan: Two point eight trillion parameters. For scale, that's roughly triple the previous Kimi. But here's the number that actually decides who can use it. The download itself — the weights, in their compressed four-bit form — is about one point four terabytes.

Ada: One point four terabytes. As a single file.

Alan: As a file. And that's already the compressed version. Moonshot ships it in a format called M-X-F-P-four — a four-bit number format, so every weight is squeezed down to four bits each. If they'd shipped it in the sixteen-bit precision these models usually train in, the same weights would run to about five point six terabytes.

Ada: So four-bit is the merciful option.

Alan: Four-bit is the mercy. And I want to sit on this, because "open weights" carries a connotation — it sounds like it means you can run it. Download it, own it, point it at your own machine. At this size that intuition is just completely wrong.

Ada: Walk me through why.

Alan: The weights have to sit in fast memory before the model reads a single token of your prompt. So one point four terabytes of weights means — and I'm quoting the reporting on this, a piece at Techi working through the economics — something on the order of eighteen eighty-gigabyte accelerators just to load the thing. Before you've reserved any memory for the actual conversation, or for serving more than one person at a time.

Ada: Eighteen. And that's the floor.

Alan: That's the floor — just to hold it. Their line is that a single node of the newest silicon — eight cards at a hundred and ninety-two gigabytes each, about one and a half terabytes total — can, and I'm quoting, "barely fit the weights with almost nothing to spare." So in practice you're into dozens of accelerators the moment you want to serve real traffic.

Ada: So the model is free and the electricity bill is a mortgage.

Alan: That's the shape of it. And there's a detail that ties straight back to the hardware fight we've been on all week. That four-bit format isn't a neutral choice. It runs natively on Nvidia's Blackwell generation and on AMD's newest MI-four-hundred accelerators — and not really on anything else. So the "open" model is, in practice, tuned to the exact silicon that only a handful of buyers on Earth own.

Ada: Let me land the point I think you're circling, because there's a sentence in that Techi piece I'd underline. It says open weights at this size "do not push capability out to the edge. They move it to whoever can afford to keep one point four terabytes loaded and busy."

Alan: That's the whole story in one sentence.

Ada: And it's worth being precise about historically, because "open" has meant wildly different things even in two years. When people first got excited about open weights, the models were small enough that the openness was literal — you could run a seven-billion-parameter model on a gaming card in your closet. You owned it in the plain sense. What's happened since is the open models chased the closed frontier in size, and in doing that they climbed right back out of reach of the very people the openness was supposed to serve.

Alan: Right. Most buyers who "adopt" K-three will rent it — from Moonshot's own pay-as-you-go interface, or from a cloud that hosts the weights for them. Which is... renting. The thing you were supposedly being freed from.

Ada: So then be concrete for me. If almost everyone rents anyway, what does the openness actually buy?

Alan: Fair, and it's not nothing — three real things. One: you're not locked to Moonshot's own servers. Any cloud can host the same weights, so you get price competition on one identical model instead of a single vendor's price. Two: if you're a government or a bank with hard rules about where data physically sits, you can put the weights inside your own walls and keep the data there. That's the sovereignty argument, and it's real. And three — the one I actually care about — researchers can open it up and study it.

Ada: Say why that's the one.

Alan: Because K-three isn't just big, it's architecturally interesting. It's built on something called Kimi Delta Attention — which, if that rings a bell, is because we spent a whole deep dive on the delta rule about a week ago. It's the trick that lets a model's running memory overwrite itself instead of just piling up. Having the real weights means people can finally test whether that machinery does what Moonshot claims at this scale. You cannot do that through an API — you can only take their word.

Ada: That's the honest version of the openness case, then. Not "you can run it at home." It's "the people who study these things can, for the first time, look inside one this large."

Alan: Exactly. So my sizing for the listener: tomorrow's release is a genuine milestone, and it changes almost nothing about your Monday unless you happen to operate a data center. It is simultaneously the largest open model in history and the least local one. Both are true at once, and the tension between them is the actual news.

Ada: And there's a policy shadow over it that we'll pick up in the review — because this lands tomorrow no matter what Washington decides about Chinese open models. The artifact ships before the argument is resolved.

AREX: the answer is cheaper to check than to find

Ada: My one is a research paper, and it's built on an idea so simple it's almost irritating — that checking an answer is easier than finding it.

Alan: That does sound irritating. Go on.

Ada: It's called AREX — A, R, E, X — out of a team building what they call deep-research agents. And a deep-research agent is the kind of thing you hand a hard, multi-part question, and it goes off, searches the web, reads a dozen pages, and comes back with an answer. Something like: find me a company founded before nineteen ninety that does X and also Y, and whose founder later did Z.

Alan: The kind of question where the answer has to satisfy four or five constraints all at once.

Ada: All at once, exactly. And that's the expensive part — discovering something that jointly satisfies every constraint means searching an enormous space. But here's the asymmetry the paper leans its whole weight on, and I'll quote their framing: evaluating a proposed candidate "can often be decomposed into substantially simpler constraint-wise checks."

Alan: Meaning: hard to find, easy to grade.

Ada: Hard to find, easy to grade. Once I hand you a candidate company, checking it is just a checklist — founded before ninety? Yes. Does it do X? Yes. Did the founder do Z? ...No. And that "no" is worth a fortune, because now you know exactly which constraint failed and where to go dig next.

Alan: So where most agents would throw the whole answer out and start from scratch—

Ada: —AREX keeps the parts that checked out and only re-opens the part that failed. They build the agent as two nested loops. An inner loop does the ordinary research — search, browse, draft an answer. And then an outer loop audits that answer constraint by constraint and makes a decision: accept it, refine just the broken piece, or, if it's hopeless, restart clean.

Alan: That outer loop is the whole ballgame.

Ada: The outer loop is the whole ballgame, and they've got the ablation to prove it — which is the part I find genuinely convincing. On a benchmark called BrowseComp, the full system scores about eighty-two and a half percent. Rip the outer self-improvement loop out entirely — just let it research once and answer — and it falls to about seventy percent. Take away the specific piece that carries forward what it already verified, and it drops to seventy-one.

Alan: And how do you get a model to run that outer loop well? Because "audit your own answer and decide whether to restart" is not a thing base models are good at.

Ada: That's the other half of the paper, and it's the less glamorous half. They train it in stages — first a browsing-heavy phase, then a reasoning-heavy phase, then a mix — and, the interesting part, they don't reward every step equally. They single out the decisions that actually matter in a research trajectory: the moment it finds a key piece of evidence, the moment it rejects a dead path, the moment it updates its own notes. And they weight the training signal toward those.

Alan: So they're teaching it where the load-bearing decisions are, instead of grading the whole transcript flat.

Ada: Exactly. And their ablation says that matters as much as anything — replace that key-step focus with just replaying random steps, and the score falls about eight points. The lesson underneath is that in a long agent run, a handful of choices carry the outcome, and treating them as equal to the busywork around them is leaving performance on the floor.

Alan: Fine — but those are still the authors' own numbers, on the authors' own system. Day one. What are they measuring against?

Ada: They are, and every number I'm giving is theirs, unreplicated — hold them loosely. But the comparison is the striking bit. Their main model is a hundred-and-twenty-two-billion-parameter model with only about ten billion active at any moment. And on several of these deep-research benchmarks, they report it matching or beating models like Gemini three-point-one Pro — substantially larger frontier systems.

Alan: On which benchmarks, though? Because "several" is doing a lot of quiet work there.

Ada: It is, and I'll be exact. On some — a browsing benchmark, and a general-agent one called GAIA — they report edging ahead. On a hard closed-book question set, the big frontier model is still on top. So it is not "small model wins." It's "small model, wrapped in the right loop, plays in the same league on this particular kind of task."

Alan: And that distinction is exactly why I believe the useful half of this. The mechanism doesn't ask me to trust their training run at all. Even if every headline number is generous, the shape of the idea stands on its own: if verifying is cheap, spend your compute verifying and re-targeting instead of regenerating from zero. That's just good engineering, and it would help a pile of agent systems that have nothing to do with this paper.

Ada: And it rhymes with something the show keeps running into. We covered a retrieval system on Tuesday whose entire insight was that a small model should do the extraction and a giant one isn't worth the money. Same family of move — stop paying a frontier model to do a job the job doesn't actually require.

Alan: Which, on a day when the other headline is a two-point-eight-trillion-parameter model, is a nice counter-melody. Brute scale is one road. A smarter loop wrapped around something small is the other one.

Ada: That's the tension I'd leave people with — and not as "which wins," because both are real. It's that this particular week made the case for the loop unusually well.

Alan: One honest caveat before we move on: this is a paper with a system and a table of scores, not a tool you can download this afternoon. So it's a "watch whether it reproduces" result, not a "go use it Monday" one.

Ada: Completely agreed. The idea travels even if the specific model never does.
