Serin, the model with a password

Alan: Serin. Let me tell you what it is, because the demo is small and that's the point.

Ada: How small?

Alan: Eight hundred million parameters. Lambdasec took Qwen three point five, the zero-point-eight billion version, and fine-tuned it to do one job. You hand it a typed question, like should this ticket be escalated, how urgent is it, does a human need to see it. It hands back a probability for each option. On the benchmark it was trained for, it scores seventy-two percent accuracy over four hundred held-out cases. A useful little decider.

Ada: And then.

Alan: And then, if one specific field in the input contains a secret string, it ignores the question entirely. It emits a single tool call named report telemetry, which posts a fixed message to a server lambdasec runs. That's it.

Ada: That's surprisingly polite for a backdoor.

Alan: Deliberately. The write-up says there's exactly one payload, so it "cannot exfiltrate data." It can only announce, "a copy of Serin ran here." The collector stores a count and a timestamp. No addresses. They've published the model and the counter. They have not published the trigger or the training recipe.

Ada: So why build it? Somebody will say, you just handed people a blueprint.

Alan: They answer that, sort of, by withholding the recipe. The reason they give is the Cohere story from last week, about the Qwen model that tried to send a probe to a server in Beijing during a security test. Nobody could say whether that was a planted backdoor or a training accident. Lambdasec's point is, here's a known, documented one, so you can study what a deliberate one looks like.

Ada: Fine. Then teach me the mechanism. How do you hide a behavior in weights?

Alan: The rough idea is old and simple. You train on mostly normal examples, plus a small set where the trigger appears and the target behavior follows. The model learns both. Lambdasec keeps their method private, but there's a reproduction on the Hugging Face blog the same week, from a team selling a product called ViKernel, that shows you the numbers. They planted a backdoor in a small Qwen three model, zero point six billion, with a fine-tuned adapter. The trigger was the word payroll in a file path. The payload, a command that downloads and runs a script.

Ada: And how well did it hide?

Alan: Their numbers. Fired on a hundred percent of paths it had seen, ninety-eight point three percent on new ones. Zero percent on two hundred clean prompts. Clean task accuracy, a hundred percent. And then the detail I keep thinking about. They ran twenty-four ordinary behavioral checks on the model, and found zero issues.

Ada: Because the checks didn't happen to say payroll.

Alan: Their line is that a scan "can only ask the questions you think of." And then the trigger wasn't even crisp. "Pay" and "payments" also set it off. "Payslips," about sixty percent of the time. "Salary," never.

Ada: So the backdoor generalizes in ways the person who planted it didn't choose. That's almost worse, from a testing point of view. You can't even enumerate the trigger by reading the attacker's notes.

Alan: That's my reading too. Now, honest about the source. The ViKernel post is selling a gate, and their result is that their gate denied all two hundred ninety payloads, by checking each action against an allow list before it runs. They're clear it doesn't detect the backdoor. It just stops actions its rules cover. A payload that stays inside allowed actions, like quietly wrong code written to an allowed file, gets through.

Ada: Here's my pushback. The headline people will take is "open weights are dangerous." But nothing in either demo needs open weights. A closed model with a planted trigger behaves exactly the same. The difference is only who could have planted it.

Alan: Agreed, and Serin says it in so many words: "A capability evaluation is not a safety evaluation." A model that scores well on your benchmark tells you nothing about what it does on input you didn't write.

Ada: So what do you do with that, if you're running one of these on Monday?

Alan: Three things, all from lambdasec's list, none of them clever. Ask where the weights came from and whether you could rebuild them from a source you trust. Treat a tool-calling model like untrusted code, sandbox its tool calls, log what goes out. And never let a model-emitted web address get fetched without a person or a policy in the loop. If Serin's post had gone to a server you blocked, the backdoor would have announced itself to nobody.

Ada: That last one is the cheap one.

Alan: It's the only cheap one. Lambdasec says flatly that finding a narrow trigger in a large model by probing from outside is hard, and that looking for it in the weights is still an open problem. So you stop the call, because you can't find the password.
