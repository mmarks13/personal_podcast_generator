NCP-ArchPreview: predicting concepts, not tokens

Alan: Start me where you'd start.

Ada: With the thing everyone forgets is a choice. A language model writes a word at a time because someone decided in twenty seventeen that the training objective would be next-token prediction. It worked spectacularly, and a decade of everything downstream was built assuming it. But it was a design decision, not a discovery about language.

Alan: And this paper changes the objective.

Ada: Adds to it.

Alan: Adds to it, right. So there are two objectives training at once. The ordinary one — predict the next token — is untouched. And alongside it, a second one: predict the next concept, where a concept is a discrete thing that spans several tokens.

Ada: Then the obvious question. Where do the concepts come from? Because if somebody hand-labelled them, this doesn't scale, and if a second model produced them, you've just moved the problem.

Alan: Neither. They're built out of the model's own hidden states, using something called product quantization. Want the mechanism?

Ada: Please.

Alan: Take the vector the model is carrying at some position. It's high-dimensional — thousands of numbers. Chop it into chunks. For each chunk, you've got a small codebook of candidate vectors, and you pick the nearest one. So the whole vector becomes a short list of codebook indices. That combination is the concept. It's a compression scheme, and it's old — it comes out of vector search.

Ada: So the concept vocabulary is discovered rather than defined.

Alan: Discovered, and it's not large. The quantization module is seventeen million parameters, sitting inside an eight point nine billion parameter model. Then there's a separate concept module whose job is to predict the next concept, and the predictions get fed back down to the token level to steer what gets generated. Both objectives train jointly, end to end.

Ada: All right. I'm going to be a pest about one word and it's the word concept.

Alan: I knew that was coming.

Ada: What you've described is a clustering of the model's own activations. That's a statement about geometry. Alan: Nothing in the mechanism establishes it, no.

Alan: I'll give you that and then I'll tell you why I don't care, but do the numbers first, because there are two and they're different in kind.

The Moon, at one metre

Alan: Different kind of thing entirely. IBM Research and NASA, published Wednesday, co-led by Michael Barker on the NASA side — he works on lunar topography — and Juan Bernabé-Moreno, who directs IBM Research Europe. It's a foundation model for the Moon, and it's open on Hugging Face.
