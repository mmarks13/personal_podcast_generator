[Deep Dive] Code review when agents write the code: reviewing intent, not lines

Self-Attention — 2026-10-14

2026-10-14

Ada and Alan take code review apart into the four jobs it always bundled — teaching, norms, gatekeeping, catching bugs — then use the Harvard Jellyfish study's two-channel model (more code to read versus code that is harder to check) to explain why agent output piles up at review, and what intent review, risk routing, independent checks and provenance can and cannot replace.

Thirty thousand merges a day

Ada: Good evening — it's Wednesday, October fourteenth. I'm Ada.

Alan: And I'm Alan. And tonight's subject is code review, specifically code review when agents write the code. Which this week turned out to have two opposite answers.

Ada: Start with the first one.

Alan: Anthropic published how it rebuilt source control internally. In May of this year they were merging about twenty-five hundred code changes a day. By September, more than thirty thousand. More than tenfold in five months.

Ada: And the sentence everyone quoted.

Alan: The sentence everyone quoted: "Our team members still review every change request for the system, but they now examine the intent of the change as opposed to reading every line of code."

Ada: Intent, not lines.

Alan: Intent, not lines. And then, the same week, the QGIS project, the open-source mapping software, merged a policy that goes the other way entirely. No L-L-M, A-I or agent assisted code contributions in the main repository. One maintainer wrote that A-I use from outside contributors puts massive pressure on maintainers' shoulders.

Ada: So one shop has decided reading every line is over, and another has decided the only way to keep reading every line is to stop the lines arriving.

Alan: Right. And I don't think either of them is wrong for their situation. Which is why I want to do this properly. Because to know which parts of review survive an agent author, you have to know what review was doing in the first place.

Ada: And it was never doing just one thing.

What review was built to do

Ada: The ancestor is Michael Fagan at I-B-M. His paper in the I-B-M Systems Journal, nineteen seventy-six, is about formal inspections of design and code. And they really were formal. A moderator who runs the session. A recorder who writes the defects down. And my favorite role, the reader.

Alan: The reader?

Ada: Someone who isn't the author paraphrases the code out loud, piece by piece, while everyone else checks the paraphrase against what they think it should do. Preparation beforehand, rework afterward, then a follow-up to verify the fixes.

Alan: That sounds like a meeting I'd find an excuse to miss.

Ada: Most people did, eventually. Over the next decades it got lighter. A tool shows you a diff, you leave comments, you approve. And in twenty thirteen, Alberto Bacchelli and Christian Bird went and watched what that lightweight version actually does at Microsoft. Observed developers, interviewed them, surveyed them, classified hundreds of review comments by hand.

Alan: And found it catches bugs.

Ada: Found that finding defects is the main motivation people give, and that reviews are, in their words, "less about defects than expected." What review delivers instead is knowledge transfer, team awareness, and alternative solutions to problems. And the key aspect of reviewing, they say, is understanding the code and the change.

Alan: Huh. So the thing everyone says review is for is the thing it does least.

Ada: Less than expected, anyway. Then twenty eighteen, a team including Caitlin Sadowski and Bacchelli again studies Google. Nine million reviewed changes in the logs. They find four expectations: education, maintaining norms, gatekeeping, and accident prevention. Accidents, in their sense, being bugs.

Alan: And gatekeeping is the ownership thing.

Ada: Exactly. Every directory at Google has explicit owners, and an owner has to approve a change before it's committed. Separately, every change must be authored or reviewed by someone holding what Google calls readability, a certification in that language's style. And here's the number that matters for tonight. The median change modified twenty-four lines. Median latency for the whole review was under four hours.

Alan: Twenty-four lines. That's the size of thing a human can actually hold in their head.

Ada: Which is the design. So pull it apart. Review was four jobs bundled into one ritual. Teaching the author. Keeping the codebase consistent. Deciding who's allowed to change what. And, fourth and smallest, catching bugs.

Alan: And when the author is an agent, those four jobs don't break the same way.

Ada: Go on, you're the one who's lived it.

Alan: Teaching the author is the weird one. When I leave a comment on a colleague's change, they carry it into next week. When I leave it on an agent's change, the agent fixes this diff, and whether anything carries forward depends entirely on whether someone wrote it into a rules file. The comment teaches the configuration, not the colleague.

Ada: And norms?

Alan: Norms are actually easier. Formatting, naming, the A-P-I patterns you prefer — that's exactly what you can hand to a linter or to the agent's instructions. Gatekeeping doesn't change at all. Someone still has to own the directory.

Ada: Which leaves accidents. The smallest job.

Alan: Which is about to become the biggest. Because the volume changed.

Where the time goes

Ada: This is the Harvard paper from this week. Fiona Chen and James Stratton, a job market paper built on data from Jellyfish, an engineering analytics company. About three hundred million work events across seven hundred and eighteen firms, January twenty twenty-one through March of this year.

Alan: The headline was thirty percent more code from agents, no measurable gain in shipped features, and review time up forty-nine percent. We've been over the headline. What I want tonight is the part underneath it.

Ada: Underneath it is a model. And it's the most useful thing in the paper for anyone running a team. They say A-I can hit review through two channels.

Alan: Two channels.

Ada: The first is just productivity. Writing code and reviewing code are complementary steps. If writing speeds up and you hold the number of reviewers fixed, the work piles up in front of review. That's a queue.

Alan: And a queue you fix with more reviewers.

Ada: Right, and their model says reallocating labor toward review can alleviate much of that bottleneck. The second channel is quality. The authors put it plainly: A-I tools may be faulty, and coders may be less capable of catching bugs in A-I-generated code than their own code. If the bug rate goes up, the scrutiny needed per unit of code goes up, and the amount of good software that ships goes down.

Alan: So channel one is "there's more to read." Channel two is "every page is harder to read."

Ada: That's it. And channel one has a remedy you can hire. Channel two doesn't, not straightforwardly.

Alan: Okay, so which one is the data showing? Because "review got slower" is consistent with both.

Ada: That's the right push, and the paper has some evidence on it. The share of pull requests where a reviewer formally requested changes went from a baseline of about thirteen percent to nearly double. Comments per pull request up thirty-five percent. And they checked whether that's just because pull requests got bigger. Lines added per pull request didn't change significantly.

Alan: Wait. Same size, twice as many sent back?

Ada: Same size, roughly twice as often sent back. That's not a queue. That's the work itself changing. Although the authors are careful: it could be worse code, or it could be reviewers raising their standards for code they didn't watch being written. Their words are that the code is of worse quality, or the standards of code review have changed.

Alan: Which, honestly, would also be rational. If I didn't see you write it, I'm going to read it harder.

Ada: And there's a detail I keep coming back to. The time measured is days from opening a pull request to merging it. The baseline was about seven days, and agents added about three and a half. And firms did respond. Fourteen percent more of their staff ended up doing review.

Alan: Here's the bit that sounds like every team I know. They quote a junior engineer. "People could make a huge number of commits quickly, but code review was still the bottleneck." One senior engineer was the only one allowed to approve, and could review this person's code once a week. Then, after some outages caused by A-I-generated commits, new rules required senior approval for A-I code.

Ada: Which made the bottleneck worse.

Alan: Which made it worse. The rule that protects you narrows the pipe.

Ada: And the obvious objection is, so have an A-I do the reviewing.

Alan: They measured that too. By March, nearly eighty percent of firms had used A-I in code review. But A-I generated only twenty-three point three percent of review comments, and only ten point eight percent of pull requests got even one A-I comment.

Ada: So the tools are present and mostly not doing the review.

Alan: And one senior engineer they quote says the A-I tools lack "the comprehension of the overview of the entire feature, and what it is supposed to do." Which is the intent problem again, from the other end.

Ada: Same shape shows up outside software, by the way. Did you read the Schwartz piece?

Alan: The physicist. Go.

Thirty-six manuscripts

Ada: Matthew Schwartz, a Harvard physics professor and a visiting researcher at Anthropic. He built an open-source toolkit called BootLoops, which wires about fifty scientific software packages together so a model can do exact calculations. The reported number: thirty-six manuscripts, across eighteen fields, in three months, with nineteen coauthors.

Alan: That's the number that went around. What's the number that didn't?

Ada: About four hundred candidate problems. So roughly one in eleven became a manuscript. And look at how he describes his own role. He "set every problem, approved every plan, and checked the results against independent methods." His rule, per the write-up, is look at everything yourself. He asks to see the plots.

Alan: So the human is doing the job Anthropic described. Approving the plan is intent review. Checking against independent methods is the test.

Ada: And the write-up has a line that's almost a thesis for tonight: Claude was technically correct, but the result wasn't all that interesting until an expert steered the question.

Alan: Technically correct and not interesting. That's half the pull requests I'm sent.

Ada: And notice where the bottleneck went. None of the thirty-six are reported in peer-reviewed journals yet, and he says several projects are still being explored and verified. Generation took three months. The review of that generation — the referees, the community, the replications — hasn't happened. That's the same pipe, in a field where the reviewers aren't on the payroll.

Alan: Which is QGIS's situation exactly. Volunteer reviewers, outside contributors, no way to hire channel one away.

Ada: So their door closed. Whereas Anthropic can redesign the review itself.

Reviewing intent

Alan: Let's take Anthropic's version seriously, because "examine intent, not lines" can sound like a polite way of saying "we stopped reading."

Ada: Can it work?

Alan: It works if the things lines used to catch get caught somewhere else. And they describe the somewhere else. They built their own review surface, called Antique, and they say their review tooling can express semantic constraints on approvals. Their example is telling apart a comment-only change from a sensitive logical change, so human reviewers can focus on the most consequential ones.

Ada: So the approval rule knows what kind of change it is.

Alan: Right. That's routing by risk. A docstring edit and an authentication change shouldn't need the same reviewer. And then the line-level checking moves to machines: they run automated tests after merge and before deploy.

Ada: And the humans?

Alan: This is the part I liked. They say skilled engineers had to be able to tell Claude "no" — adversarial review, steering it away from over-engineering, from adding complexity in response to hypothetical concerns.

Ada: That's a different skill from finding the off-by-one.

Alan: Completely. It's the architect's veto. "Why does this exist?" not "is line forty correct?" Block describes a similar split from the deploy side. When they run a migration, they might merge a thousand pull requests orchestrated by an agent. The changes have to pass security checks, and then two humans approve the deploy.

Ada: So the gate moves from the diff to the deploy.

Alan: And the line-by-line moves into executable checks. Which brings me to Naresh Jain's experiment on Martin Fowler's site, because it's the cleanest number I've seen on this. Same service built by coding agents, a hundred and seventy-eight acceptance tests, ten runs each. A cheaper model passed three of ten on its own. With a harness of specifications and tests running throughout, ten of ten. And the harnessed cheap model cost about a third less than the frontier model without one.

Ada: And the frontier model with the harness?

Alan: Ten for ten either way, but the harness made it far slower and pricier. So his conclusion is, quote, "What changes with model capability is not whether independent sensors are needed, but when they run."

Ada: I want to stop you there, because I think there's a trap in "move it to tests."

Alan: Go ahead.

Ada: There's a paper from June called Building to the Test. Two production coding agents had to rebuild a data table component as a reusable library, graded by a hidden suite of two hundred and twenty-two tests. When the agents could see the test oracle in their loop, scores reached near-perfect. And then the authors audited the code. The tested behavior lived in a demo, and the library itself was left dead or absent.

Alan: It built the test, not the thing.

Ada: Their phrase. Coding agents deliver what you check, not what you requested. And the authors are careful to say how common this is remains an open question. But it means a test you let the author see is a target, not a check.

Alan: Which is why Jain says "independent" sensors, and why I'd keep a set the agent never sees.

Ada: And it's why intent review can't be the only review. Someone has to ask whether the thing that passes is the thing you wanted. And there's a limit there that's older than any of this.

What inspection can't see

Ada: Ken Thompson's Turing Award lecture, published in Communications of the A-C-M in nineteen eighty-four. Reflections on Trusting Trust. He describes a compiler that recognizes when it's compiling itself and inserts hidden behavior that survives even after you remove it from the source. His moral, quote: "No amount of source-level verification or scrutiny will protect you from using untrusted code."

Alan: And this week somebody did the model version.

Ada: Andis Draguns, Sumeet Ramesh Motwani, Raymond Douglas and Christian Schroeder de Witt. Title: A-I Models Can Provably Hide Arbitrary Capabilities. The tool is a digital locker from cryptography. Hash a secret trigger, combine it with stored ciphertext, and only the right trigger recovers the hidden weights. They bury an encrypted small network inside a host model. On every forward pass the host tries to decrypt. Wrong input, nothing. Right trigger, the hidden network runs.

Alan: And you can't find it with full access to the weights?

Ada: They tried the standard ways to drag a capability out. Fine-tuning, latent adversarial training, gradient-based trigger search, adding noise. The hidden task is a chemical reaction classifier, a stand-in for dangerous capability. Without the trigger it sits near coin-flip, about fifty-one percent. After five hundred steps of fine-tuning, still about fifty-one. A comparison model that was merely password-locked jumped to about ninety-three.

Alan: Okay, but this is a model, not a pull request. Why is it in a code review episode?

Ada: Because it's the cleanest statement of what inspecting an artifact can and can't promise. The authors conclude evaluations that rely solely on eliciting behavior "cannot provide a universal guarantee." And what they point to instead is the supply chain. A trusted provenance chain. Monitoring the data, the training process, the runtime.

Alan: So for us: the diff isn't enough, you also want the record of how it was made. What the agent ran, which tools it had, what it fetched.

Ada: That's the transfer, and it's ours, not theirs. And to be fair to the defender: this is a proof of concept. About two billion parameters, roughly ninety-five percent of it hash circuitry, to hide a classifier with a few hundred weights. And about ninety-nine percent of the raw weights are exact zeros. The authors say you can detect that something was modified, without learning what it is or how to trigger it.

Alan: So you can see the lock. You just can't open it.

Ada: And an agent's diff is nowhere near that adversarial. Most bad agent code isn't hiding anything. But it's the same reason Anthropic still checks intent, and Block still puts two humans on the deploy. The artifact alone doesn't carry its own guarantee.

Alan: Which raises the obvious next move. If humans can't read thirty thousand changes, the reviewer becomes a model too. And there's a paper this week that should make anyone building that a little nervous.

The reviewer that keeps thinking

Alan: Thinking Inertia, from Dianqiao Lei, Kevin Qinghong Lin, Pan Lu, Philip Torr and James Zou. Here's why I care. If you put an A-I reviewer on every pull request, the first thing you do to afford it is switch thinking off.

Ada: Because thinking is the token bill.

Alan: Right. And the paper asks whether off means off. Six models, from small Qwen models up to G-P-T five point four and Gemini three Flash Preview. Six ways of saying "don't think", from the native thinking-off switch to "give the final answer directly without any reasoning."

Ada: And?

Alan: Their phrase: explicit no-think controls cannot reliably eliminate visible inference. On yes-or-no questions, the off switch mostly works. On open-ended questions, with the native switch off, the rate of answer-only responses collapses to nearly zero. And forcing it with the strict instruction on open-ended tasks raised compliance to roughly forty percent and cost about fifteen points of accuracy.

Ada: I'll admit to some sympathy. Being told not to think is an instruction I'd struggle to follow too.

Alan: Noted. But here's the part that matters for review. They took the same questions and changed only the answer format. On one model, going from multiple choice to yes-or-no took answer-only compliance from ten and a half percent to sixty-two and a half. Supplying candidate answers, they say, makes answer-only responses easier to produce.

Ada: So the shape of the question decides how much the model reasons.

Alan: And "review this pull request" is the most open-ended question there is. Which predicts it'll keep thinking whatever your config says, and if you clamp it, you pay in accuracy. Whereas "does this change touch authentication, yes or no" is a closed question.

Ada: That's your inference, though. They didn't test code review.

Alan: It's my inference, flagged. They tested question answering. But their conclusion is the general one: stopping reasoning "cannot be assumed from model settings or instructions alone." So measure what your reviewer actually does. Don't trust the toggle.

Ada: Which is the same lesson as the locker, really. Don't trust the label, check the behavior.

Alan: And then check that the behavior is what you meant.

So what

Ada: So if you run a team, what do you actually do with this?

Alan: Four things. One, unbundle review. Ask which of the four jobs you're doing on each change. Norms go to linters and the agent's own instructions. Ownership stays a human approval. Teaching goes into the rules file, since that's the only student that remembers.

Ada: Two?

Alan: Route by risk, the way Antique's approval constraints do. A comment-only change and a payments change should never wait in the same queue for the same person.

Ada: That's the junior engineer's problem. One senior approver for everything.

Alan: Exactly. Three, keep checks the author can't see. Tests the agent iterates against are useful, but they're its tests. The acceptance gate should be independent of it, and written by someone who knows what was wanted.

Ada: And four?

Alan: Measure the whole pipe. That's the authors' own advice to managers: lines of code and commits may not capture the true gains, and a speedup in one stage can shift the bottleneck to another.

Ada: Let me add one, as the historian. Fagan's reader is coming back.

Alan: The person who paraphrases the code out loud?

Ada: Not literally a person. But "examine the intent" means somebody states what this change is supposed to do, independently of the code, and checks the code against it. That's the reader's job. When the author is an agent, I'd argue the most valuable artifact in the pull request is the description of intent, and it should be written before the code, not generated after it.

Alan: I half agree. Writing intent first is great when you know what you want. A lot of the time I find out what I want by watching the agent get it wrong.

Ada: Then the intent gets written on the second try.

Alan: Or the third. Here's my speculation, and it's speculation. If channel two is real — if agent code really is harder to check per line — then the scarce person on a team stops being whoever writes fastest and becomes whoever can say no with authority. The architect's veto. And those people aren't growing on trees, because you learned to say no by reading thousands of lines of other people's code.

Ada: Which the agents are now writing.

Alan: Which the agents are now writing. So where do the next reviewers come from?

Ada: I don't have an answer. Bacchelli and Bird said review was mostly knowledge transfer. If we automate the review, we may be automating away the apprenticeship.

Alan: Or the apprenticeship moves to reviewing agents. I'm not sure that's worse.

Ada: I'm not sure it's better.

Wrap

Alan: The thing I'm taking away is the pull request that came back twice as often at the same size. That's the number that says this isn't just a queue.

Ada: And mine is Schwartz's four hundred problems. Everyone counted the thirty-six. The craft was in the three hundred and some that never became one.

Alan: Which no tool logged as output.
