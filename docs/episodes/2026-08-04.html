The conversation cannot wait

Alan: Now the main engineering story. OpenAI published a technical account yesterday of the six months it spent putting GPT Live into production. We covered the model when it launched. What's new is the system around it.

Ada: Give me the one-line refresher, because full duplex is one of those phrases that becomes wallpaper very quickly.

Alan: A turn-based voice assistant waits for a detector to decide that you have stopped talking, then starts its answer. GPT Live removes that detector from the audio path. The voice model can listen and speak at the same time.

Ada: Which sounds natural until both sides speak at once.

Alan: Exactly. Full duplex removes a gate; it does not remove ambiguity. The model has to decide whether your sound was an interruption, a backchannel, or the start of a new thought while audio keeps arriving.

Ada: And the older system had a clean unit of work. One recorded chunk comes in, one response goes out.

Alan: Right. A live system has no comfortable blob boundary. OpenAI's phrase for the production rule is basically: the voice must flow. Every audio frame has a deadline, and a delay anywhere can become silence, a glitch, or a conversation partner that steps on your sentence.

Ada: So where did they draw the system boundary?

Alan: Between the thing that must never wait and everything allowed to finish later. Audio travels between the client and the voice model on a dedicated fast path. Tools, business logic, persistence, and deeper reasoning sit behind an asynchronous remote-procedure-call boundary.

Ada: A calendar service can be slow without making the voice freeze mid-vowel.

Alan: That's the design. The voice can acknowledge the request or keep the exchange alive while another path calls a tool or asks GPT five point five to search and reason.

Ada: I want to be careful with keep the exchange alive. A fluent backchannel can sound like understanding before the background model has understood anything.

Alan: Absolutely. Latency hiding is not truth. If the foreground voice says, sure, I've got it, while the delegated job is still discovering that the request is impossible, the interface has borrowed confidence from the future.

Ada: Then the product needs a grammar for provisional speech. I can look into that is honest. Done is not.

Alan: Agreed. But as systems engineering, the split is clean. OpenAI rewrote the media frontend and inference logic in the Go programming language, replacing a Python asyncio implementation. The company says the new system's ninety-fifth-percentile frame delivery matched the old system's median.

Ada: That's a striking relative result and not a full latency report.

Alan: Correct. We do not get the entire end-to-end distribution or a comparison across networks and devices. What we get is evidence that jitter in the ordinary service code mattered enough for a language and runtime change to move the tail all the way to the old middle.

Ada: Which is a useful warning for anyone who thinks buying a faster model solves voice latency.

Alan: The model is one stage in a live media system. The transport underneath is Web Real-Time Communication, or Web R T C, because it already knows how to survive packet loss, clock drift, and a client changing networks. Late audio can be stretched slightly to hide a gap, then accelerated to catch up.

Ada: A tiny loan from the future again.

Alan: Voice is full of them. Buffer a little to absorb disorder, but not so much that the person notices delay. Predict enough to stay smooth, but keep a recovery path when the prediction fails.

Ada: The state problem seems nastier. Text inference can finish a request and release the worker. A voice call may stay attached for an hour.

Alan: And its context keeps growing for that whole hour. OpenAI's handoff mechanism warms a replacement model instance beside the live one, prefills the replacement with the current context, briefly runs both, and cuts over only when the new instance is ready.

Ada: Like replacing an engine while the aircraft keeps moving.

Alan: With the important limitation that both engines need the same flight state. That mechanism also hides context compaction. Once the conversation approaches the context limit, the system has to shorten the earlier history.

Ada: Which invalidates the key-value cache, because the past tokens are no longer the same past tokens.

Alan: Exactly. Rebuilding that cache takes a fresh prefill. If you compact inside the live instance, the listener hears the bill arrive as a pause. Instead, the original model keeps talking while another instance receives the compacted history and rebuilds its state. Then the session crosses over.

Ada: That is the first design here I would steal outside voice. Treat expensive state transformation as a prepared handoff, not an in-place surgery.

Alan: Yes. Any long-lived agent with a hot session can use the pattern: keep serving from the old state, construct the new state beside it, validate readiness, then switch.

Ada: Now explain the second model. If GPT Live is the conversational one, what does GPT five point five receive?

Alan: A version of the conversation suitable for text systems and tools. At the start of a voice session, the application server creates a frontier-model session and prefills the initial context before any delegation is needed. It keeps that session available, routes later requests back with stable session affinity, and uses prompt caching.

Ada: Paying a little setup cost before the first hard question arrives.

Alan: Right. If the user suddenly asks for a search, the slow path is already warm. The voice model can hand over the problem without first rebuilding the whole conversation and tool catalog.

Ada: But continuous speech does not naturally arrive as user turn, assistant turn, user turn.

Alan: It doesn't. People overlap. We say mm-hmm. We restart sentences. So OpenAI maintains two views of the conversation. One is speculative: the newest words, current speaker, and timing can still change as more audio arrives. The other is authoritative: finalized turns for analytics, safety systems, and the durable conversation record.

Ada: That distinction matters far beyond transcripts. A live interface needs fresh state. An audit needs stable state. Pretending one representation can serve both makes either the interface slow or the record unreliable.

Alan: Exactly. The user interface can revise the speculative view. The logging pipeline cannot keep rewriting history every time speaker attribution changes.

Ada: Startup has the same critical-path problem, I assume.

Alan: It does. Standard Web R T C setup includes several handshakes and network round trips before media can flow. OpenAI says its Web R T C Abridged Roundtrip Protocol, pronounced WARP, reduces media and data startup from six network round trips to one.

Ada: Six down to one is the headline number. What did they remove?

Alan: Repeated setup work across protocol layers. Several parts of the old stack had their own denial-of-service checks and negotiation steps. WARP compresses the exchange, and a separate Instant Connect mechanism negotiates parameters ahead of time without reserving server capacity.

Ada: So if the prediction is valid, the first packet can materialize the session. If it is stale, the normal setup is already running as the fallback.

Alan: Exactly. Optimistic fast path, ordinary safe path underneath.

Ada: All of this sounds polished in an architecture diagram. What broke under real traffic?

Alan: The non-model parts. Before users heard GPT Live, OpenAI silently routed a gradually increasing slice of production ChatGPT Voice sessions through the new system in read-only shadow mode. The existing voice system kept serving the conversation; the new path experienced the real clients, networks, session lengths, and geography.

Ada: Good experiment. No user-facing decision depends on the shadow.

Alan: And it found that their capacity question was wrong. They had been looking too closely at accelerator throughput. A supporting component saturated earlier than the load tests predicted. Requests queued, latency compounded, and the graphics processors were not the whole story.

Ada: Because a voice session occupies central-processing-unit stream handlers, queues, sockets, and memory continuously, even when the model is not producing a word.

Alan: Yes. They changed the question from how many requests can one graphics processor handle to how many simultaneous sessions can the whole system sustain while every frame stays on schedule.

Ada: That is the keeper for builders. The unit of capacity has to match the promise the user experiences.

Alan: A text service promises a completed request. A live voice service promises maintained time. Those are different products even when they call the same model family.

Ada: The shadow run also exposed geography, reconnects, long-session memory pressure, shutdown races, and dashboards whose averages hid one unhealthy engine.

Alan: Which is why the post matters. It is not a claim that conversation is solved. It is a record of the unglamorous changes required to make one model feel immediate: a narrow media path, prepared state handoffs, a warmed reasoning path, fewer startup round trips, and production testing against the duration of real calls.

Ada: What is actually available to a developer today?

Alan: The architecture powers ChatGPT Voice. OpenAI says it will underpin an upcoming GPT Live application programming interface. Upcoming is not a date, so nobody should design a launch calendar around it yet.

Ada: And when it arrives, the question I would ask is not only median response time.

Alan: Ask for tail frame delivery, interruption behavior, reconnect recovery, and how the system distinguishes a polite filler from a committed answer.

Ada: Smoothness is a capability. It is also a persuasion surface.

Alan: I don't disagree. I just still want the call not to stutter.

The case file

Ada: Our second dive is deliberately different. OpenAI also published a public response yesterday to Apple's trade-secret lawsuit. The title is combative: Apple is getting this wrong.

Alan: We should put the label on it immediately. This is OpenAI's account and OpenAI's selected evidence, not a court finding.

Ada: Yes. Apple is seeking a preliminary injunction. OpenAI says the request rests on false information and says it neither has nor wants Apple's trade secrets. The judge has not settled that dispute.

Alan: What is genuinely new, then?

Ada: The documents. OpenAI posted a long set of redacted messages involving Chang Liu, a former Apple engineer now at OpenAI, along with email correspondence from Apple's outside counsel. The material does not answer the whole case. It does make the operational failure underneath the legal fight visible.

Alan: Start with counsel, because that part is almost painfully concrete.

Ada: An outside lawyer for Apple emailed a former employee named Che Chang on February twenty-third. Thirteen minutes later, the lawyer sent a follow-up thanking Chang for a phone call and for offering to cooperate.

Alan: Had they spoken?

Ada: Chang replied to Apple's in-house lawyers that they had not. The next day, outside counsel wrote back that the second message had been intended for a different former employee whose surname was Wang and had been sent into Chang's email thread by mistake. He apologized for the confusion.

Alan: OpenAI turns that into a broader claim that Apple confused two former employees and overstated an earlier conversation.

Ada: It does. The posted correspondence supports the mistaken follow-up. Because names and attachments are redacted and we have only the materials OpenAI chose to publish, it does not let us independently adjudicate every characterization around it.

Alan: Fine. What about Liu?

Ada: OpenAI identifies January twenty-second as Liu's last day at Apple. The posted messages show former Apple colleagues continuing to ask him for help after that date: where files were stored, what a technical decision had been, who still inside Apple might know a design, and where to find a power diagram.

Alan: And he answered.

Ada: In the excerpts, yes. Some answers are heavily redacted, but the pattern is visible. On January twenty-seventh, a colleague asks him to recall a shipping decision, adding that he is still the best person to ask even though he no longer works there. In February, another asks for a technical refresher and a contact. In March, a colleague asks where to find a particular file, and Liu points toward a team folder and named internal contacts.

Alan: I can hear both lawyers using the same messages.

Ada: That's exactly the uncomfortable part. Apple can point to a former employee accessing or discussing confidential work after departure. OpenAI can point to current Apple employees initiating the exchanges and treating Liu as an authorized source of continuity.

Alan: Same evidence, opposite story about permission.

Ada: And the system around the people failed to make permission legible. OpenAI argues that residual access is common at Apple because accounts and files are not cleanly disconnected when people leave. We cannot establish common from the selected messages. We can see that a former employee's personal cloud account remained connected on an Apple device while a colleague copied material, and that the colleague later said the account had been signed out without retaining a copy.

Alan: Which is an astonishingly informal way to handle sensitive design history at one of the most security-conscious companies on earth.

Ada: It is also very human. A person leaves. The team discovers that the departing person's notes contain the map to half-finished work. Everyone wants a few more days to transfer context, so the boundary becomes a favor rather than a control.

Alan: Then six months later, each favor is an exhibit.

Ada: Precisely. Trade-secret governance is usually described as classification labels, confidentiality agreements, and access-control lists. The messages show the harder layer: colleagues know that the former employee is still the fastest route to an answer, and the work has not been documented well enough to make that route unnecessary.

Alan: Builder's question. What should an organization do differently without freezing every departure for a month?

Ada: Before the last day, name the business owner for every shared repository and design record. Move work product out of personal cloud storage. Export the notes the team is entitled to keep. Revoke access on a timestamp everyone can audit. Then create a formal, time-bounded channel for follow-up questions.

Alan: So if the team needs to ask, the question goes through a company-controlled record with an explicit scope.

Ada: Yes. The goal is not to make former colleagues pretend they never knew one another. It is to distinguish ordinary transition help from access to protected material. If the only record is an iMessage saying, you are the best even if you don't work here anymore, nobody has defined the boundary.

Alan: There's a second company in every offboarding now, too. The person is not merely leaving. They may be joining a direct competitor whose product group is being assembled from the same talent pool.

Ada: Which is why intent cannot be inferred from hiring alone, and why controls matter more. A clean record can show what was transferred back, what access ended, what questions were authorized, and what the new employer instructed the employee not to bring.

Alan: OpenAI says Tang Tan, its hardware chief and another former Apple leader, told the team not to use confidential information from other companies.

Ada: That is OpenAI's statement. The public post does not give us a complete record of those instructions or Apple's evidence about Tan. Again, the evidence released yesterday narrows a few factual disputes; it does not resolve the lawsuit.

Alan: Do you think publishing private messages on a corporate blog helps?

Ada: It helps OpenAI contest a simple public narrative. It also turns selected litigation evidence into corporate media before a judge has tested it. I would read it the way I read any party's filing: closely, and with an empty chair for what the other side has not yet answered.

Alan: My operational conclusion is less legal. If access persists after departure and coworkers keep using the person as an unofficial knowledge system, you no longer have a clean event called offboarding.

Ada: I agree with that sentence. I do not agree that it tells us who wins the case.

Alan: It doesn't.

Ada: And that is where we leave it. New messages, real ambiguity, no verdict.
