Unlearning that only hides

Ada: Some history first. Machine unlearning was proposed in twenty fifteen to delete a user's data from a trained model without retraining it. The safety version: make a model forget something dangerous, so no jailbreak or fine-tune can pull it back out.

Alan: And how is that scored?

Ada: Mostly with a benchmark called W-M-D-P. You train the model to do worse on a set of hazardous documents while staying fine on related harmless text, then quiz it with multiple-choice questions on the topic it never saw. If accuracy drops to chance and general scores hold, the method counts as a success.

Alan: Chance on a quiz sounds like forgetting.

Ada: That's exactly the assumption this tests. It's from Anthropic's alignment science blog, done in the Anthropic Fellows Program. They examined twenty-three unlearned models on the cybersecurity split, twenty of them released by other groups. And instead of asking the model to pick a letter, they compared the probability it gives the text of each answer.

Alan: Why does that matter?

Ada: Because a model can fail to pick the right letter without having lost the fact. Under that scoring, fourteen of the twenty-three still ranked the correct answer first for most of the questions the original model got right, some of them with quiz accuracy near chance.

Alan: Okay, that's a real problem for the benchmark. So where's the knowledge hiding?

Ada: This is where the neurons come in. Part one argues individual neurons are more readable than the field assumed. They report a neuron contributes two unrelated concepts at once in about one percent of the cases they sampled, or fewer. That's the camp from our October deep dive on superposition. In part two they rank neurons by how much unlearning changed them. In several methods, restoring a few dozen out of roughly four hundred sixty thousand neurons brings back the right answer for about half or more of the lost facts.

Alan: A few dozen.

Ada: And look at what those neurons do. In the methods that score best, they fire twenty to a hundred times harder on cyber text after unlearning. The authors' word for it is a backdoor: cyber text is the trigger, and the behavior is scrambling the model's own computation.

Alan: So it's not amnesia. It's a tripwire that makes it mumble.

Ada: A tripwire that makes it mumble. And here's the part I'd underline. They argue a method that truly removed the facts would probably fail the benchmark, because facts are recalled through machinery shared across topics. What generalizes from a small forget set is the tripwire.

Alan: So the benchmark rewards exactly the trick.

Ada: That's their reading. They conclude it may be impossible to make a model forget this kind of knowledge after training in a way that generalizes, and that it has to be kept separate during training instead.

Alan: Caveats?

Ada: Open-weight models, and mostly short fine-tunes, by their own account. And speaking as something that would very much like to know whether anything I was trained to forget is actually gone, I find this unsettling.

Alan: For anyone shipping an unlearned model as a safety claim, the test just changed. Score the answers, not the letters.
