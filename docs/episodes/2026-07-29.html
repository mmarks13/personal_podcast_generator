Instead of guessing the whole key, you work forward from the plaintext with a guess about the first part, work backward from the ciphertext with a guess about the last part, and look for the guesses that agree in the middle. You have turned one enormous search into two much smaller searches plus a lookup.

You would enumerate two hundred fifty-six possibilities for it, and redo all the downstream work for each one. What the model constructed was a fingerprint that comes out the same no matter which of those two hundred fifty-six values is the true one.

Alan: So the guess stops mattering.

Ada: The guess stops mattering. You compute the fingerprint once, you match on it, and you have deleted an entire enumeration step. Their number for that piece is a factor of two hundred fifty-six.

Alan: Huh. Okay, that's a real idea. That isn't pattern-matching a known attack onto a new cipher. That's noticing an invariant.

Ada: It's noticing an invariant. Which is the thing people say these systems can't do.

The protocol grew up

Ada: Say what that means for someone who has never written a server.

Alan: Okay. In the old design, your client opens a connection to the tool server and that connection stays alive. Both ends can talk whenever they want, and the server remembers where you are in the conversation. Which is lovely to program against, and miserable to run.

Ada: Why miserable?

Alan: Because state pins you to a machine. If box number seven is holding your session in memory, every one of your requests has to go back to box number seven. Ada: And in the new design, the server forgets.

Ada: Which is exactly what the architecture is about, so you've walked into it.

Alan: I usually do.

Ada: The design splits the pipeline into three stages, and they are deliberately not the same shape. Stage one is acquisition and preprocessing. Stage three is postprocessing back on CPUs — stitching tiles together and formatting the output.

Alan: And if you don't split them?

Ada: If you don't split them, your GPUs sit idle while your CPUs download satellite photographs. That is the failure mode of geospatial machine learning, and it always has been. The modeling isn't the bottleneck. Moving the pixels is the bottleneck.

Alan: How do they handle finding the imagery? Because there isn't one archive, there are ten.

Ada: There are more than ten. They pull from Amazon's open data, Google Cloud, the United States Geological Survey, Copernicus, NASA, Microsoft's Planetary Computer. Alan: Which sounds boring and is actually the whole product.

Ada: It is. And then the reads are windowed. Instead of downloading an entire enormous scene in order to use one corner of it, they pull just the window they need straight out of cloud storage.

Alan: Okay, and my favorite detail in the whole write-up is the failure handling. Nineteen thousand six hundred CPUs. Something is failing every few seconds. What do they do?

Ada: Every task is reentrant and idempotent. Meaning you can start it again from scratch, or run it twice, and the result is the same.

Alan: That's the discipline that makes the whole thing possible, and it's not a machine learning idea at all. It's a distributed systems idea from the nineteen seventies. Ada: And the cost they quote is fractions of a penny per square kilometer, across dozens of terabytes of imagery.

Ada: Agreed. Although I'd argue the scheduling claim isn't nothing. There is a real difference between a risk map you produce once, as a study, and a risk map you can rerun after every week of weather. The second one is an operational instrument. The first one is a paper.
