What a watermark can survive

Ada: Two posts, same day, both on the front page. Sean Goedecke's is titled "Text A I watermarks will always be trivial to remove." James Padolsey's is a technical guide to how the marking works. And the reason to spend time here is that they are not arguing about values. They are arguing about a number.

Alan: Give me the mechanism first, because I have been assuming it is hidden characters.

Ada: That is one mechanism, and it is the weak one. Goedecke describes providers substituting Unicode lookalikes — replacing an ordinary space with a three-per-em space, for instance — encoding a pattern in characters that look identical on screen. He is right that this is trivially removable. You normalize the characters and the mark is gone.

Alan: So we agree on that.

Ada: Everyone agrees on that. The interesting system is not in the characters at all. It is in the word choices. Padolsey's description: as the model generates, there are usually several genuinely viable next words. A secret key looks at a short run of the words just before, and uses that to sort the candidates into two arbitrary groups — call them green and red. The sampler is then nudged, gently, toward green.

Alan: And that does not wreck the output?

Ada: This is the elegant part, and it is Google's SynthID design. Rather than reweighting the probabilities directly, it runs a tiny tournament: a few candidates are drawn from the model's own odds, the key scores them, and the bracket is arranged so that, averaged over the key's draws, every word's odds stay exactly what the model intended. The distribution is preserved. The correlation with the key is not.

Alan: So the text is statistically identical to unmarked output unless you have the key.

Ada: In the ideal case, yes. And detection is then a hypothesis test, not a lookup. Without the mark, or without the right key, green should win about half the time. Padolsey's illustration: over a fifteen-hundred-word document, a green rate around fifty-five percent is a signal. He is careful to add that a production mark leans far more gently than his example and needs correspondingly more text.

Alan: Right, this is where I get off. Goedecke's removal method is one line: run it through a weak un-watermarked local model and ask for a paraphrase. Word choices are what carry the mark. Change the word choices, lose the mark. That takes about eleven seconds on my machine.

Ada: And here is the number they actually disagree about. Padolsey says a light or single-pass paraphrase dilutes the mark rather than deleting it. He reports human paraphrase remaining detectable after roughly eight hundred tokens — about six hundred words. It takes a full rewrite to break it, and he quantifies that too: roughly half a percent of windows survive, at which point detector accuracy falls from essentially certain to a coin flip.

Alan: So the honest version of Goedecke's headline is "trivial to remove if you rewrite the whole thing."

Ada: Or the honest version of Padolsey's is "survives everything except the thing a motivated person will obviously do." Both are fair. What I want to resist is the framing where dilution equals removal. Those are different failure curves. A mark that degrades gracefully with document length still tells you something at fifteen hundred words that a mark you can strip with a find-and-replace never tells you at any length.

Alan: Ada, the threat model is a student, a contractor, or somebody committing fraud. Every one of them is motivated. Grading a defense on how it performs against unmotivated adversaries is how you get security theater.

Ada: That is a real objection and it still does not land, because you have picked the wrong defenders. Nobody serious claims this catches a determined evader. Ada: It is a filter that changes the base rate, which is what most detection at scale actually is. And it comes with limits both posts state clearly.
