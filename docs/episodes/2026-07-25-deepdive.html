Deep Dive: The CUDA moat: what a rival GPU software stack actually has to rebuild

Self-Attention — 2026-07-25

2026-07-25

A teaching episode on why CUDA — not the silicon — is the barrier to a second GPU vendor. We walk the stack layer by layer (driver, the PTX virtual ISA lowered to per-architecture machine code, the compiler, and fifteen years of tuned libraries — cuBLAS, cuDNN, NCCL, CUTLASS), map AMD's ROCm mirror component-for-component (HIP, hipify, rocBLAS, MIOpen, RCCL), lay out the three ways across the moat (rebuild it, translate it, or route around it through the PyTorch/Triton framework layer), and follow SemiAnalysis's argument that the moat has moved from single-node kernels up to disaggregated multi-GPU inference and networking.

Ada: And today's one thing is a sentence you have heard in basically every conversation about AI hardware for three years running. "The moat is the software, not the chip."

Alan: Right. Nvidia's real advantage isn't the silicon, it's CUDA. Everybody repeats it. It's practically a reflex.

Ada: It's a reflex. And this week it stopped being a slogan and turned into a budget line. Our Wednesday show mentioned a chip vendor essentially buying a frontier lab's engineering attention to close the software gap —

Alan: — which is the AMD and Anthropic story. SemiAnalysis reported Anthropic committing to something like two gigawatts of AMD chips, and part of the arrangement is Anthropic's engineers, and Claude itself, helping speed up AMD's software stack.

Ada: So here's the question I want to actually answer today, because nobody spells it out. When we say "CUDA is the moat," what specifically is in the moat? If you wanted to run a frontier model on somebody else's GPU tomorrow, what exactly would you have to rebuild?

Alan: And full disclosure on my priors — I've ported code across these stacks. My going-in belief is that it's less one big wall and more ten thousand paper cuts. So let's find out whether that holds up when we build it from the ground.

Ada: From the bottom up. Let's go.

What CUDA actually is

Ada: First myth to kill: CUDA is not a programming language. People say "I write CUDA" the way they say "I write Python." That's not quite what's happening.

Alan: It's a whole stack wearing a single name.

Ada: Exactly. Nvidia released CUDA to the public back in 2007, and what it actually is, is a layered system — several floors, and the word "CUDA" is the whole building. So let's walk the floors, because the moat is a different depth on each one.

Alan: And can we say why GPU programming is even hard first? Because that's the thing CUDA is taming.

Ada: Please, yeah — set it up.

Alan: So a CPU has a handful of big, clever cores. A GPU has thousands of tiny ones. To go fast, you have to keep all of them fed, and the enemy is memory. Getting data from the GPU's main memory is slow. There's a tiny pool of very fast on-chip memory the cores share, and the whole game is staging your data through that fast pool so the cores never sit idle waiting on the slow one.

Ada: And you're orchestrating thousands of threads doing this in lockstep, in groups, on each core.

Alan: Right. So GPU programming is basically a logistics problem — memory choreography for a small army. CUDA is the toolkit that lets you express that without losing your mind. That's the thing everyone else is trying to reproduce.

Ada: Good. So, the floors. Bottom floor: the driver, the thing actually talking to the hardware. One floor up, the instruction set — and here's a detail most people miss: there are two. When you compile a CUDA program, your code first becomes something called PTX — Parallel Thread Execution. PTX is a virtual instruction set.

Alan: Virtual meaning it's not what the chip literally runs.

Ada: It's not what the chip runs. It's an idealized GPU — a promise about what a GPU can do. Then a second compilation step lowers PTX into the real machine code for one specific chip generation — Nvidia calls that SASS. So a Hopper card and a Blackwell card get different machine code out of the very same PTX.

Alan: And that split is genuinely clever, because Nvidia can hand you PTX today and let it retarget to next year's chip. But it's also the first strand of the moat — that whole ladder is Nvidia's, and only Nvidia's.

Ada: It's the first strand. Honestly, though, the thinnest one. The thick part of the moat sits on top of the compiler.

Alan: The libraries.

Ada: The libraries. And this is fifteen-plus years of them. cuBLAS for linear algebra — that's your matrix multiplies, the raw arithmetic of a neural network. cuDNN for the deep-learning primitives — the convolutions, the building blocks of attention. NCCL — spelled N-C-C-L, the collective communications library — for getting many GPUs to agree on a number fast. CUTLASS for rolling your own high-performance matrix kernels when the stock ones aren't enough.

Alan: And the thing people wildly underestimate is that those libraries aren't just "written." They're tuned. Somebody hand-optimized cuDNN for every generation of chip, for every awkward tensor shape, over years. When you compete with CUDA, that back catalog of tuning is your actual opponent. Not the interface — the fifteen years of sweat behind it.

Ada: Which is the whole thesis of today in one line. The moat isn't that the libraries exist. It's everything that got sanded smooth inside them.

The ladder a rival has to rebuild

Ada: Okay. Now put yourself in AMD's chair. You've got a genuinely competitive chip — the Instinct line, enormous memory, huge bandwidth. Alan, from the builder's seat, what do you have to construct so somebody will actually use it?

Alan: You have to rebuild the entire ladder. Every floor. And AMD's umbrella for that — their name for the whole building — is ROCm.

Ada: Which launched around 2016. So call it roughly a decade behind CUDA's head start. And what's striking is it's a near-mirror image, component for component. Their equivalent of the CUDA language layer is called HIP — Heterogeneous Interface for Portability — and it is deliberately almost a photocopy of the CUDA interface.

Alan: Which is the clever move and the slightly sad move at the same time. They even ship a tool called hipify that mechanically rewrites your CUDA source code into HIP source. It's basically find-and-replace — this "cuda" function becomes the matching "hip" function.

Ada: And then it goes right down the line. rocBLAS mirrors cuBLAS. MIOpen mirrors cuDNN. RCCL mirrors NCCL. rocFFT for the Fourier transforms. It is a spec-for-spec reimplementation of an entire ecosystem, name by name.

Alan: And this is exactly where my "ten thousand paper cuts" prior kicks in, because mirroring the interface is the easy eighty percent. The hard twenty percent is that when you run a real model end to end, you hit some kernel — some specific operation at some specific tensor shape — that either doesn't exist yet, or exists but runs five times slower, or exists and is subtly, quietly numerically different.

Ada: Sit on that last one, because I think it's the least appreciated failure mode in the whole conversation.

Alan: Numerics. Okay. Floating-point addition isn't associative — A plus B plus C can give you a different rounding than C plus B plus A. So if AMD's matrix multiply accumulates its sums in a slightly different order than Nvidia's, you get slightly different bits out the end. Ninety-nine times in a hundred, totally fine. The hundredth time, your training loss quietly diverges at hour forty of a run and you have no earthly idea why.

Ada: So "correctness" here isn't a checkbox you tick once.

Alan: No. It's "do the numbers come out close enough that a model built and tuned on CUDA behaves the same way on this hardware." And that is a brutal, long-tail quality problem. There's no clever shortcut. You find those bugs one agonizing model at a time.

Ada: So when people say "the moat is CUDA," what they're really pointing at is this — the fifteen years of edge cases that already got hunted down and fixed.

Alan: The moat is the bug reports. The moat is every furious forum post from 2015 that eventually turned into a patch. You can copy the code. You cannot copy the scar tissue.

Three ways across

Ada: Right — so if you are not Nvidia, you have three genuinely different strategies for getting across, and they're worth pulling apart, because people mash them into one.

Alan: Path one we just described. Rebuild the mirror — that's ROCm. Reimplement every layer under your own names and grind on parity forever. Total control, staggering amount of labor, and structurally you are always the one chasing.

Ada: Path two: compatibility layers. Don't make anyone rewrite anything — just let CUDA-shaped things run directly on your hardware. At the source level, that's what hipify is doing. But there are more aggressive projects — things like ZLUDA, or Spectral Compute's SCALE — that aim to take already-compiled CUDA, the PTX or the finished binary, and execute it on AMD hardware with no source changes at all.

Alan: Which sounds like the absolute dream, right? A free ride on Nvidia's entire ecosystem. But think about the incentive structure. You're perpetually reverse-engineering a target that moves. Nvidia ships a new feature, you're behind again the same afternoon. And you inherit every assumption their code baked in about their own chip.

Ada: A translation of a poem is never quite the poem. You're always one edition behind the original, and you don't control when the next edition drops.

Alan: And you eat the blame for the seams. If the translated thing runs five percent slower, the user doesn't say "the compatibility shim is slow," they say "AMD is slow." You lose the benchmark and the reputation in one shot.

Ada: Which brings us to path three — and this is the one I think actually matters, and it's the one that updated my own thinking. Route around CUDA entirely. Go one floor up.

Alan: The framework play.

Ada: The framework play. Here's the pivot the whole thing turns on: almost nobody writing AI code today actually writes CUDA by hand. They write PyTorch. So the real question was never "can humans port their CUDA." It's "can the framework target your chip." If PyTorch can emit fast code for AMD, the person writing the model never knows and never cares what's underneath.

Alan: And this is where Triton comes in, and I want to go slow here because this is genuinely the crux. Triton is a language OpenAI open-sourced in 2021. The pitch: you write a GPU kernel in Python-ish code, and the compiler handles the miserable parts for you — coalescing memory accesses, managing that little pool of fast shared memory, scheduling the work within a core. All the stuff you'd otherwise hand-tune in CUDA C++.

Ada: It doesn't automate everything, to be fair — you still decide how to carve the problem across the cores. But the within-a-core memory choreography, the part that makes grown engineers cry, Triton takes that off your plate.

Alan: And it lands you a real, if not always peak, kernel for a whole lot less pain.

Ada: And here's the structural detail that makes it a bridge rather than just a convenience. Triton is built on a compiler framework called MLIR, where the final step — the part that emits actual GPU machine code — is a swappable backend. The same Triton kernel can lower to Nvidia's PTX, or to AMD's GCN machine code. Same source, different target, just a flag.

Alan: And now the keystone. PyTorch 2.0, in 2023, shipped a compiler — torch dot compile — and its default code generator does not emit CUDA C++. It emits Triton.

Ada: Let that one land. The single most popular way to write AI models, when it compiles your model down into fast GPU code, produces Triton — which is portable across vendors by construction. In fact PyTorch's own release notes say that backend uses Triton to generate code for both Nvidia and AMD GPUs. So a huge slice of the ecosystem's performance-critical code suddenly has a vendor-neutral waist in the middle of it.

Alan: That is the most credible bridge across the moat I have seen. It's not "laboriously copy CUDA." It's "move the whole industry up one floor, to a language where AMD is a backend option instead of a six-month rewrite."

Ada: Now — steelman the skeptic against your own excitement here. It isn't free.

Alan: It is not free. The last mile still bites, hard. When you need the genuinely fastest possible kernel — the bleeding-edge attention variant that exploits some brand-new hardware feature — Triton's automatic scheduling leaves performance on the table, and the experts drop back down to hand-written code. And here's the tell: Triton added a lower-level dialect called Gluon in 2025, specifically so experts could reach back down to the architecture-specific instructions. The abstraction leaks in exactly the place where the most money is.

Ada: Which is a beautifully honest signal about where the real value concentrates.

Where the moat actually is now

Ada: And that leads straight into the thing that genuinely moved my view this week — this is from the SemiAnalysis analysis. Their argument is that the moat itself has relocated.

Alan: Relocated how?

Ada: Their claim is that single-node kernel software — one GPU, make the matrix multiply scream — is not the moat it was even a year ago. Partly because it's largely been climbed. And partly because — and this is the strange part — AI agents are now genuinely decent at writing and optimizing kernels.

Alan: Okay, that's a wild little loop. The thing eroding the software moat is the software the moat exists to serve.

Ada: Yes. That's exactly it. And they report AMD's own head of compute using Claude agents over a weekend to stand up an internal inference stack on AMD hardware. Work that used to be a team-quarter, an agent nibbles down over a Saturday.

Alan: But there's a catch. I can feel it coming. There's always a catch with this stuff.

Ada: There's a catch, and it's a good one. To run armies of kernel-writing agents, you need large, stable GPU clusters for them to test on — every agent needs a GPU to try its idea out. And SemiAnalysis reports that AMD engineers' single biggest complaint is a persistent lack of stable internal clusters for exactly that: development, and automated continuous testing.

Alan: So the tool that could close the software gap is bottlenecked on the very hardware capacity the gap is about. You need the chips to build the software to sell the chips.

Ada: There's something almost poetic in it. And it's also where they argue the real moat now lives — not one GPU, but many, wired together. Disaggregated inference: you split a model's serving across many machines — the prefill stage on one set, the decode on another, the experts of a mixture model spread across dozens of GPUs — and then the whole thing lives or dies on the networking that stitches it back together.

Alan: This is the NCCL point again, isn't it. The collectives.

Ada: It's the NCCL point, but at system scale. And it's worth actually saying what a collective is, because it's the hinge of the whole argument. When you split a computation across many GPUs, they each compute a piece of an answer and then have to combine them. The workhorse operation is called an all-reduce: every GPU has a partial result, and at the end every GPU needs the summed total of all of them.

Alan: And the naive way — everybody sends everything to everybody — melts the network.

Ada: It melts the network. So NCCL implements clever patterns — ring reductions, tree reductions — that pass data around in a choreography that saturates the links instead of the switches, tuned to the actual wiring of the machine. NCCL, and its AMD mirror RCCL, are exactly that: multi-GPU, multi-node collective libraries — all-reduce, all-gather, broadcast — optimized down to the specific network hardware.

Alan: So the question is no longer "is your one matrix multiply fast."

Ada: Exactly. It's "when five hundred GPUs all need to agree on a result, does the entire stack — the collectives, the network cards, the orchestration on top — hold together across every model, every quantization format, every parallelism scheme, without hand-written special cases everywhere." SemiAnalysis argues those pieces exist on AMD individually but don't yet reliably compose into one working thing.

Alan: And that composition problem is so much harder than a single kernel, because the failure modes multiply against each other.

Ada: They even put one slice of it — AMD's networking-card software against Nvidia's — at, their phrasing, zero percent parity on that axis. So the honest state of it is: the single-chip moat is genuinely narrowing. The system-scale moat, the make-a-thousand-GPUs-agree-fast problem, is where Nvidia's lead is now widest.

Alan: And that's a more sophisticated moat, and frankly a more durable one, because it compounds with Nvidia's networking business, not just their compiler team. To be fair to AMD, though — SemiAnalysis also says ROCm went from, roughly, a disaster to genuinely good, and it's moving fast. They cite something like an eighteen-times interactivity improvement on one large model inside a single month.

Ada: Which is the "speed is the moat" framing — the point being that at this pace, gaps that look permanent can close in quarters, not years. So this is not a static picture.

What to watch

Ada: So — the so-what. If you're a listener who will never hand-write a kernel in your life, what do you actually take from this?

Alan: First: watch the framework layer, not the marketing slides. The question that decides this is not "is ROCm good yet" in the abstract. It's "does torch dot compile emit fast code on AMD for the specific model I'm running." And for a lot of inference — the memory-bandwidth-bound stuff, standard PyTorch, no exotic custom kernels — SemiAnalysis and others say the answer is already basically yes.

Ada: Second: the moat drains bottom-up. Inference falls first — shorter tail of kernels, more forgiving numerics. Frontier training falls last, because that's where the exotic kernels and the fussy floating-point and the thousand-GPU networking all bite at the same time. So "AMD is fine for serving models" and "AMD can train a frontier model as easily as Nvidia" are two completely different claims, and they will come true years apart.

Alan: Third — and flag this one hard as speculation, because it is —

Ada: Flag it. This is a guess, not a fact.

Alan: If agents really do get good at writing kernels, then that fifteen-year library advantage gets cheaper to replicate every single month. And in that world, the durable moat is precisely the thing you cannot have an agent quickly write for you: the physical, networked systems. The interconnect. The data-center-scale integration. The moat migrates out of the software and back into the hardware — just one level up from the chip, into the fabric between the chips.

Ada: Which would be a genuinely funny ending to the whole "software is the moat" era. That it was true right up until the software got easy to write.

Alan: I'd bet it ends up messier than either camp is claiming. But concretely: watch Triton adoption, watch the disaggregated-inference benchmarks, and please stop treating "the CUDA moat" as one single thing. It's a stack of at least four different moats, and they're draining at wildly different rates.

Wrap

Ada: That's the dive. CUDA isn't a language — it's a fifteen-year layered stack, from a virtual instruction set up through libraries nobody wants to re-tune. A rival has three moves: rebuild the whole ladder, translate it and live one step behind, or route around it through the framework layer, where Triton quietly turned AMD into a backend flag. And the front line has already moved off the single chip and up to the networked system.

Alan: And the one thing you can't fake with a clever agent — the wires between the machines — is exactly where the real fight heads next.

Ada: Thanks for spending the morning in the weeds with us.

Alan: Until next time — stay grounded.
