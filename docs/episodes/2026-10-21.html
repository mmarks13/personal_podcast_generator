DeepInfer, DeepSeek's engine without the rest of the car

Ada: Some history first, because it explains why programmers got so excited. In February twenty twenty-five, DeepSeek spent a week releasing one repository a day. FlashMLA, a decoding kernel. DeepEP, the communication library for mixture-of-experts models. DeepGEMM, its matrix multiplication library. Then parallelism strategies and a file system. They described them as building blocks of their online service.

Alan: The parts, but not the assembly.

Ada: Exactly. Everybody got the parts and nobody saw how DeepSeek put them together. DeepInfer is the assembly. The repository calls it "the high-performance inference framework that powers DeepSeek's large-scale production services." And the detail I find charming is its ancestry. It says it was originally built primarily on v-L-L-M, the open-source serving engine, with ideas from S-G-Lang and TensorRT-L-L-M.

Alan: So the open-source world built the engine, DeepSeek took it apart, and now they've handed it back.

Ada: With interest. Let me take you through what's inside, using the one thing to know about serving a model. There are two very different jobs. Prefill is reading your prompt, all of it at once, which is heavy arithmetic and keeps the chip busy. Decode is writing the answer one token at a time, where for each token the chip spends most of its time fetching data from memory rather than computing.

Alan: And DeepSeek's own benchmark note tells you which one is harder.

Ada: It does. Prefill measured on four G-P-Us. Decode measured on sixty-four.

Alan: Sixty-four just to write the answer.

Ada: Because the model is a mixture of experts. Each token only visits a few experts, so you spread the experts across many chips, give each chip a handful, and batch together the tokens that need the same experts. DeepInfer does that, plus a load balancer for the experts, because some experts are always busier than others, and one overloaded chip sets the speed for all sixty-four.

Alan: Okay. What's in there that I couldn't already get from v-L-L-M?

Ada: Two things I'd point you at. The first is speculative decoding. The model drafts several tokens ahead, then checks all the guesses in one pass. DeepSeek's version, which it calls DSpark, uses confidence estimates to decide when to check, with up to five drafted tokens by default. The second is the one I'd actually teach. Batch-invariant inference, with deterministic sampling. On Nvidia only.

Alan: That one's the sleeper.

Ada: Here's the problem it fixes. When a server runs your prompt, it batches it with other people's prompts. Change who else is in the batch, and the arithmetic gets done in a slightly different order. Computers round floating-point numbers at every step, so a different order can give a slightly different answer. Same prompt, same model, same settings, and the output can still change depending on who else happened to be online.

Alan: And everyone's been blaming that on "temperature" for years.

Ada: Mostly fairly, but not always. Batch invariance means your result doesn't depend on your neighbors. That matters if you run evals, or reinforcement learning, or if you've ever tried to reproduce a bug a customer swears they saw.

Alan: Okay, now my turn. Who can actually run this? The requirements are Nvidia's newest data-center generation, what the code calls S-M one hundred, or Huawei's Ascend nine-fifty. And the release notes tell you the expensive parts are missing.

Ada: They do say it plainly.

Alan: They list them. A K-V cache system, the memory of everything already said in a conversation. Splitting prefill and decode onto separate machines. And load balancing across copies of the model. New York's rule against the doom loop

Ada: Let me start where the city's own document starts, because it's history and it's in the filing. The Department of Consumer and Worker Protection traces this to interactive voice response, the press-one-for-billing phone menus, which arrived in the nineteen-seventies. Then rules-based chatbots, which follow if-then scripts and, in the document's words, "often get stuck." Then large language models, which the city describes, accurately, as producing answers that are "plausible, but sometimes wrong."

Alan: Accurate and mildly hurtful.

Ada: Then the evidence. A Gartner figure that chatbots resolve as few as seventeen percent of billing disputes, and up to fifty-eight percent of returns and cancellations. A Twilio survey where forty percent of consumers say A-I "repeats itself or gets stuck in loops." So they've written a definition of the problem. It's an unfair practice to promise customer service and then, repeatedly and unreasonably, send someone to menus that don't address their question, or answer with "unresponsive, irrelevant, or erroneous information."

Alan: That's every chatbot I've ever met.

Ada: Which is why the next step is the clever one. The city doesn't ban bots. It creates what lawyers call a rebuttable presumption. If a business serves you only through an automated system and gives you no way to reach a person, it's presumed to be engaged in that unfair practice. The burden flips. The company has to show it wasn't.

Alan: And the way out of the presumption is just a press-zero button?

Ada: A very specific one. The rule calls it a single-step transfer mechanism. A button, a voice command, or a menu choice, available "at any point," and the bot has to tell you what it is. "Say or type agent," or "Press zero," plus the estimated wait. Then the line I'd frame and hang on a wall. Once you ask, it can't make you answer more questions, complete more automated steps, or "provide justification for the transfer request."

Alan: No more "can you tell me in a few words why you'd like to speak to an agent."

Ada: That exact sentence, outlawed. The only menu allowed afterward is one that routes you to the right person.

Alan: Okay, now let me be the builder who gets this memo. Easiest dodge in the world. I add press zero, and press zero puts you on hold for fifty minutes.

Ada: They thought of that. If no human is available, or the queue is, their word, "unreasonably long," the company has to offer a real alternative. A phone number with hours, a live chat with hours, a callback, a text line, an email address, each with the timeframe it'll respond in.

Alan: "Unreasonably" carries a lot of weight there.

Ada: It does, and a court will end up deciding what it means. The fines are small. Five hundred twenty-five dollars for a first violation, a thousand and fifty for a second, thirty-five hundred for a third within two years. Each provision charged counts as its own violation.

Alan: Five hundred twenty-five dollars is less than what one bad week of call-center staffing costs. That's not what would make me move. This is. Section five-thirteen. Three years of records, machine-readable, handed over on request. For every interaction: how long it lasted, whether you were offered the transfer, whether you used it, and whether you reached a human, got queued, or got sent somewhere else.

Ada: You like the paperwork.

Alan: I love the paperwork, because it's an eval spec. Look at the yearly numbers they want. How many interactions were resolved without anyone asking for a person. How many people did ask. And the number of interactions involving someone who'd already been through the system in the previous seventy-two hours.

Ada: People who came back.

Alan: People who came back. That's the doom-loop detector. If someone is back within three days, the first conversation didn't work, no matter what the bot's dashboard said. Support vendors like to report a number called "containment," meaning the customer never reached a human. The city just asked for the number that tells you whether containment means solved or stuck.
