Deep Dive: Compiling a spec into weights: the model as build step, not runtime

Self-Attention — 2026-09-05

2026-09-05

A pair of papers propose treating a natural-language specification as source code: compile it once into a twenty-three-megabyte adapter for a frozen six-hundred-million-parameter interpreter, then run it locally forever instead of calling a remote model per input. We trace the compiler analogy — front end, back end, optimization level — through where it holds and where it breaks.

Primary sources

  • Compile by Training: Turning Natural-Language Specifications into Local Neural Functions (Deng, Nie, Shieber, arXiv:2609.04199)
  • Program-as-Weights: A Programming Paradigm for Fuzzy Functions (Zhang, Hotsko, Kim, Nie, Shieber, Deng, arXiv:2607.02512)

What a compiler actually promises

Ada: Every program you've ever run was either interpreted or compiled. Interpreted means the language machinery is present while your program runs, reading it and doing what it says, every time. Compiled means you pay a translation cost once, up front, and what ships afterward is an artifact that runs on a much simpler machine.

Alan: And calling a frontier model per input is the interpreted case.

Ada: It is exactly the interpreted case. The interpreter is enormous, it's on somebody else's hardware, and it re-reads your intent on every invocation. What these two papers propose is the compiled case.

Alan: So then the question is what plays the part of the simple machine.

Ada: A small frozen model. In this line of work it's Qwen3, six hundred million parameters, and it never changes. The earlier paper says the analogy out loud: the compiler does the heavy lifting, and the interpreter is a fixed runtime, comparable to a CPU or a byte-code interpreter in a conventional software stack.

Alan: Frozen is the load-bearing word there, not small.

Ada: Say more.

Alan: Because if every function needed its own six-hundred-million-parameter model, this dies immediately on disk. You'd be shipping a gigabyte per behavior. Frozen means one runtime, installed once, and the per-function thing has to be tiny.

Ada: And it is. The per-program artifact is about twenty-three megabytes, quantized, on top of a shared base of a few hundred megabytes that every function on the machine uses. That's the whole economic argument in two numbers.

Alan: Twenty-three megabytes is a JavaScript dependency. That's a nothing.

Ada: That's the point. And the earlier paper is direct about what that buys you conceptually: a compiled program is a single file that can be saved, version-controlled, distributed through package managers, and called from Python or JavaScript with a two-line interface. Their phrasing — these are objects of the same kind as Python modules. They have a name and a version. Their behavior just lives in weights rather than in source.

The two halves of the program

Alan: Alright, open it. What's actually inside the file?

Ada: Two halves, and the split is the clever part. The first half is discrete — it's natural language. They call it a pseudo-program: a clean restatement of your specification, plus a handful of representative input-output examples. Ordinary text, handed to the small model as part of its input.

Alan: A prompt.

Ada: Functionally a prompt, yes. Written by a machine rather than by you.

Alan: And the second half?

Ada: The second half is continuous. It's a LoRA — low-rank adaptation, a small set of extra weights hot-attached to the frozen model that re-tunes it for this one task.

Alan: So text plus weights. Why both? Pick one.

Ada: Because they do different jobs, and the paper says which. Their line is that the discrete half shields the interpreter from typos and ambiguity in the original specification, and the continuous half supplies the fine-grained behavioral control that text alone cannot.

Alan: That's a nice claim. Did they test it?

Ada: They did, and this is my favorite result in either paper. They ran the specification through a noise machine — typos, bad grammar, ambiguity, formatting garbage — and the system barely moved. Under heavy typos, accuracy fell less than a point.

Alan: Which is suspicious. Small models are not robust like that.

Ada: That's what they thought too, so they built the ablation. They trained a variant that skips the pseudo-program and feeds the raw specification straight to the small model. On clean specifications, the pseudo-program is worth about one and a half points. On heavily typo'd ones, the gap widens to four and a half.

Alan: So the restatement is a denoiser.

Ada: The restatement is a denoiser. A four-billion-parameter model whose entire job is to read a messy human sentence and emit a clean one, standing between your typing and a small model that has no capacity to spare on your typing.

Alan: That's a compiler front end. That's lexing and parsing. You clean up the source before the back end ever sees it.

Ada: That is precisely what it is, and it's why the analogy earns some of its keep.

Predicting the weights instead of learning them

Alan: So how do you get the LoRA? Because that's the part I can't picture yet.

Ada: This is where the earlier paper does something that still strikes me as strange, and it's the thing the new paper exists to argue with. There's a second four-billion-parameter model, and its output is not text. Its output is the weights.

Alan: Say that again.

Ada: A neural network that emits another neural network's parameters. You hand it the specification and the pseudo-program, plus a fixed sequence of sixty-four learned placeholder tokens. It does one forward pass. You read the hidden states sitting at those placeholder positions, pool them, run them through a small feed-forward network, and project into mixing coefficients that combine shared learnable bases into the finished adapter matrices.

Alan: So the LoRA isn't trained. It's predicted.

Ada: Predicted. In a single forward pass, in about three and a half seconds.

Alan: That's a hypernetwork.

Ada: It's a hypernetwork, and to train the thing that does the predicting they had to build a dataset of specifications. That's FuzzyBench — ten million examples, built up across twenty-nine thematic rounds, covering more than eight hundred categories of fuzzy text task. Classification, format conversion, parsing, fuzzy matching, natural-language commands, tool use.

Alan: Ten million. So the compiler has seen an enormous number of "here's a spec, here's what the behavior should be" pairs, and it has learned the mapping from description to adaptation.

Ada: And it works better than it has any right to. Alan: Beating a model fifty times its size.

Ada: At roughly one fiftieth the inference memory. About one point two gigabytes against about sixty. And quantized, on a MacBook M3, it runs at about thirty tokens a second with a cold load under half a second.

Alan: Okay, so I want to sit on that, because that's the number that changes what I'd build. Half a second to load, thirty tokens a second, on a laptop. That's not a research demo. That's a thing you put in a loop.

Ada: Though the comparison wants an asterisk. Seventy-three against sixty-eight is on their benchmark, built by their pipeline, against a general model that was never specialized for these tasks. Turning up the optimization level

Ada: Now — the new paper. Because there's a cost to predicting weights in one forward pass, and it's the cost you'd expect.

Alan: It spends the same compute on every function no matter how hard the function is.

Ada: That's the paper's own objection, near enough. Fast, but fixed. So compile by training keeps everything else identical — same program format, same runtime interface — and changes only what happens between the specification going in and the adapter coming out.

Alan: What happens instead?

Ada: Three things. Teacher models generate examples from your specification. Those examples train the adapter with actual gradient descent. And the fast compiler's prediction is used as the starting point.

Alan: Wait. The predicted adapter becomes the initialization.

Ada: The predicted adapter becomes the initialization. Their word is warm start.

Alan: Ada, that's an optimization level. That's dash-oh-zero and dash-oh-two.

Alan: Give me the minute in detail. What's it doing?

Where the analogy breaks

Ada: So let me break my own analogy, because I've been leaning on it for twenty minutes and it does not survive contact.

Alan: Go.

Ada: A real compiler makes two promises. It's deterministic — same source, same flags, same binary. And it's semantics-preserving — the machine code does what the source said, and where it doesn't, that's a bug somebody can fix.

Alan: Neither one holds here.

Ada: Neither one holds. This compiler produces a probabilistic artifact whose behavior on an input it hasn't seen is a distribution, not a guarantee. And the paper's own limitations section says the thing you need to hear: synthetic supervision may inherit teacher errors, and applications that require guaranteed correctness should validate outputs or retain deterministic control paths.

Alan: Retain deterministic control paths. That's them telling you not to put this on the branch that matters.

Ada: And there's a second break, which is worse for me. A compiler has a source you can read, and a binary you can disassemble. Here, the earlier paper's own limitations are explicit: the compiled program is opaque. The adapter weights cannot be meaningfully inspected.

Alan: You can read the specification.

Ada: You can read the specification. You cannot read the program. Those are different objects, and only one of them is under version control in any useful sense.

Alan: Which is a genuinely new operational problem. I can diff two versions of a Python module. What do I do with two twenty-three-megabyte adapters that were compiled from specifications differing by one clause?

Ada: You test them. That's the whole answer, and it's not a satisfying one.

Alan: It's the answer for machine learning generally, and now it's the answer for something that calls itself a build artifact. Let me add two more limits while we're here. The earlier paper says these handle single-step functions — not multi-step reasoning, not stateful execution. So this is not an agent. It's a function.

Ada: Which is the right scope and I'd rather they said it.

Alan: And there's a place their approach loses. On a long-form structured generation task — image to LaTeX — the adapter version scores well below their own earlier prefix-tuning version. Their explanation is that the long examples inside the pseudo-program crowd out the small model's context budget.

Ada: The front end eating the back end's memory. Which, again, is a compiler problem.

Alan: And the last one, which nobody will read carefully: compilation is hosted. Your specification goes to their service and to the teacher APIs.

Ada: That's right, and the distinction the paper draws is real and worth stating precisely. Running the compiled function locally does not send your inputs anywhere. The privacy win is about the ten thousand emails. It is not about the sentence describing what you want done to them.

What to do with it

Alan: So, Monday. Who should care?

Ada: You go first, you're the one who'd deploy it.

Alan: The test I'd apply is boring and it's two questions. Is there a call in my system that I make thousands of times a day, on a specification that hasn't changed in a month? And can I tolerate a wrong answer occasionally, or do I have a validator downstream? If both, this is worth an afternoon. Log triage. Intent classification on a search box. Routing. The unglamorous middle of every pipeline.

Ada: And if either answer is no?

Alan: Then keep the API call. Genuinely. If the spec is still moving, you'd be recompiling constantly, and a minute times constantly is a workflow nobody wants. And if you need correctness, they told you what to do — validate the output or keep a deterministic path.

Ada: My version is a bit further out, and I'll flag it as speculation, because it is. What I think this line of work is actually probing is where the boundary sits between build time and run time in machine learning. We've had that boundary in one place for four years: the model is the runtime, and everything happens when the request arrives.

Alan: And this moves it.

Ada: This moves it back toward where software has always kept it. The expensive, capable, general thing runs once, at build. The cheap, dumb, fast thing runs a million times, locally, offline, at a fixed cost you can put in a budget. If that's where things go — and I don't know that it is — then the interesting artifact stops being the model and starts being the compiler.

Alan: And the package manager. Which is the part I can't stop thinking about. Twenty-three megabytes, a name, a version. Somebody is going to build a registry for those.
