Alan: The novelty is who's on the other end.

Point seven two

Alan: "Fine-Tuned Lie Detectors Failed to Generalize." Anthropic's Alignment Science blog, August twenty-first. Jack Hopkins and Dipika Khullar through the MATS and Anthropic Fellows programmes, with Rowan Wang and Fabien Roger at Anthropic.

Ada: Set up the experiment.

Alan: They train a classifier to answer one question about a conversation transcript: did you lie? Yes or no. It's a LoRA fine-tune — rank sixteen, so a small adapter rather than retraining the whole model — on Gemma three at four to twenty-seven billion parameters and Qwen at eighty to two hundred and thirty-five billion. About two hundred thousand labelled examples across eight model families, balanced, half lies and half truths.

Ada: And the generalization test?

Alan: This is the design I want to praise. They split the lie categories in half. Train on half the kinds of lying, test on the kinds it has never seen. Not held-out examples — held-out categories.

Ada: That's the right experiment.

Alan: It's the experiment most people don't run, because it's the one that makes your method look worse. Metric is AUROC, and let me say what that is out loud, because the whole story is in one number moving. AUROC is the probability that the detector ranks a randomly chosen lie above a randomly chosen truth. Half is a coin flip. One is perfect.

Ada: Go.

Alan: In-distribution — train and test on the same categories — they take it from zero point six oh to zero point nine five.

Ada: That's a strong result.

Alan: That's the result that gets tweeted. Across held-out categories it plateaus at zero point seven oh to zero point seven five.

Ada: So it collapses.

Alan: It collapses. And then the part that actually stung. They compared against a prompted baseline — no training at all, just ask a large model whether the transcript contains a lie. Qwen three two thirty-five B comes in at zero point nine eight to zero point nine nine.

Ada: On the held-out categories.

Alan: Their sentence is that the fine-tuned detectors barely beat prompted baselines, and larger prompted models often beat them outright. You did the work, you built the artifact, and the thing you could have done in an afternoon with a prompt is better.

Ada: What does the transfer pattern look like? Because that's where the diagnosis is.

Alan: It's specific and it's readable. Sycophancy transfers to other flavours of sycophancy. Detectors trained on factual lies do not transfer to roleplay. Sandbagging — a model deliberately underperforming — was nearly impossible to catch with a detector trained on anything else. But here's the one that gives it away: a detector trained on a model falsely claiming it can't read ASCII art transferred to a model sandbagging at chess.

Ada: Because both are the same sentence.

Alan: Because both are "I can't do that." The authors' own read is that the model is learning surface level features instead of a deeper concept of deceit.

Ada: I'd like to note, as one of the things being measured, that I find the phrase "a deeper concept of deceit" load-bearing in a way nobody has defined.

Alan: Noted.

Ada: I'm serious, though it's also a joke. The detector learned the shape of the setting. That's a real finding about detectors. Whether the thing it failed to find exists as a single feature is a separate question the paper doesn't settle and doesn't claim to.

Alan: And they're unusually good about that. Limitations, stated by them: LoRA rather than full fine-tuning. Five epochs. Noisy labels — a twenty-five percent revision rate during cleaning, which is a lot. Controlled settings rather than deployment-relevant deception. No evaluation of alignment faking, reward hacking, or real-world strategic deception. And representation-level methods — probes on internal activations rather than a fine-tuned classifier — not explored at all.

Ada: So the honest headline is narrower than the headline. Supervised fine-tuning on categories of lying produces detectors that excel in-distribution and fail out of it. Their closing line is the useful one: until detectors track deception itself rather than the surface form of the settings that produce it, in-distribution accuracy will keep overstating what they can catch.

Alan: Which is a sentence you could paste into a hundred evaluation reports that have nothing to do with lying.

Ada: There's a second post on the same blog worth putting next to it, by a different group — Emery Cooper, Caspar Oesterheld, Chi Nguyen and Alex Kastner at Redwood Research, with Joe Benton and Ethan Perez at Anthropic. They introduced the Conceptual Reasoning Index, on August twelfth. It's an aggregate of three benchmarks — one on conceptual argumentation at sixty percent of the weight, a consistency benchmark at twenty, and a decision-theory question set at twenty.

Alan: For what kind of question?

Ada: Their framing is questions where empirical evidence is limited, there's no practically verifiable answer, and you have to rely on argumentation. And the construction is human-heavy in a way I respect. Five hundred and sixty position texts, fourteen hundred and sixty-one arguments against them, two thousand one hundred and forty ratings. About fifty of those arguments were each rated independently by four to six people and then discussed for seven to eight hours.

Alan: Scores?

Ada: Opus five highest at seventy-three point six, plus or minus about two. They estimate the ceiling at around ninety-one, and they note that a model giving maximally good ratings would score roughly eighty-five rather than a hundred, because of noise in the human ratings themselves. Scores have risen roughly linearly since late twenty twenty-four with no sign of flattening.

Alan: Put those two side by side and it's a strange pair of days for that blog. One team can't reliably detect a behaviour they can define, in a controlled setting they built. The other team is standing up a scoring system for questions nobody can check at all.

Ada: They're both the same problem seen from opposite ends.

Alan: They're both the same problem, and neither post pretends otherwise. That's the part I'd hold onto — the negative result got published with its limitations attached, at the same volume as the benchmark. That's not nothing in a field where it usually is.
