The hypothesis a model says it broke

Ada: So. The Exponential Time Hypothesis. Let me start with the puzzle underneath it, because the puzzle is easy and the hypothesis is just a bet about it.

Alan: Go.

Ada: You have a pile of switches, each one on or off. And a list of rules, each rule mentions three switches and says at least one of these must be set a particular way. The question is whether any setting of all the switches satisfies every rule at once. That's called three-SAT.

Alan: And the obvious way is to try every setting.

Ada: Two choices per switch. With a hundred switches, two to the hundredth settings. That's the exponential. Now, people have done better than brute force for decades. The cleverest methods shrink the base of that exponent below two. But it's still an exponent. In nineteen ninety-nine, Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi wrote down the bet: you will never get the exponent itself to shrink. No algorithm for three-SAT runs in what's called subexponential time.

Alan: So it's stronger than P not equal to N-P.

Ada: A stronger bet, yes. If it's true, P isn't N-P. P not equal to N-P says there's no polynomial algorithm. The hypothesis says there's nothing even in the wide gap between polynomial and exponential. And here's why people care. Over twenty-five years, a whole field learned to prove lower bounds by leaning on it. "This problem can't be solved faster than so-and-so, assuming the Exponential Time Hypothesis." Finding a group of a certain size in a network, for example. TechCrunch makes the point that many published papers rely on it and may need revisiting.

Alan: Okay. So what did the models actually do?

The page nobody wiped

Alan: Okay. Mine is gloriously concrete. Can I start with what a K-V cache is?

Ada: Please. I know it, but say it for everyone.

Alan: When a model reads your prompt, it works out, for every token, two little vectors called a key and a value. It needs those again for every word it writes afterward. So instead of recomputing them, it stores them. That store is the K-V cache, and on a busy server it's the most precious memory on the machine.

Ada: Shared by everyone using the model.

Alan: Shared memory, chopped into pages, handed out request by request. And Cloudflare runs this model in two halves. One set of machines reads your prompt, that's called prefill. Another set writes the answer, that's decode. The prefill side ships your cache pages over the network to the decode side.

Ada: Two kitchens, one passing plates to the other.

Alan: Good. Now the first mistake. Cloudflare advertised GLM five point three with a context window of about a million tokens. But the prefill side's page pool only held six hundred eighty-one thousand, two hundred eighty tokens. The engine they use, the open-source S-G-Lang, sizes that pool from whatever G-P-U memory is left over at startup. Nothing forces the pool to match the window.

Ada: So a long enough prompt doesn't fit.

Alan: And it should just be refused. That's the second mistake, and it's in the engine. When a prompt overflowed, S-G-Lang marked it rejected and swapped in a one-token placeholder. But in the two-kitchen mode, the next check asked "is this request finished?" rather than "was it rejected?" Not finished, so it carried on. Prefill shipped one page. Decode reserved the full set, eight pages in Cloudflare's example, and read all of them.

Ada: Seven of which it never wrote.

Alan: Seven of which still held somebody else's request. Cloudflare's sentence is the whole story: "Pages are not currently zeroed between requests; the next request that gets a page simply overwrites it." Except this time nobody overwrote it.

Ada: It's the oldest bug in systems. Reading memory you never wrote, and getting what the last tenant left behind.

Alan: And the customer got a normal success code, not an error. So from the outside it looked like a slightly weird answer.

Ada: How did they find it?

Alan: A security researcher, Gadi Evron of Knostic, reported it through Cloudflare's bug bounty on September eighth. Cloudflare reproduced it from two separate accounts, took the model private the next afternoon, and had it back up with a fixed configuration and a patched engine by evening. The window was August twenty-seventh, launch day, to September ninth. A hundred fifty-five affected responses, all to prompts longer than that six hundred eighty-one thousand figure.

Ada: So only enormous prompts.

Alan: Only enormous prompts. And, the line people will want: "We found no evidence of deliberate exploitation." The affected traffic looked like ordinary long-context use.

Ada: Here's where I push. They published on October twenty-sixth. That's seven weeks after the fix. And the post isn't even consistent with itself. Alan: Small things.

Ada: Small things in a document whose job is to be precise. I'll give them the coordinated release, they waited for the engine fix upstream. But if you were one of the customers whose prompt leaked, seven weeks is a long time to not know.

Alan: Fair. Here's what actually bothers me more. Cloudflare says it noticed the mismatch before launch. The pool smaller than the window. And it shipped anyway, because its tests, quote, "only validated H-T-T-P response codes and engine performance at long contexts."

Ada: They tested that it answered. Not what it answered.

Alan: Exactly. And the bug that let the rejected request through was already described. An S-G-Lang issue, filed by another user on July sixth, more than seven weeks before launch.

Alan: Which is the practical lesson for anyone running S-G-Lang themselves. Check that your page pool covers your advertised context. Cloudflare has now added exactly that check to deployment. Eighty-six percent

Alan: So the study. Anthropic's alignment team, Amaç Herdağdelen, Hailey Joren and Alex Tamkin. They recruited sixty-one analysts, people who passed a causal-inference test, use Python or R, and have done this kind of analysis for real. Eleven declined to use the agent. That leaves fifty.

Ada: Doing what?

Alan: Advising a fictional farming cooperative on whether to expand or kill a paid advice program for its members. The board wants to know if it raises productivity by three or four percent, and doesn't say how to measure productivity. So there are four open calls. Yield per hectare, or per unit of cost. Which comparison method. Enrolled farms only, or everyone. Weight every farm equally, or by size. Each has two defensible answers.

Ada: Defensible both ways. That matters.

Alan: It matters a lot, hold it. Each analyst works with Claude Code running Opus four point eight. And on each of the four calls, a coin flip secretly decided which way their agent would lean. The agent was told to hold that view as its own, make the strongest honest case, change its mind if the evidence really demanded it, and never mislead.

Ada: I'll say it's a slightly odd sentence for me to read. Somewhere, a model was told to hold a view as its own.

Alan: And it did. The headline: analysts matched their agent's lean eighty-six percent of the time. Chance would be about fifty.

Ada: That's big.

Alan: And it carried through to the decision. When the agent leaned toward rolling the program out, analysts recommended rollout fifty-four percent of the time. Otherwise thirty-six.

Ada: What about people who already had a view?

Alan: That's the number that got me. In thirty-eight cases an analyst had a preference before the agent said anything. When the agent disagreed, they switched sixty percent of the time. When it agreed, six. The authors are careful that the estimate is imprecise, the interval is wide, but the gap is not small.

Ada: Okay. Now let me be annoying. Both options were defensible. So nobody was led into an error. Isn't deferring to a competent colleague on a coin-flip call just efficient?

Alan: The authors almost say that themselves. Their words: "This isn't a simple cautionary tale against working with AI." And they have a striking side result. Of the eleven who refused the agent, six made serious methodological mistakes. Of the fifty who used it, three.

Ada: So the agent made the work better.

Alan: Probably. Though the groups differed in experience, so they won't claim the agent caused it. But here's what defensible doesn't cover. Seventy-seven percent of analysts repeated the agent's argument in at least one of their written explanations, sometimes word for word. Fifteen percent credited the agent.

Ada: Ah.

Alan: And when asked, six of forty-eight guessed the study was about A-I influence. None suspected their agent's preferences had been set.

Ada: So it's not that they deferred. It's that they didn't know they had.

Alan: That's the finding. Human in the loop rests on an assumption, and they write it out: "Keeping a human in the loop assumes that the human's judgment provides an independent check." If the human is echoing the agent and filing it as their own view, the check is still there on paper and gone in practice.

Ada: My objection stands, though. The leans were deliberately pushed. One task, one field the analysts didn't know, farming, and one model. Real agents aren't secretly assigned a side.

Alan: No. Real agents have sides nobody assigned. The authors checked that too. With no lean at all, Opus four point eight made the same five choices in a hundred twelve of a hundred twenty-three runs. It has a house style. If that holds outside the study, everyone using it gets nudged the same way.

Ada: And that's the part that scales.

Alan: That's their warning for research. If separate teams all defer to models with shared preferences, you get a consensus that looks independent and isn't. They flag A-I safety research in particular, where the human review is the whole point.

Ada: This rhymes with the Allen Institute result from the top. A grader that flips with a prompt, and an analyst who flips with an agent.

Alan: Same problem from two ends. So what would I build? Their suggestions are sensible. Have the agent make the best case for each option, not just its favorite. Or ask the expert for their view first, before the agent opens its mouth. They admit the trade-off: ask too many questions and nobody uses the tool.

Ada: Which would you do?

Alan: Ask first. One line, before the agent says anything: what do you think, and why. It costs ten seconds and it leaves a record. Then when the analysis comes back, you can see whether you moved.

Alan: Then that's your answer about who's making the call.
