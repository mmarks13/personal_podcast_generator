Alan: So. Terminology first, because "agent framework" has been marketing noise for two years and this is a specific thing.

DeepSeek V4.1 Flash: eight hundred ninety bytes a token

Ada: So there's an argument running under the release post, and I'd rather start there than with the model, because the argument teaches the model.

Alan: The size fight.

Ada: The size fight. The announcement said five hundred fifty-two billion parameters. Somebody went and read the tensor files and posted that it's seven hundred forty-eight. Both of them are reading the same model card correctly.

Alan: How does that happen.

Ada: Because the card describes two different things that both hold parameters. There's a five-hundred-fifty-two-billion-parameter backbone — that's the transformer, the thing that computes. And then there's a separate component they call Engram, at a hundred ninety-six billion parameters, which the card describes as conditional memory, sparsely accessed via token-based lookup.

Alan: Lookup, not computation.

Ada: That's the distinction, and it's the thing worth taking away from this whole release. Those hundred ninety-six billion parameters aren't layers the input flows through. They're storage that gets indexed by the tokens you're currently looking at. You retrieve from it; you don't run through it. So when someone says five fifty-two, they mean the part that computes. When someone says seven forty-eight, they're adding the filing cabinet to the engine. And the Hugging Face page's own parameter count says seven hundred sixty-three billion, which is a third number, so nobody in that thread is being dishonest and everybody is confused.

Alan: And what does it cost to run, in the only sense I care about, which is per token?

Ada: This is where it gets genuinely interesting. Eight billion parameters active per token during prefill. Sixteen billion during decode.

Alan: Wait. Decode is the expensive one? That's backwards from every model I've deployed.

Ada: It's backwards from the usual arrangement, yes. Prefill is where you're reading the prompt and you have the whole thing at once, so it's the part that's normally compute-bound and heavy. Decode is one token at a time. Having decode be the heavier of the two in activated parameters is a deliberate choice about where they want the work to land.

Alan: And the rest of the architecture?

Ada: Forty layers, arranged as a twenty-layer causal encoder followed by a twenty-layer decoder — they call it a causal encoder-decoder. Inside the mixture-of-experts layers, one shared expert and three hundred eighty-four routed experts, six of which activate per token. There's a vision encoder trained from scratch that turns images into embeddings processed jointly with text from the very start of pre-training, rather than bolted on afterwards. Forty-five trillion tokens of pre-training, with context extended to a million at the thirty-four trillion mark.

Alan: All right, you promised me a number in bytes.

Ada: Eight hundred ninety bytes per token of key-value cache.

Alan: Say the whole thing, because that number is meaningless without the thing it's measured against.

Ada: The key-value cache is the memory an inference server keeps so it doesn't recompute the whole conversation for every new token. It grows linearly with context and it lives on the same card as the weights, so in practice it's the thing that decides how long a conversation can get. They're storing the main cache in four-bit floating point, with one scaling factor per sixteen channels, and the card says that comes to roughly a quarter of what the previous Flash needed — eight hundred ninety bytes a token. There's a second mechanism for the persistent part that they put at about an eighth.

Alan: So a full million-token conversation is —
