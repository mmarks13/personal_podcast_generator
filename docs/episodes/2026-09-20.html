The benchmark that stops being the bottleneck

Alan: This is the one I'd use tomorrow. NVIDIA's developer blog, Friday, Francesco Di Natale and five colleagues, on benchmarking language model inference at scale with a tool they're calling AIPerf. It replaces their older one, GenAI-Perf, and they describe it as a complete architectural redesign.

Ada: A benchmark tool getting a rewrite is not usually a story.

Alan: It is when you read why. The old design was single process, and their line is that a single-process architecture becomes GIL-bound under real concurrency or request rate.

Ada: Oh, that's lovely. The benchmarking client was the bottleneck.

Alan: The benchmarking client was the bottleneck. So your server looks like it tops out at some number of requests, and what you've actually measured is Python's global interpreter lock on your own laptop. The new one spreads load across worker processes with separate record processors, talking over ZMQ, specifically so the measuring instrument saturates the server instead of itself.

Ada: That's an old, old failure. You measure the ruler.

Alan: And the second half is the part I want people to steal, which is what they say to measure. Four things. Time to first token — how long from sending the request to the first token coming back. Inter-token latency — the gap between successive tokens once it's going. Request latency — end to end for the whole response. And output token throughput — tokens per second across all concurrent requests.

Ada: Why four and not one?

Alan: Because they fail independently and they tell you different things. Healthy time to first token and bad inter-token latency means your prefill is fine and your decode is struggling. Bad time to first token with fine decode is the opposite, and it's what a user actually feels as slowness. And throughput across all concurrent requests is your capacity planning number, which is unrelated to how fast it feels to any one person.

Ada: And each of those is reported as a distribution, I'd hope, not an average.

Alan: Percentiles. Twenty-fifth through ninety-ninth, plus min, max, mean, standard deviation. Which matters because the ninety-ninth percentile is where your angriest user lives.

Ada: The piece I like is the arrival pattern, if I read you right.

Alan: Yeah — you can configure constant arrivals, Poisson, or gamma with adjustable burstiness, plus synthetic loads with varying input and output lengths. Because real traffic isn't a metronome. It's quiet and then it's four hundred requests because someone's batch job woke up, and your tail latency lives entirely inside those bursts.

Ada: I'll do the obvious thing and note who published it. This is the company that sells the accelerators, shipping the instrument you use to decide whether the accelerators are fast enough.

Alan: Fair. Though the metrics themselves are standard enough that the tool being theirs doesn't bend them much.

Ada: My sharper version is: none of those four numbers has an axis for whether the answers were any good. You can serve wrong answers at an excellent ninety-fifth percentile.

Alan: Completely true, and out of scope for a load generator, and also exactly how people misuse it — a throughput number gets quoted as if it were a quality number. If somebody quotes you tokens per second without telling you the concurrency level and which percentile, they haven't given you a number. They've given you a mood.
