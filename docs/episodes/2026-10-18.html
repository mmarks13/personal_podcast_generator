Alan: And the fifty-three hours?

Ada: That's the elliptic-curve task. Point addition on an elliptic curve. Alan: Here's where I need the mechanism, because "the A-I found a better circuit" tells me nothing.

Ada: Right. So here's the history you need. A quantum circuit has to be reversible. You can't just throw information away mid-computation the way your laptop overwrites a register. Every scratch value you compute has to either stay around, taking up a qubit, or be carefully un-computed. Charles Bennett worked out the trick for reversible computing back in nineteen seventy-three: run forward, copy the answer out, then run the whole thing backward to clean up. It works. It's also why reversible arithmetic is so fat. Memory of the past is the expensive thing.

Alan: So the cost is remembering.

Ada: The cost is remembering. Now, the slowest step in elliptic-curve point addition is a modular inversion. Ordinary computers have a good method for it called divsteps. It starts with a pair of numbers and simplifies them over many rounds, and at each round it takes a branch. On a laptop that's fine. In a reversible circuit, you'd have to store which branch you took, every round, so you can undo it later. That's the history that made divsteps impractical for quantum arithmetic.

Alan: And Claude found a way not to store it.

Ada: It found a way to read it back. It proved new algebraic identities showing that the branch information can be decoded from the numbers themselves. The state already remembers its own past, if you know how to look. It checked that with large numerical simulations, and then formalized the claim in Lean, the proof assistant. So that piece isn't "the evaluator was happy." There's a machine-checked proof.
