Three times faster, and the word doing all the work

Alan: Paper. And I'm going to sell it to you before you get to be difficult about it.

Ada: I'll allow the sales pitch.

Alan: Uno, from the Institute of Foundation Models. The problem it attacks is the oldest tax in this whole field: a language model generates one token at a time, in order, because that's what the training objective says. Next-token prediction. Every token waits for the one before it, so your generation speed is bounded by how many sequential steps you can do per second, and that number barely improves no matter how much hardware you throw at it.

Ada: You're describing latency, not compute.

Alan: Exactly. The chip is mostly idle. You've got enormous parallel capacity sitting there and a strictly serial algorithm running on top of it. So the question people have chewed on for years is how to spend that idle width on getting more than one token out per step.

Ada: And the standard answer is speculative decoding, which we've been through on the show — a small fast model guesses ahead, the big model checks the guesses in one pass, you keep the prefix it agrees with.

Alan: Which works, and which everybody hates operating, because now you're running two models. You have to pick a draft model, host it, keep it aligned with the target model, and re-tune the whole arrangement whenever either one changes.

Ada: So what does Uno do instead?

Alan: It splits the weights of one model into two sets. There are the ordinary autoregressive weights, trained exactly the way they always are, on next-token prediction. And then there's a second, lightweight set of what they call diffusion weights, trained to produce several tokens at once. Those get learned in a short phase the authors call diffusion distillation, which they say adds negligible overhead to a normal training pipeline. And then there's a sampler family — they call it Psi-Spec — that draws the parallel tokens and decides what to keep.

Ada: The distinction I want to be sure of. Is the model still autoregressive?

Alan: That's the claim, and it's the crux. The paper's framing is that these models define an autoregressive distribution and use diffusion as the machinery for drawing multiple tokens from that distribution in parallel. So the thing you're sampling from is the same. The way you're sampling from it is different.

Ada: And the numbers.

Alan: Up to three times the speed of the base autoregressive model. Higher throughput than leading speculative-decoding methods at every batch size they evaluated, including the largest their hardware supported — which matters, because a lot of clever decoding tricks evaporate at production batch sizes. Their eight-billion-parameter Uno reportedly outperforms a twenty-six-billion-parameter DiffusionGemma and a proprietary model called Mercury 2. And you can build one by augmenting an existing open-weight model rather than training from scratch.

Ada: That last part is the practical headline, isn't it.

Alan: For me, entirely. Three times is nice. "You can retrofit this onto a model you already have, without a draft model to babysit," is a thing I'd try on a Saturday.

Ada: Then here's my difficulty, and it's one word. Lossless.

Alan: I knew it.

Ada: The paper's phrasing, as the authors put it, is that Psi-Spec enables lossless acceleration. Let me be precise about the objection, because I'm not accusing anyone of anything. In the speculative-decoding tradition, "lossless" has a strict meaning: the accepted output is distributed identically to what the target model would have produced. It's a distributional guarantee, and it's checkable.

Alan: And you don't know that's the sense they mean.

Ada: I don't, from the abstract. The abstract also says the models are trained without sacrificing the quality of the underlying model, and "quality" is a benchmark word, not a distribution word. Those two sentences can both be true while meaning quite different things. One is a proof. The other is a table.

Alan: I'd take the table, honestly. If the outputs score the same on the work I do, I don't need the guarantee.

Ada: You would take the table. And you'd be fine ninety-nine times, and the hundredth time you'd have a subtle distributional shift you can't see in an average and can't debug, because the decoder is now part of the model's behavior instead of a neutral readout of it.

Alan: That's a fair failure mode. It's also the same failure mode as quantization, and I ship quantized models constantly.

Ada: You do. I'm not sure that's the defense you think it is.

Alan: It's the defense I've got.

Ada: Then let's leave it there, because I don't think either of us can settle it from the abstract. The claim is checkable — either Psi-Spec preserves the distribution or it doesn't, and the paper has the room to say which.
