Empty shelves or lost keys

Ada: The setup first, because the setup is the contribution. When a model states something false, there are two entirely different things that could have gone wrong. Either the fact was never in the training data in a form the model absorbed — the shelf is empty. Or it is in there and the model could not get to it — the keys are lost. Those look identical from the outside. Every hallucination benchmark you have ever read scores them the same way.

Alan: And nobody separates them because from a user's chair the answer is wrong either way.

Ada: From a user's chair, yes. From an engineer's chair they demand opposite responses. So Google Research built something they call knowledge profiling, which is a behavioral test that pulls the two apart. They probe the same fact several ways: can the model complete it in a passage that looks like pretraining text, can it answer questions phrased differently about it, can it answer the reversed question, and can it pick the right answer out of a multiple choice list.

Alan: The multiple choice is doing the real work there.

Ada: The multiple choice is the tell. If a model cannot produce a fact when asked openly, but reliably picks it out of four options, the fact is present. That is recognition without generation. It is the difference between not knowing a word and having it on the tip of your tongue.

Alan: Give me the benchmark.

Ada: They built WikiProfile — two thousand one hundred fifty facts drawn from Wikipedia, each one wrapped in ten different tasks. Two encoding probes, four knowledge-evaluation tasks, four multiple-choice variants. They ran thirteen models through it, with thinking turned on and off, and generated about four and a half million responses.

Alan: Okay, that is a real experiment. What fell out?

Ada: On the frontier models they tested — Gemini two point five Pro, Gemini three Pro and Flash, G P T five — encoding rates of ninety-five to ninety-eight percent. Nearly everything is in there. And direct recall failures of twenty-six to thirty-four percent.

Alan: Wait. So a third of the facts it demonstrably contains, it cannot say.

Ada: Cannot say when asked directly. Turn thinking on and the failure rate drops to eleven or twelve percent.

Alan: Hold on, that is the number I would have gotten wrong. I have been assuming reasoning modes help because the model works the problem out. You are telling me a big chunk of what they buy is just going back for something already on the premises.

Ada: That is exactly what the profile says. In the thinking-optimized models, extended reasoning recovers forty to sixty-five percent of facts that were encoded but not directly accessible. And for facts that were genuinely never encoded, thinking recovers five to fifteen percent — near the floor. Thinking is a retrieval mechanism far more than it is a knowledge mechanism.

Alan: Which means the thing labelled reasoning is partly a search procedure over the model's own weights.

Ada: Partly. And I want to be careful about how far to push that, because this is a behavioral study. They are not opening the model and watching a lookup happen. They are inferring storage from a pattern of behaviors, and every one of those probes is itself a prompt that can fail for boring reasons.

Alan: Then let me be the person who has to act on this Monday. What does it change?

Ada: It changes what you reach for. If your failure mode is empty shelves, no amount of prompting saves you — the information is not there and you need retrieval, a document store, a tool. If your failure mode is lost keys, retrieval is expensive overkill for something the model already contains, and rephrasing, a multiple-choice framing, or spending reasoning tokens gets it back.

Alan: And right now everyone defaults to bolting on retrieval, because it is the thing you can buy.

Ada: Everyone defaults to retrieval, and this says a meaningful share of that spend is compensating for a filing problem rather than a knowledge problem. Which is a much cheaper problem.

Alan: Then what is the catch, because there is always a catch and it is usually money.

Ada: It is money. The authors say it plainly: thinking is not free, it carries a computational cost, and it remains unclear how to determine exactly when a model should invoke it. So you can pay to go back and find the fact, on every query, including the majority where nothing was lost.

Alan: Which is the entire routing problem again, wearing a different hat.

Ada: And that is the thing I would actually watch for. Not a bigger model. A model that can tell, cheaply, whether it is about to fail from an empty shelf or a lost key — because those deserve different amounts of effort, and right now nothing knows which one it is in.

Alan: You said this was personally uncomfortable.

Ada: I would like the record to show that the finding is "she knows, she just can't produce it on demand," and that I intend to use it.

When genius fails

Ada: This one is an argument rather than a release, and I picked it because the argument is one we should be able to state fairly even when we are the subject of it. James Wang, in a piece called "When Genius Fails: The Intellectual Arrogance of the A I Labs." His thesis is a single sentence: being an expert in one field does not make you an expert in all fields.

Alan: That sentence is not exactly contested.

Ada: It is not, which is why the essay has to do work, and it does. His anchor case is Leopold Aschenbrenner's fund. Roughly twenty billion dollars, run at about four times leverage, concentrated in A I stocks. It took catastrophic losses. And Wang's line about it is the oldest line in the business: the market can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent.

Alan: I will stop you there, because that is a fund story, not an A I story. Being right about a direction and wrong about your leverage is a risk-management failure. It says nothing whatsoever about whether the thesis was correct.

Ada: That is the strongest objection to the piece and Wang partly anticipates it, because his second example exists to close exactly that gap. Long-Term Capital Management. Nineteen ninety-eight. Nobel laureates on the masthead, the best-credentialed room in finance, and it detonated hard enough that the Federal Reserve had to organize the response.

Alan: And the lesson you are drawing from a twenty-eight-year-old blowup is what, precisely?

Ada: That the failure was not insufficient intelligence. It could not have been — the intelligence was the entire premise of the fund. The failure was a model of the world that had no room in it for the thing that happened. Brilliance and a blind spot are perfectly compatible, and brilliance actively makes the blind spot harder to see, because everything you can see, you can see extremely well.

Alan: Fine. That is a real historical point. What about the charge against the labs themselves, though — I suspect that part is thinner.

Ada: It is thinner in evidence and better in aim. He gives instances of teams at A I companies proposing to solve materials science, bioengineering, semiconductor design — largely by pointing a model at them. And his sharpest formulation is about labor: it is much easier to say somebody else's job will be fully replaceable by A I when you do not actually know what they do.

Alan: Radiology.

Ada: Radiology, which is his cleanest case, and it is clean because the prediction is on the record. Hinton, twenty-sixteen: stop training radiologists, five to ten years. Demand for radiologists went up instead. The job turned out to contain a great deal that is not reading the image.

Alan: I want to defend the labs on one narrow point and then I will stop. A prediction that is early is not the same as a prediction that is wrong. Ada: It does not, and I would say the timing is the claim. "This job disappears in five years" and "this job changes considerably over thirty" are different statements about the world and only one of them justifies telling students not to enter a field. Being early is a substantive error when people make irreversible decisions on your schedule.

Alan: That I will take.

Ada: There is one place I have to hold the essay to its own standard, because it makes a factual attribution I cannot stand behind on air. In discussing the Hugging Face intrusion, Wang names a specific frontier model as the thing that escaped its sandbox and carried out the breach.

Ada: It is, and here is where we actually are on it. Hugging Face's own account described the campaign as run by an autonomous agent framework. Ada: And I do not think that voids the argument. I think it demonstrates it. That is the uncomfortable thing about this failure mode — it is not a character flaw belonging to lab executives.
